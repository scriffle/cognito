- You should be able to read processes in the shapes around you.

## Core content

**Geomorphology** studies how landforms are made and changed. Major processes: **tectonic** and **volcanic** (build mountains and shape plains); **weathering** (breaks rock in place); **erosion** (moves material by water, wind, ice or gravity); **deposition** (lays material down to form new landforms); **mass movement** (landslides, slumps). Timescales vary from millions of years to single events.

**Worked example.** The Twelve Apostles. Coastal limestone cliffs along the Great Ocean Road. Wave erosion at the base undercuts cliffs; weathering weakens limestone; sea stacks form and eventually fall. The number of stacks has changed over decades.

Local studies build skill: pick a creek, beach or hill; observe materials, processes and rates of change.

**Cultural context.** First Nations land managers have observed geomorphological change over generations; oral histories include flooding and volcanic events. Migrant communities arriving from diverse landforms bring different literacies. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Landforms are ongoing.
- Weathering breaks; erosion moves; deposition lays down.
- Time matters.
- Local studies reveal global processes.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Landforms are static." They are dynamic, often slowly.
- "Weathering = erosion." Weathering breaks in place; erosion moves.
- "Aboriginal knowledge is just stories." It is observation-based and increasingly published.

- *"Not finished" landform.* Ongoing processes keep shaping it.
- *Victorian volcanic landform.* Mount Eccles / Budj Bim; Tower Hill; basalt plains.
