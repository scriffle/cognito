## What you are learning
- You are learning to **collect, organise and process information and data** from **primary and secondary sources**, including **fieldwork** and **geospatial technologies and digital tools**.
- You are learning to build a working evidence base for geographic inquiry.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **fieldwork**, **GIS**, **primary source** and **secondary source** in your own words.
- Identify three geospatial tools and what they show.
- Design a simple fieldwork plan for a local landform or place.
- Organise data in a spreadsheet or map.

## Core content

**1. Primary and secondary sources.** **Primary**: data you collect or that comes from the original source (fieldwork, ABS Census, BoM data). **Secondary**: someone else's interpretation (news, textbooks, encyclopaedia). Both useful for different parts. *Quick check:* Why does fieldwork count as primary?

**2. Fieldwork basics.** **Fieldwork** is direct observation in the field: counting trees, sketching a creek, photographing a coastal cliff, surveying neighbours. Plan ahead: question, location, method, safety, recording. *Quick check:* Why is a written record more reliable than memory?

**3. Geospatial tools.** **GIS (geographic information system)** layers data on maps; **Google Maps and Google Earth** allow free viewing; **ArcGIS Online**, **QGIS** and **Atlas of Living Australia** offer richer analysis. Many schools have access. *Quick check:* What is one question GIS answers that a paper map cannot?

**4. Worked example: a school neighbourhood fieldwork study.** Question: how walkable is the school's neighbourhood? Method: map a 1 km radius using Google Maps; walk and record sidewalk condition, tree cover, traffic, crossings; survey ten students. Tools: Google Maps, smartphone camera, spreadsheet. Output: a labelled map with annotations. *Follow the thinking:* plan → execute → record → organise → analyse.

**5. Digital data tools.** Spreadsheets (Excel, Google Sheets); coding (Python, R); visualisation (Tableau, Datawrapper). Even simple tools (a spreadsheet with map images) go a long way. *Quick check:* Why is "I will remember" not enough for data?

**6. Organisation.** Use consistent column headers; record date, location, source; save raw and cleaned versions. Document your method so others can repeat. *Quick check:* What is one risk of overwriting your raw data?

**7. Ethics and safety.** Fieldwork requires care: permission for surveys; safety in field locations; cultural protocols on Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material. *Quick check:* What is one ethical issue in surveying classmates?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABS, BoM, Melbourne Water and Parks Victoria publish accessible primary geospatial data. Schools can access free GIS tools through Education Victoria and many universities.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Cultural mapping projects led by Traditional Owners increase Indigenous spatial knowledge in public planning. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual fieldwork (surveys translated; bilingual researchers) helps collect data from diverse communities accurately.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Memory is enough." Records are essential.
- "GIS is for experts only." Free tools are accessible.
- "Primary is always better." Secondary sources add context.
- "Fieldwork is just walking around." Plan and record matter.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why fieldwork is primary.* You are collecting original observations, not interpreting someone else's.
2. *Why a written record beats memory.* Memory fades and adjusts; written records can be checked.
3. *GIS vs paper map.* GIS can layer multiple data and update; paper shows one snapshot.
4. *Why "I will remember" is not enough.* Volume; accuracy degrades quickly.
5. *Risk of overwriting raw data.* Errors become permanent; insights from earlier states are lost.
6. *Ethical issue in surveying classmates.* Consent; anonymity; privacy of responses.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Park fieldwork.* Map the park; count facilities; observe and record user types; photograph features.
2. *Maribyrnong primary-data methods.* Door-to-door surveys; in-person workshops; online consultation.
3. *Junior GIS analyst on canopy.* Aerial imagery; council-tree inventory; ABS demographic data.
4. *Neighbour survey.* Issues: consent and respect for opinion. Approach: clear introductions, anonymous responses, opt-out option.
5. *Wadawurrung cultural mapping.* Step 1: contact the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: participate under their direction. Step 3: ensure outputs respect community wishes.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Study: walkability around my school.* Question: how walkable is the 1 km radius? Method: walk transects, record sidewalk condition, count crossings; survey ten students. Tools: Google Maps, phone camera, spreadsheet. Ethics: anonymous surveys with consent; safety: buddy system; daylight hours. Output: labelled map plus 200-word summary. Sources cited.
