# VC2HG8S01 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG8S01. Develop questions for a geographical inquiry related to a phenomenon, issue or challenge.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Geography (Geographical inquiry).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Strong geographical questions are spatial and specific.
  - Place, scale and time sharpen questions.
  - Concepts guide question design.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy; Numeracy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop questions** for a **geographical inquiry** related to a **phenomenon, issue or challenge**.
- You are learning to ask questions a geographer could actually investigate.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **inquiry question**, **scope** and **scale** in your own words.
- Convert a broad topic into three increasingly investigable questions.
- Apply geographic concepts to a question.
- Justify the final form.

## Core content

**1. Topic vs inquiry question.** A topic is general (e.g., "bushfires"). An **inquiry question** is specific and answerable (e.g., "How has bushfire risk in the Greater Bendigo area changed between 2003 and 2023?"). Refining narrows place, scale, time and measure. *Quick check:* Why is "bushfires" still a topic?

**2. Scope and scale.** **Scope** is what is in and out of your inquiry. **Scale** is how big a slice you take: local, regional, national, global. Different scales reveal different patterns. *Quick check:* What is one scale question for studying urbanisation in Australia?

**3. Concept-driven questions.** Using geographic concepts (space, place, interconnection, change, sustainability) helps frame questions: a space question, a place question, a change question. *Quick check:* What is one space question about your suburb?

**4. Worked example: refining a flood-risk question.** Initial: Are floods getting worse? Refined v1: Has flooding in regional Victoria become more frequent since 2000? Refined v2: How has the frequency of major flooding in the Goulburn-Murray region changed between 2000 and 2024, based on Bureau of Meteorology and floodplain-management data? Each step adds specificity and operationalises measurement. *Follow the thinking:* draft → refine → check criteria → final question.

**5. Criteria for a strong question.** Specific (place, time, actor, measure); investigable (sources exist); open (allows multiple perspectives); neutral (avoids loaded wording); operationalisable (you can define and measure). *Quick check:* Which criterion is most often overlooked?

**6. Stakeholder considerations.** Inquiries affecting communities should follow ethical and cultural guidance: AIATSIS for First Nations contexts; community input on multicultural questions. *Quick check:* Why might community priority shape the question?

**7. Iteration.** Real inquiries refine questions as data is gathered. Keep a "question log" to track changes. *Quick check:* Why is iteration not failure?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Parliament of Victoria research papers, Bureau of Meteorology Australia and Geoscience Australia publish accessible primary sources for student inquiry.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Inquiries affecting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities should follow AIATSIS guidelines; the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria publishes community-approved materials. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual community media and councils provide voices and data often missing from mainstream sources.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one Victorian or Australian geographical issue. Develop five increasingly refined questions, naming what was added at each step. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Bigger question is stronger." Specific questions get tested.
- "Specific = narrow." Specific means clear; can still be substantive.
- "Indigenous topics are off-limits." Valid with appropriate ethics.
- "Single-question inquiries are enough." Often several questions are needed.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"Bushfires" as topic.* Names a phenomenon but no place, time, measure or actor.
2. *Scale question for urbanisation.* National vs metropolitan vs suburb-scale data?
3. *Space question about your suburb.* How are services distributed across walking distance from home?
4. *Most overlooked criterion.* Operationalisability: students often pick variables they cannot measure.
5. *Why community priority shapes questions.* Research benefits those most affected; questions should reflect their concerns.
6. *Iteration not failure.* Refining responds to evidence and improves the inquiry.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Drought refinement.* Q1: Is drought getting worse in Victoria? Q2: Has the Victorian Mallee experienced more dry years since 2000? Q3: How has the number of months below long-term-average rainfall in the Mallee changed between 2000 and 2024, using Bureau of Meteorology data?
2. *Wyndham urban-growth research.* Population growth and school capacity; transport access; canopy cover trends.
3. *Junior geographer refinement.* Define the place and time; identify data source; check ethical considerations.
4. *Sensitive community-issue research.* Reframe to systemic factors; protect anonymity; partner with community organisations.
5. *Wadawurrung environmental project.* Step 1: contact the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: co-design the question. Step 3: ensure outputs benefit the community.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: Melbourne urban heat.* Q1: Is Melbourne getting hotter? Q2: Has Melbourne canopy cover changed since 2010? Q3: How has canopy cover and summer surface-temperature changed in three Melbourne LGAs between 2010 and 2024, based on Victorian Government and ABS data? Q4-5: refinement adds detail and measurement. Sources cited.
