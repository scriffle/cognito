# VC2HG8S07 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG8S07. Create and present explanations and responses, using geographical knowledge, concepts and methods, and referring to sources.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Geography (Communicating).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Geographic communication uses words, maps and visuals.
  - Concepts and methods make explanations rigorous.
  - Audience shapes format.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Literacy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **create and present explanations and responses**, using **geographical knowledge, concepts and methods**, and **referring to sources**.
- You are learning to communicate as a geographer.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **explanation**, **geographic concept**, **method** and **citation** in your own words.
- Build a four-part response using concepts and sources.
- Choose format and tone for an audience.
- Adapt one explanation for two formats.

## Core content

**1. What geographers communicate.** Explanations (what is happening and why), descriptions (what is there), evaluations (how well something works), recommendations (what to do). Each uses concepts, methods and sources. *Quick check:* What is the difference between description and explanation?

**2. Using concepts.** Space, place, interconnection, change, environment, sustainability and scale frame the analysis. Stating which concepts you used signals rigorous thinking. *Quick check:* Why do concepts help readers follow your argument?

**3. Using methods.** Fieldwork, GIS, surveys, statistics and comparisons are methods. Naming them lets readers check the work. *Quick check:* Why does method naming build trust?

**4. Worked example: a response to "Should our suburb plant more trees?"** Audience 1: school assembly. Format: 90-second video showing canopy data with three reasons. Audience 2: council. Format: 200-word submission with one chart, two sources cited and a recommendation. Audience 3: family. Format: a one-page summary with photos and a call to action. Same explanation, three formats. *Follow the thinking:* identify audience → choose format → use concepts → cite sources → adapt language.

**5. Visuals and maps.** Charts, infographics and maps support text. Each needs a clear title, legend, source and date. *Quick check:* What is one common map error to avoid?

**6. Citing sources.** Cite by author, year and where accessed. Use a consistent style. Cite each significant claim. *Quick check:* Why is "Google says" not a citation?

**7. Honest communication.** State limits; avoid overclaiming; acknowledge community voices. Plain English beats jargon for general audiences. *Quick check:* Why is "the data prove" usually too strong?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABC News, SBS, The Conversation Australia, ABC Science and Victorian Government communications model various explanation styles.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Cite First Nations authors directly. NITV, IndigenousX and AIATSIS publish accessible content. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual presentation supports reach across Melbourne; community-language media broadcast geographic content.

## Memorable takeaways
- Concepts plus methods plus sources.
- Audience first, format second.
- Visuals carry meaning.
- Cite by author, year, source.
- Honest communication earns trust.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Big words sound smart." Plain language sounds clear.
- "Long quotes prove the point." Use sparingly; reasoning is yours.
- "If it looks polished, it is right." Polish is not provenance.
- "Citations slow me down." They build trust and let others check.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Description vs explanation.* Description says what; explanation says why.
2. *Why concepts help readers.* They signal careful thinking and frame the argument.
3. *Why method naming builds trust.* It lets readers check how you produced the result.
4. *Common map error.* Missing legend; unclear units; misleading colours.
5. *Why "Google says" is not a citation.* It hides the source and is not checkable.
6. *Why "the data prove" is usually too strong.* Data support; reasoning links them to claims.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Family explanation.* Pick a specific issue (e.g., flood risk); use one number; one source pointer.
2. *Yarra canopy presentation.* Story-shaped explanation; map with citation; concrete call to action.
3. *Junior geographer media kit.* Media release; fact sheet; short explainer video. Each audience needs different length and tone.
4. *Strong-opinion publication.* Issues: factual accuracy and respect for those affected. Cite sources; allow right of reply.
5. *Multilingual climate-adaptation explanation.* Translate carefully; check community feedback; use simple examples.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: extreme heat in Melbourne's west.* Format 1: 90-second TikTok for peers with one chart and one call to action. Format 2: 200-word community-newsletter article with two cited sources. Format 3: 600-word op-ed for council with a recommendation and three sources. Each adapts language and emphasis; the underlying claim and evidence stay the same.
