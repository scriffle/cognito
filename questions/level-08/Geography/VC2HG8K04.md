# VC2HG8K04 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG8K04. The nature of water scarcity and ways of overcoming it, with examples from Australia, and West Asia and/or North Africa.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Geography (Water in the world).
- **Big ideas:**

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **nature of water scarcity** and **ways of overcoming it**, with examples from **Australia, and West Asia and/or North Africa**.
- You are learning to read scarcity and the toolkit for managing it.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **physical water scarcity**, **economic water scarcity**, **desalination** and **water recycling** in your own words.
- Identify three causes of water scarcity.
- Compare an Australian and a West Asian or North African case.
- Discuss two ways to overcome scarcity, with trade-offs.

## Core content

**1. What scarcity means.** **Physical water scarcity** is when total water available is too low to meet demand. **Economic water scarcity** is when there is enough water but infrastructure, governance or affordability limits access. Many places have both. *Quick check:* Why is "lack of water" not always physical scarcity?

**2. Causes.** Geography (low rainfall, evaporation); population growth; agricultural and industrial demand; pollution that reduces usable water; conflict and governance failure; climate change. *Quick check:* What is one cause that affects most regions today?

**3. Australian scarcity cases.** Murray-Darling Basin: regulated flows, over-extraction in some periods, sharing across states. Western Sydney and outer Melbourne: peak-demand pressure. Remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities: infrastructure gaps. *Quick check:* Which type of scarcity affects remote Australian communities most?

**4. West Asia and North Africa cases.** Israel-Palestine: shared aquifers and disputed allocations. Saudi Arabia: heavy reliance on desalination and groundwater drawdown. Egypt: dependence on the Nile, with upstream development affecting flows. *Quick check:* Why does Saudi Arabia rely so heavily on desalination?

**5. Worked example: overcoming scarcity in Melbourne.** Toolkit: efficiency (low-flow showers, water-saving appliances); rainwater tanks; reservoir augmentation (Thomson, Cardinia); recycled water for non-drinking uses (Werribee West); desalination (Wonthaggi); pricing signals; restrictions in drought. Trade-offs: energy cost of desal, capital cost of new infrastructure, equity in pricing. *Follow the thinking:* identify type of scarcity → list tools → name trade-offs → recommend balanced response.

**6. Tools for overcoming scarcity.** Demand-side: efficiency, pricing, education, restrictions. Supply-side: dams, desalination, recycling, groundwater, transfers. Each tool has costs and limits. *Quick check:* Why is demand management often cheaper than building new supply?

**7. Equity in scarcity.** Scarcity hits poorer households hardest. Rebates, subsidies and remote-community infrastructure investment are part of responses. Aboriginal-led water management is increasing. *Quick check:* Why does honest water policy include equity, not just engineering?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Desalination Plant near Wonthaggi, the Western Treatment Plant at Werribee and Melbourne Water's catchments provide a layered system. DEECA and the Victorian Auditor-General publish reviews.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Closing the Gap targets include water and sanitation for remote communities. The Murray-Darling Basin Aboriginal Water Entitlements Program supports First Nations water rights. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant communities often arrive from water-scarce regions and bring practices that suit Melbourne's dryer summers. Multilingual water-efficiency campaigns work across communities.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Scarcity means no water at all." Most scarcity is partial and uneven.
- "Desalination solves everything." It has high energy and capital costs.
- "Western examples beat all others." Each region's approach has lessons.
- "Equity is not part of water policy." It is central.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"Lack of water" not always physical.* Some places have water but not infrastructure, governance or affordability to access it.
2. *Cause affecting most regions.* Climate change is increasing variability and stress almost everywhere.
3. *Remote Australian scarcity.* Mostly economic: infrastructure, governance, distance.
4. *Why Saudi Arabia relies on desalination.* Limited natural surface water; high oil-energy makes desal viable; large population in arid land.
5. *Why demand management is cheaper.* It avoids capital investment in new supply; saved water has no infrastructure cost.
6. *Why equity matters.* Water is a basic need; scarcity hits the poor hardest; honest policy designs for fairness.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Home water-saving.* Shorter showers; rainwater tank; full loads in dishwasher and washing machine.
2. *Casey restrictions communication.* Plain English; multilingual; positive framing on what people can still do.
3. *Recycled-water expansion.* Cost, public-trust messaging, end-use selection, environmental benefit.
4. *Higher prices for wealthy in drought.* For: signals scarcity and supports targeted help. Against: still hits middle-income; designs need care. Both sides agree on need for rebates.
5. *Mediterranean practices.* Share through community-gardens groups; ensure intercultural respect; integrate with Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung knowledge where relevant.
