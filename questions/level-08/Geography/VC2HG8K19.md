## Core content

**1. Two kinds of migration.** **International migration** crosses national borders. **Internal migration** moves within a country (e.g., rural to urban). Both reshape cities. *Quick check:* Why are both kinds important to track?

**2. Australia's international migration.** Major waves: post-WWII European; Vietnamese refugees in the 1970s–80s; skilled migration from India, China, the Philippines; humanitarian arrivals from East Africa and West Asia. Migration policy shapes who arrives and how. *Quick check:* What is one current source country for Australian migration?

**3. Australia's internal migration.** Major patterns: rural to urban; interstate movement (e.g., to Queensland for lifestyle and jobs); within-state shifts (city to regional during COVID-19). *Quick check:* What is one recent Australian internal-migration trend?

**4. Worked example: internal migration in China.** Hundreds of millions of people have moved from rural areas to coastal cities (Shenzhen, Shanghai, Beijing) for work since the 1980s. The **hukou** household-registration system regulates settlement. Eastern coastal megacities have grown enormously; western and central inland cities have grown but more slowly. Impacts: workforce growth, housing pressure, services pressure, rural depopulation in some areas. *Follow the thinking:* origin → destination → policy → growth → impact.

**5. Net migration.** **Net migration** = arrivals minus departures. Positive net migration adds to a city or country's population; negative subtracts. Migration plus natural increase drives urban growth. *Quick check:* What is the difference between net migration and net population growth?

**6. Impacts on cities.** Housing demand; service pressure; cultural change; economic dynamism; sometimes inequality. Honest analysis names both opportunities and challenges. *Quick check:* What is one Melbourne suburb shaped by recent migration?

**7. Comparison.** Australia's international migration is large relative to natural increase; China's internal migration is dominant. Different patterns, different policies, similar urban-growth pressures. *Quick check:* Why is comparison useful?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABS Census data show Victorian migration patterns. Melbourne's outer suburbs (Wyndham, Casey, Hume) grow strongly from both international and internal migration.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people maintain mobility between Country and cities; community networks span both. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant communities shape Melbourne suburbs: Vietnamese in Footscray and Springvale; Indian in Tarneit and Point Cook; Chinese in Box Hill; Sudanese in Sunshine.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Migration is one-way." Many move back or back-and-forth.
- "Migration causes all city growth." Natural increase also matters.
- "Migrants are a homogeneous group." Backgrounds, reasons and outcomes vary.
- "Aboriginal mobility is rural-only." Many are deeply connected to cities.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why both matters.* Internal migration explains city growth within a country; international migration explains diversity and connections beyond.
2. *Current source country.* India; China; the Philippines; UK; Vietnam (varies year to year).
3. *Recent Australian internal-migration trend.* City-to-regional shift during COVID-19; ongoing growth in outer suburbs.
4. *Net migration vs net population growth.* Net migration is arrivals minus departures; net population growth adds natural increase (births minus deaths).
5. *Melbourne suburb shaped by migration.* Footscray (Vietnamese); Box Hill (Chinese); Tarneit (Indian); Sunshine (Sudanese, Vietnamese).
6. *Why comparison is useful.* Reveals what policy and conditions do; helps spot transferable ideas.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Three-generation family migration.* International (arrival generation); internal (city moves); back-and-forth visits.
2. *Wyndham settlement services.* Language support; school enrolment; community-organisation partnerships.
3. *Junior demographer.* Age structure; origin countries; suburb of settlement; year-over-year change.
4. *Encouraging regional internal migration.* For: spreads growth and supports regions. Against: service-capacity strain; transport challenges. Both sides agree on need for planning.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian intergenerational conversation.* Listen to first-generation reasons for leaving; ask about local family choices; share future plans.
