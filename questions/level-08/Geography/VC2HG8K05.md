## Core content

**1. Hazards vs disasters.** A **hazard** is a natural event with the potential to cause harm (e.g., a flood). A **disaster** is when the hazard meets exposed and vulnerable people and infrastructure. Two different floods can lead to different disaster outcomes depending on context. *Quick check:* Why does the same flood produce different damage in different towns?

**2. Atmospheric and hydrometeorological hazards.** These include floods, droughts, storms (including cyclones), severe thunderstorms, hailstorms and heatwaves. Each has typical causes, impacts and responses. *Quick check:* What is one Australian example of each?

**3. Causes.** Most causes are natural (weather patterns, climate variability). Human factors increase exposure and vulnerability: land-use planning, building standards, urban heat-islands, climate change. *Quick check:* Why do many experts say "natural disaster" is misleading?

**4. Impacts.** Direct: loss of life, injury, damage to property, infrastructure, crops, ecosystems. Indirect: mental health, displacement, economic loss, school disruption, insurance and policy effects. *Quick check:* What is one indirect impact often underestimated?

**5. Worked example: the 2022 floods in northern Victoria.** Cause: heavy and prolonged rainfall associated with a La Niña pattern; saturated ground; full reservoirs. Direct impacts: homes flooded in Echuca, Rochester, Shepparton; livestock losses; road closures. Indirect impacts: mental-health strain; insurance costs; agricultural losses; school interruption. Responses: emergency evacuation; community sandbagging; SES and ADF support; Commonwealth disaster payments; later, build-back-better planning. *Follow the thinking:* cause → exposure → impacts → emergency response → recovery → mitigation.

**6. Phases of response.** **Prevention/mitigation** before (planning rules, levees, education). **Preparedness** (early-warning, kits, plans). **Response** (during) (evacuation, rescue, shelter). **Recovery** (after) (clean-up, rebuild, mental-health support). Communities that invest before disasters tend to recover faster. *Quick check:* Why does "invest before" beat "respond fast" in many cases?

**7. Resilience and climate change.** **Resilience** is the capacity to absorb shocks and adapt. Climate change is shifting hazard frequencies; investments must consider future patterns, not only past. *Quick check:* What is one way climate change is shifting Australian hazards?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Emergency Management Victoria coordinates state responses. VICSES, CFA, FRV and Ambulance Victoria are key agencies. The Bureau of Meteorology Australia and DEECA publish hazard data.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal-led cool burning reduces wildfire intensity. Traditional knowledge about flood-prone areas informs land-use decisions in some catchments. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual emergency communication has expanded after lessons from the 2022 floods and 2024 storms. Faith-based and community organisations play key roles in recovery.

## Memorable takeaways
- Hazard plus exposure equals disaster.
- Many causes, many impacts.
- Four phases: mitigate, prepare, respond, recover.
- Invest before, recover faster.
- Climate change shifts the picture.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Natural disaster." Often misleading; human factors shape outcomes.
- "Only the affected area matters." Indirect impacts spread.
- "Past data predicts future hazards." Climate change shifts the baseline.
- "Recovery is just rebuilding." Mental health and community fabric matter too.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Same flood, different damage.* Differences in land use, building standards, warning systems and community capacity.
2. *Australian example of each hazard.* Flood (2022 Victoria); drought (Millennium Drought); cyclone (Tropical Cyclone Tracy, 1974); thunderstorm asthma (2016, Melbourne); heatwave (2009, southeast Australia).
3. *Why "natural disaster" misleads.* Outcomes depend on human factors; some hazards become disasters only because of choices.
4. *Underestimated indirect impact.* Mental health; long-term insurance and housing affordability shifts.
5. *Why "invest before" wins.* Mitigation often costs less than recovery and saves lives.
6. *Climate change shift.* More frequent extreme rainfall; longer fire seasons; sea-level rise increasing coastal flooding.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Severe-weather warning.* Pack essentials; charge devices; check on neighbours.
2. *Greater Shepparton flood plans.* Update flood maps with climate projections; improve early-warning in multiple languages; invest in mitigation infrastructure.
3. *Junior emergency-management officer.* Multilingual communication; partnerships with community organisations; clear pre-event education.
4. *Stop rebuilding in flood-prone areas debate.* For: future safety and cost. Against: community attachment and rights. Both sides can be reconciled with planning and compensation.
5. *Vietnamese-speaking community emergency information.* Translate alerts; partner with community organisations; use Vietnamese-language radio and social media.
