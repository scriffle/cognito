- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Geography (Landforms and landscapes).
- **Big ideas:**

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **human causes of landform change** and **ways of managing it**, including a **study of a local landform**.
- You are learning to read human fingerprints on landforms.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **land-use change**, **management** and **sustainable use** in your own words.
- Identify four human causes of landform change.
- Describe two management approaches for one local landform.
- Discuss why some changes are reversible and others not.

## Core content

**1. Direct causes.** Mining; quarrying; dam-building; coastal works (groynes, seawalls); urban development (roads, buildings); agriculture (ploughing, irrigation, clearing); forestry. *Quick check:* What is one Victorian example of direct landform change?

**2. Indirect causes.** Climate change altering coastal erosion rates; invasive species changing vegetation that stabilises landforms; pollution affecting waterways; over-extraction lowering groundwater. *Quick check:* Why are indirect causes often harder to manage?

**3. Reversible vs irreversible change.** Some change can be reversed (revegetation, dune stabilisation). Others are practically irreversible (mountain quarrying, deep mining). Honest management names which it expects. *Quick check:* What is one Victorian example of a hard-to-reverse change?

**4. Worked example: Port Phillip Bay coastline.** Causes: urban development, port works (Port of Melbourne), sea-level rise, climate change effects on storms, sand-mining historically. Management: seawalls in some areas; dune restoration; beach renourishment with imported sand; planning rules for new development. Trade-offs: cost, environmental impact, community expectations. *Follow the thinking:* identify causes → identify responses → list trade-offs → measure outcomes.

**5. Management approaches.** Hard engineering (seawalls, groynes, levees); soft engineering (dune restoration, beach renourishment); planning rules (setbacks, buffers); community programs (Coastcare, Landcare); Indigenous-led caring for Country. Mixing approaches is common. *Quick check:* Why do some councils prefer "soft" over "hard" engineering?

**6. Local landform study.** Choosing a local landform — a beach, riverbank, hill — and tracing the human causes of change builds practical skill. Field observation, council reports and historical maps all help. *Quick check:* What is one local landform you could study?

**7. Aboriginal-led care.** Traditional Owners across Victoria lead caring-for-Country programs that restore landforms and ecosystems. Cultural burning, native revegetation and waterway care are examples. *Quick check:* Why does Aboriginal-led care often improve long-term outcomes?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Coastcare Victoria, Landcare Victoria, Parks Victoria and the Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action manage landform-change responses across the state.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Traditional Owner corporations lead joint-management and caring-for-Country programs. The Gunditjmara Aboriginal Corporation co-manages Budj Bim. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant volunteers participate in Coastcare and Landcare programs across Melbourne and regional Victoria.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Human change is always bad." Some change supports valued uses; the question is whether change is sustainable.
- "Management = total control." It is influence over time, not absolute control.
- "Seawalls solve everything." They often shift problems elsewhere.
- "Restoration is easy." It is slow and depends on multiple conditions.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Victorian direct landform change.* Quarries near Bacchus Marsh; dam at Lake Eildon; Port of Melbourne dredging.
2. *Why indirect causes are harder.* Many sources, long lags, complex feedback.
3. *Hard-to-reverse Victorian change.* Brown-coal pits in Latrobe Valley; large urban subdivisions on former farmland.
4. *Why soft engineering.* Often cheaper, more flexible and less disruptive ecologically.
5. *Local landform to study.* A nearby beach, creek, hill or quarry.
6. *Why caring for Country improves outcomes.* Long-term observation, integrated practice, cultural authority.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Beach erosion.* Causes: storms, sand-mining history, sea-level rise. Responses: dune restoration; renourishment; planning setbacks.
2. *Surf Coast long-term plan.* Mapped risk zones; engineering and soft-protection mix; community engagement; long-term funding.
3. *Riverbank restoration.* Stabilise banks; replant natives; control invasive species.
4. *Seawalls for expensive properties debate.* For: protecting investment. Against: equity and shifting problems. Both sides can be reconciled with shared-cost models.
5. *Wadawurrung restoration.* Step 1: contact the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: follow site protocol. Step 3: participate under their direction.
