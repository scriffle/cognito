# VC2HG8K20 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG8K20. The comparison of urban patterns in highly urbanised countries, including a comparison of Australia and another country.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Geography (Changing nations).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Highly urbanised countries are not identical.
  - City patterns vary by history, geography and policy.
  - Comparison reveals options for Australia.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **comparison of urban patterns in highly urbanised countries**, including a **comparison of Australia and another country**.
- You are learning to read city shape against the world.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **highly urbanised**, **urban pattern** and **primate city** in your own words.
- Identify three features of Australian urban pattern.
- Compare Australia with one other highly urbanised country on three features.
- Discuss what Australia could learn from comparison.

## Core content

**1. What "highly urbanised" means.** Generally, countries where 75%+ of the population lives in cities. Australia (~86%) is one of the most urbanised; others include Japan, Singapore, the Netherlands, Argentina, Chile, Israel. *Quick check:* Why is Australia "highly urbanised" despite a vast inland?

**2. Australian urban pattern.** Major cities along the coast (Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth, Adelaide, plus Hobart, Darwin and Canberra). Most are mid-sized by global standards. Suburbs are extensive; densities are moderate. Public-transport networks vary; cars are dominant in many cities. *Quick check:* What is one feature of Australian cities that surprises visitors?

**3. Concept: primate city.** A **primate city** is one that dominates a country's urban population. Bangkok in Thailand and Seoul in South Korea are primate cities. Australia has no single primate city; population spreads across capitals. *Quick check:* Why does Australia not have a primate city?

**4. Worked example: Australia vs Japan.** Australia: coastal cities; spread population; moderate density; car-dominant suburbs; English-speaking; recent migration adds diversity. Japan: Tokyo–Yokohama megalopolis plus Osaka–Kobe and Nagoya; very high density; transit-dominant; long urban history; ageing population. Both: highly urbanised but with different shapes. *Follow the thinking:* describe each → identify similarities → identify differences → discuss lessons.

**5. Other comparisons.** Australia and Singapore: density and transit; Australia and Canada: spread along coastlines; Australia and the Netherlands: water management and density. Each comparison highlights different choices. *Quick check:* Why is one comparison not enough?

**6. Lessons for Australia.** Possible lessons: better public transport investment from Japan or Singapore; integrated water management from the Netherlands; regional balance from Canada. *Quick check:* Why does "copy what works" rarely work directly?

**7. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives.** Australian urban patterns often ignore First Nations Country in their early planning. Recent reform (Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung consultation in planning; Acknowledgement of Country) is beginning to integrate First Nations geography. *Quick check:* Why does urban planning need First Nations input?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne is one of the world's most liveable cities by some rankings; its growth makes it Australia's fastest-growing capital in recent years. Plan Melbourne sets long-term urban strategy.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Bunurong/Boon Wurrung and Wadawurrung Country underlies Melbourne. Recent initiatives recognise these in planning. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Melbourne's urban pattern reflects multiple migration waves. Multilingual services and faith centres are now integral to its urban geography.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Highly urbanised cities look the same." History and policy create big differences.
- "Density is always good." Trade-offs apply.
- "Copy what works directly." Local context matters.
- "First Nations input is symbolic." It improves planning substantively.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why Australia is highly urbanised.* Most people live near the coast; inland areas are arid and sparsely populated.
2. *Surprising Australian feature.* Coastal concentration; moderate density and large house sizes by global standards; car dominance; large suburbs.
3. *No primate city.* History of multiple state capitals; federal structure; coastal geography.
4. *Why one comparison is not enough.* Each comparison reveals only part of the picture.
5. *Why "copy what works" rarely works directly.* Geography, history, policy and values differ.
6. *Why First Nations input matters in planning.* Improves environmental outcomes, respects custodianship, supports broader legitimacy.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Suburb vs overseas.* Density; transport mix; mix of land uses; cultural amenities; climate-shaped design.
2. *Maribyrnong international lessons.* Japan (transit); Netherlands (water); Singapore (density and design).
3. *Junior planner transit-oriented development.* Hong Kong rail and property models; Singapore land-value capture; light-rail experiences in Australian cities.
4. *Densify vs expand debate.* For density: lower emissions, more amenity. For expansion: housing supply, choice. Both sides can be reconciled with mixed strategies.
5. *Tokyo vs Melbourne intergenerational chat.* Density and quiet; café and food culture; public transport; nature access.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Country: Singapore.* Five features: very high density; transit-dominant; integrated planning; strong housing programme; managed migration. Lesson: long-term integrated planning. Limitation: governance and land-area differences make direct copy impossible. Sources cited.
