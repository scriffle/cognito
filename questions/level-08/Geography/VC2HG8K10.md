## What you are learning
- You are learning how the **concepts of space, environment and interconnection** can be applied to **evaluate the liveability of a place**.
- You are learning to think with geographic concepts.

By the end of this you should be able to:

- Identify a question each concept opens.

## Core content

**1. Geographic concepts.** Geographers use big ideas to organise inquiry: **space** (location, distribution, distance, scale); **environment** (the natural world and how people interact with it); **interconnection** (how places and people influence each other). Other concepts include place, change, sustainability and scale. *Quick check:* Why do concepts help you evaluate, not just describe?

**2. Space.** **Space** asks where things are and how they are distributed. Pattern of services, density of housing, distances between work and home all relate to space. *Quick check:* What is one space question about your suburb?

**3. Environment.** **Environment** considers the natural setting (climate, water, biodiversity, hazards) and how people shape and respond to it. Air quality, canopy and waterways belong here. *Quick check:* What is one environment question about your suburb?

**4. Interconnection.** **Interconnection** asks how places and groups influence each other: how a Goulburn Valley orchard supplies Melbourne markets; how a Wyndham resident commutes to the CBD; how a Melbourne suburb is shaped by global migration. *Quick check:* What is one interconnection question about your suburb?

**5. Worked example: applying concepts to Sunshine, Melbourne.** Space: Sunshine sits on the western train line, with new infrastructure planned. Environment: lower canopy than some suburbs, near Maribyrnong River, parts at flood risk. Interconnection: Vietnamese, Indian, Sudanese communities link Sunshine globally; transport links connect to CBD and Geelong line; future Suburban Rail Loop will reshape interconnection. Liveability evaluation combines all three. *Follow the thinking:* apply each concept → identify a question → connect across concepts → evaluate.

**6. Combining concepts.** Strong analysis combines two or three concepts. For example, environmental quality (environment) plus access (space) plus migration ties (interconnection) together explain Sunshine's evolving liveability. *Quick check:* Why is combining concepts better than using one alone?

**7. Asking better questions.** Each concept opens different questions: space asks "where and how distributed?"; environment asks "what natural conditions and impacts?"; interconnection asks "what links shape this place?". *Quick check:* What is one question that combines two concepts?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Plan Melbourne; Victorian Suburban Rail Loop; Department of Transport and Planning Victoria; local-government planning frameworks all reflect spatial-thinking concepts.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations geography uses concepts of Country, kinship, seasonal indicators and interconnected ecological systems. These complement and sometimes critique Western geographic concepts. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant communities make spatial and interconnection decisions about where to live based on proximity to community networks, places of worship and culturally specific services.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Concepts are jargon." They organise thinking.
- "One concept is enough." Combination gives a fuller picture.
- "Geography is only about maps." It also asks why and how.
- "Indigenous concepts are alternative." They are complementary and current.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why concepts help.* They direct your attention to important features and prevent superficial description.
2. *Space question.* Where is housing located in relation to schools and shops?
3. *Environment question.* What climate hazards does my suburb face?
4. *Interconnection question.* How does my suburb depend on other places for food, water, energy?
5. *Why combine concepts.* Single concepts miss the whole; combinations reveal trade-offs.
6. *Two-concept question.* "How does environment (canopy) plus space (distribution of green) shape liveability across this council?"

**Try these scenarios**

1. *School evaluation.* Space: location relative to transport. Environment: nearby green and air quality. Interconnection: where students travel from and how community supports the school.
2. *Maribyrnong public-space project.* Where will it sit (space)? How does it interact with the river (environment)? Who will it connect with (interconnection)?
3. *Junior urban planner.* Combine canopy with service distribution; combine transport with migration ties; combine flood risk with population growth.
4. *Indigenous concepts in liveability.* For: more comprehensive evaluation. Against: integration challenges. Both sides agree on need for respectful inclusion.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian festival planning.* Space: location accessible by train. Environment: shaded outdoor areas. Interconnection: links to family, food vendors, performers.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Place: Bendigo.* Space: regional centre, dispersed services. Environment: temperate, low rainfall, heritage landscapes. Interconnection: Melbourne train line, agricultural hinterland, growing tech sector. Trade-off: regional space-environment balance vs interconnection with metro. Improvement: stronger transport links and canopy investment. Sources cited.
