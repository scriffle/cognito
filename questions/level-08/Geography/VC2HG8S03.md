# VC2HG8S03 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG8S03. Represent and describe information and data using a range of formats, including maps constructed with geospatial technologies.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Geography (Geographical inquiry).
- **Big ideas:**

  - Maps are powerful, but not the only tool.
  - Good representation makes patterns visible.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Digital Literacy; Numeracy; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **represent and describe information and data** using a range of formats, including **maps constructed with geospatial technologies**.
- You are learning to make patterns visible.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **map projection**, **thematic map** and **choropleth** in your own words.
- List five formats for geographic information.
- Build a simple thematic map using free tools.
- Describe what a map shows in plain English.

## Core content

**1. Choosing the format.** Maps, graphs, tables, diagrams, infographics, photographs and videos all carry information. Pick the format that suits your message. *Quick check:* Why is "always use a map" a poor rule?

**2. Map basics.** A **map** simplifies real space onto paper or screen. **Map projection** is the rule for turning a curved Earth into a flat map; choices produce different distortions. **Scale** shows how distance on the map relates to ground distance. *Quick check:* Why do projections always distort something?

**3. Thematic maps.** A **thematic map** shows one specific theme (population density, vegetation cover, election results). A **choropleth** map uses colour or shading to show values across areas. *Quick check:* What is one Victorian thematic map you have seen?

**4. Worked example: a thematic map of Melbourne's tree-canopy cover.** Source: state government and council canopy data. Format: choropleth by LGA with darker shades for higher canopy cover. Title: "Tree-canopy cover by LGA, Greater Melbourne, 2023". Legend: shows cover ranges. North arrow and scale included. Description: "Outer eastern LGAs show higher canopy; growth areas in the west and north show lower cover." *Follow the thinking:* pick data → choose format → label → describe.

**5. Geospatial tools.** Google Maps, Google Earth, ArcGIS Online, QGIS, Atlas of Living Australia all let you make maps. Each requires data with location attributes (coordinates, addresses, LGA boundaries). *Quick check:* What is one open-data source you could use?

**6. Charts and tables.** Bar charts compare categories; line charts show change over time; scatter plots show relationships; tables hold detail. Charts and maps often complement each other. *Quick check:* When is a chart better than a map?

**7. Describing a representation.** State direction (where), magnitude (how much), pattern (what shape), exceptions (outliers), source and limits. Plain English beats jargon. *Quick check:* Why is "the map shows it" too vague?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABS spatial data, Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action Victoria mapping tools, and council open-data portals offer free Victorian-relevant data.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Indigenous mapping projects (e.g., AIATSIS AusLang map; Wadawurrung and Wurundjeri cultural mapping initiatives) offer alternative spatial knowledge. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Maps in multiple languages support diverse Melbourne communities; community organisations create map-based resources.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Maps are objective." All maps embed choices.
- "Bigger map is better." Right scale matters.
- "Charts and maps are the same." They show different things.
- "If it looks good, it is right." Check data quality.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"Always use a map" is poor.* Some data are best shown as charts or tables; maps suit spatial questions.
2. *Why projections distort.* Flattening a sphere onto a plane is geometrically impossible without trade-offs.
3. *Victorian thematic map.* Election-results maps; canopy maps; flood-risk maps.
4. *Open-data source.* ABS DataExplorer; Victorian Government data.vic.gov.au; council open-data portals.
5. *Chart better than map.* When showing change over time; when the question is not spatial.
6. *"The map shows it" too vague.* Specify direction, magnitude, pattern, exception and source.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Neighbourhood map.* Add layers for shops, parks, transport, schools; describe distribution.
2. *Frankston parks-access map.* Park locations; walking-distance buffers; demographic overlay.
3. *Bushfire history map.* Combine historic fire data with vegetation and population layers.
4. *Household income map.* Issues: privacy and stigma. Use aggregated data; consider potential misuse.
5. *Multilingual services map.* Translate labels carefully; consult community organisations; check colour-symbolism.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Map: median weekly rent by Melbourne LGA, 2024.* Data source: REIV. Format: choropleth. Title: "Median weekly rent by Melbourne LGA, December 2024 quarter". Description: "Inner-city LGAs show higher rents; outer LGAs lower; some inner-west LGAs show fast change." Limits: data quarterly; LGA boundaries may obscure within-area variation. Sources cited.
