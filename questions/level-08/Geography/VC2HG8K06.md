# VC2HG8K06 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG8K06. The environmental, economic, social and other measures used to evaluate places for their liveability, and the influence of liveability on where people live.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Geography (Place and liveability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Liveability has multiple dimensions.
  - Measures and weights shape rankings.
  - People choose places for many reasons.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Numeracy; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **measures used to evaluate places for liveability** and how **liveability influences where people live**.
- You are learning to read liveability as a multi-dimensional, contested measure.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **liveability**, **indicator** and **weighting** in your own words.
- List five common liveability measures.
- Identify two trade-offs in choosing where to live.
- Discuss why "best city to live in" rankings vary.

## Core content

**1. What liveability means.** **Liveability** is how suited a place is for living, considering many factors. There is no single definition; different bodies (e.g., The Economist Intelligence Unit, Mercer, OECD) use different methods. *Quick check:* Why is "Melbourne is the world's most liveable city" a contested claim?

**2. Environmental measures.** Air and water quality; climate; green space; biodiversity; noise; risk of natural hazards. *Quick check:* Why is access to green space part of liveability?

**3. Economic measures.** Employment opportunities; cost of living; housing affordability; income levels. *Quick check:* Why can a "wealthy" place still have low liveability?

**4. Social measures.** Health services; education; safety; community connectedness; cultural and recreational opportunities. *Quick check:* What is one social measure that is hard to quantify?

**5. Worked example: a Year 8 student comparing Melbourne and Bendigo.** Melbourne: more job opportunities, transport, services; higher housing costs; air-quality concerns. Bendigo: lower costs, regional community, growing services; smaller employment base, fewer specialist services. The "better" choice depends on what the student and family value. *Follow the thinking:* list measures → identify the household's priorities → weigh → make a reasoned choice.

**6. Weighting matters.** Two ranking bodies can use the same data and reach different conclusions because of weighting. Honest analysis names the weights. *Quick check:* Why does naming weights make a ranking more useful?

**7. Influence on where people live.** Job opportunities and affordability are the largest movers. Family ties, lifestyle and climate also drive choices. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander connection to Country shapes some decisions. *Quick check:* What is one non-economic driver of internal migration in Australia?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Liveability Census and reports from local councils provide local data. The State of the City reports across Melbourne LGAs are useful sources.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Connection to Country shapes liveability for many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people; access to Country, family and cultural sites matter. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Different communities weight liveability differently: proximity to family, places of worship, multilingual services and culturally specific shops. The Victorian Multicultural Commission researches this.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You and a friend rank three Melbourne suburbs for liveability. Outline three measures you would use.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Casey produces a liveability report. Suggest three measures unique to the area.
3. **Vocational.** A junior planner at a Victorian council designs a liveability survey. Outline three questions she should include.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates whether "liveability rankings" should be public. Identify two considerations on each side.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Sudanese-Australian community group adapts liveability measures for their community. Outline three culturally relevant measures.

## Memorable takeaways
- Liveability is multi-dimensional.
- Measures and weights matter.
- Rankings depend on choices.
- People prioritise differently.
- Honest analysis names the weights.

## Watch out for these traps
- "There is one true ranking." Different measures produce different results.
- "More expensive = more liveable." Not always.
- "Everyone wants the same things." Cultural and family priorities vary.
- "Liveability is permanent." Places change over time.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "most liveable city" is contested.* Different methods, weights and audiences produce different rankings.
2. *Why green space matters.* Health, recreation, climate cooling, mental wellbeing, biodiversity.
3. *Wealthy place, low liveability.* High wealth with poor air, traffic, or social isolation; high cost driving inequality.
4. *Hard-to-quantify social measure.* Community connectedness; cultural belonging; safety from harassment.
5. *Why naming weights makes a ranking more useful.* Readers can judge the result against their own priorities.
6. *Non-economic driver of migration.* Family ties; climate preferences; cultural connections to Country.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Three Melbourne suburbs.* Public-transport access; green space; housing affordability.
2. *Casey liveability report.* Suburban-park access; multilingual services; transport links.
3. *Junior planner's survey.* Sense of safety; access to services; recreational satisfaction; specific local-area questions.
4. *"Liveability rankings" public debate.* For: informs choices. Against: oversimplifies and can drive inequality. Both sides agree on transparency.
5. *Sudanese-Australian community measures.* Proximity to community hubs; multilingual services; faith-based facilities.
