## Core content

**1. Voting is the floor, not the ceiling.** Voting in elections (federal, state, local) is compulsory in Australia for citizens over 18. It is the foundation of democratic participation. But many decisions happen between elections. *Quick check:* Why is voting "necessary but not sufficient"?

**2. Being informed.** Citizens get political information from public broadcasters (ABC, SBS), commercial news, independent media, social media, school education and government sources like the AEC. Quality matters: cross-check sources, look at primary documents like Hansard, and notice who is funding what. *Quick check:* Why is "I read it on social media" not enough by itself?

**3. Contacting elected representatives.** You can write, email, phone or visit your federal, state or local representative. Most MPs have suburb offices. Effective contact names the issue, gives your address (to show you are a constituent), and asks for a specific action. *Quick check:* Why does specifying the action you want make a contact more effective?

**4. Lobby and interest groups.** A **lobby group** is an organised body that aims to influence policy. An **interest group** represents shared concerns; it may or may not lobby. Examples: the Australian Council of Social Service, the Business Council of Australia, the Australian Conservation Foundation, the Council of Australian Postgraduate Associations. *Quick check:* What is one difference between joining a lobby group and acting alone?

**5. Worked example: a local issue in Melbourne's west.** Imagine a proposed freeway extension affecting Footscray. Citizens can: read council and government planning documents; attend public consultations; write to their local council, State MP and federal MP; join an interest group (e.g., neighbourhood association); contact a lobby group (e.g., environmental NGO); take direct action (a peaceful protest, a petition, a letter campaign). *Follow the thinking:* identify the issue → gather information → choose channels → coordinate effort → measure impact.

**6. Direct action.** **Direct action** is collective action that demonstrates support for a cause, like protests, marches, strikes, boycotts and petitions. It is legal when peaceful and within agreed boundaries. Civil disobedience (deliberately breaking a law to make a point) carries legal risk and is a separate decision. *Quick check:* Why is "peaceful" the key word in this descriptor?

**7. Digital participation.** Social media, e-petitions and online town-halls have expanded participation but also raised risks (misinformation, harassment). Knowing the limits and the affordances of digital tools is part of modern citizenship. *Quick check:* What is one advantage and one risk of an online e-petition compared with a paper one?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Parliament hosts public petitions on its website. Victorian local councils (Maribyrnong, Yarra, Greater Geelong) hold public consultation regularly. The Victorian Electoral Commission has accessible voter-education resources.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander participation has grown through dedicated bodies like the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria, advocacy organisations (e.g., the Victorian Aboriginal Legal Service), and individual representatives in parliament. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural participation includes councils such as the Ethnic Communities' Council of Victoria, language-specific media (SBS Radio in over 60 languages), and community associations across faiths. Engagement is stronger when materials and meetings are accessible in community languages.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Voting is enough." No. Decisions between elections need participation too.
- "Lobby groups are corrupt." Often controversial, but legally permitted advocacy is normal in democracies.
- "Direct action is illegal." No. Peaceful protest is a legal right.
- "Online petitions do nothing." Mixed; they raise awareness and can influence decisions when paired with other channels.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why voting is necessary but not sufficient.* It legitimises representatives but does not direct day-to-day decisions. Engaged citizens influence between elections too.
2. *Why social media alone is not enough.* Algorithms favour engagement over accuracy; misinformation spreads quickly; cross-checking with primary sources is essential.
3. *Why specific asks are more effective.* They give the recipient something concrete to consider or do, rather than a general complaint.
4. *Joining a group vs acting alone.* Groups amplify voices, share expertise, and persist over time. They can also dilute individual control over the message.
5. *Why "peaceful" is key.* Peaceful action stays within legal bounds and protects participants and bystanders, while pressuring decision-makers.
6. *Online vs paper petitions.* Online: broad reach, fast signing. Risk: lower verification, less accountability per signature. Paired with other channels, they can still influence decisions.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Safer pedestrian crossing.* Step 1: gather information from council and VicRoads on existing plans. Step 2: contact your local council and state MP with a specific ask (e.g., a school-zone refuge island). Step 3: collect supporting signatures from school families and present them.
2. *Knox tree-planting consultation.* Submit written feedback via the council's online portal; attend the public meeting; talk with neighbours and submit a joint position.
3. *ACF campaign.* Comply with charity-law rules on political activity; transparently disclose funding sources; ensure all claims are factual and well-sourced.
4. *School climate strike.* For: speech, assembly, civic learning. Against: school attendance obligations and impact on classmates. Reasonable plans involve parents and schools.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian aged-care.* Step 1: translate consultation materials and engage Vietnamese-language media. Step 2: hold a community meeting at a familiar venue with bilingual support. Step 3: submit a joint position via the local Ethnic Communities' Council representative.
