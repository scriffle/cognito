## Core content

**1. Lawmaking is a multi-stage process.** A bill in Australia goes through drafting, first reading, committee review, debate, votes in both houses, and royal assent. Citizens can influence it at almost every stage, not only on polling day. *Quick check:* Why does it matter that citizen input is not limited to elections?

**2. Contact with elected representatives.** The most direct route. Write, email, phone or meet your federal, state or local representative. Effective contact is clear about who you are, where you live, what you want, and why. *Quick check:* Why does identifying as a constituent strengthen your contact?

**3. Lobby groups and interest groups.** **Lobby groups** professionally engage decision-makers. **Interest groups** represent shared concerns. Both can amplify individual voices and bring expertise. Examples: ACOSS (welfare policy), the Business Council of Australia, ACTU (unions), state-level groups like Friends of the Earth Australia and the Victorian Trades Hall Council. *Quick check:* What does a lobbyist usually bring that an individual letter cannot?

**4. Direct action.** Peaceful protest, marches, petitions, boycotts and strikes signal the strength of public feeling. Direct action is legal when peaceful and within agreed limits. Its purpose is to put pressure on decision-makers. *Quick check:* Why might a sustained campaign be more effective than a single protest?

**5. Submissions to parliamentary committees.** A **parliamentary committee** is a small group of MPs that examines bills, policy areas or issues in detail. Committees usually call for **submissions** from the public. Submissions can be from individuals, groups or experts. They are read, summarised and influence committee recommendations. *Quick check:* Why does submission to a committee carry more weight than a generic letter?

**6. Worked example: a new online-safety bill.** A federal bill is referred to a Senate committee. The committee opens a public submissions period for six weeks. You research the bill, find a section that worries you (e.g., scope of surveillance), and submit a 300-word response with one specific recommended change. The committee report cites your submission and other input. The government responds, sometimes amending the bill before it returns to the chamber. *Follow the thinking:* track the bill → find the committee → read the issues paper → write a focused submission.

**7. Coordinated voices.** Single voices are easy to discount. A neighbourhood coalition, a school petition with 800 signatures, or a coordinated submission campaign by an interest group amplifies impact. Coalitions across party lines are especially persuasive. *Quick check:* Why does a coalition across party lines carry weight?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Parliament's website lists open inquiries by parliamentary committees. The Scrutiny of Acts and Regulations Committee reviews each new Victorian bill against human-rights and rule-of-law principles. Local government also runs public submissions on planning and amenity issues.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community organisations regularly make submissions to inquiries on health, justice and treaty matters. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria represents Aboriginal Victorians in treaty negotiations and shapes state-level lawmaking. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural and faith-based organisations file submissions in their members' interests, often translated into multiple languages. The Ethnic Communities' Council of Victoria coordinates many of these.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Only experts can write submissions." No. Committees explicitly welcome individual citizens.
- "Direct action and submissions are alternatives." No. Combining them works better.
- "If a bill passes, you have lost." No. Implementation, regulations and follow-up reviews are also influenceable.
- "Lobbying is illegal." No. It is legal and regulated; transparency rules apply.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why participation between elections matters.* Most policy is shaped after the vote, in the daily work of committees, regulations and amendments.
2. *Why constituent identity matters.* Representatives are accountable to people in their electorate, so contact from local voters carries more weight.
3. *What lobbyists bring.* Sustained relationships, expertise, and tracking of legislative detail that one-off letters lack.
4. *Why sustained campaigns work.* Decision-makers see persistence as proof of commitment and as a political risk worth addressing.
5. *Why submissions are stronger than generic letters.* Committees specifically call for them, read them, and reference them in reports.
6. *Why coalitions across party lines.* They show the issue transcends partisan interests and signal broader public support.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Safer pedestrian crossings.* Step 1: petition with local school families. Step 2: written contact to the council mayor and your state MP, with a specific design ask. Step 3: attend the council's planning meeting and speak briefly.
2. *Knox amenity bylaw.* Submit written feedback via the council's online portal; attend the public meeting and speak; mobilise a small group of supporters for the meeting.
3. *Community legal centre submission.* Clear summary of the issue; evidence (case examples, statistics); specific recommended changes with their reasons.
4. *Paid lobbyists debate.* For: provides expertise and access for under-resourced causes. Against: privileges those who can afford lobbying, distorting equal participation. Both sides agree transparency rules are essential.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian aged-care.* Translate consultation materials and brief community elders; organise a meeting at a familiar venue with bilingual support; submit a joint position through the Ethnic Communities' Council of Victoria.
