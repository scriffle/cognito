## What you are learning
- You are learning to **engage in democratic decision-making**, including **reaching consensus** and **voting**.
- You are learning to choose the right process for the decision in front of you.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **consensus**, **majority vote** and **quorum** in your own words.
- Compare consensus and voting for two types of decisions.
- Lead or contribute to a small-group democratic decision.
- Explain why the chosen process affects how legitimate the outcome feels.

## Core content

**1. What "democratic" means in a decision.** A **democratic** decision involves the people affected having a real say. It can be made through consensus, a vote, or a mix. The hallmark is participation and respect for the rules. *Quick check:* Why is "loudest voice wins" not democratic?

**2. Consensus decision-making.** **Consensus** is reached when everyone in the group can live with the decision, even if it is not their first choice. It takes time and active listening, and works best for small or trust-based groups. *Quick check:* What is one strength and one limit of consensus?

**3. Majority voting.** **Majority vote** is faster: a defined majority (e.g., more than half) decides. It works well for larger groups or when time is short. Australian parliaments use majority voting for most decisions. *Quick check:* What is the difference between simple majority and absolute majority?

**4. Quorum and procedure.** A **quorum** is the minimum number of people needed for a meeting to make a binding decision. Rules also cover who can speak, when to vote, and how the result is recorded. Without procedure, voting can be unfair. *Quick check:* Why does a meeting need a quorum?

**5. Worked example: school council meeting.** A Year 8 representative council in a Melbourne school decides how to spend a small budget. Step 1: agenda and quorum check. Step 2: open discussion to surface options. Step 3: short break to allow groups to talk. Step 4: a clarifying round before any vote. Step 5: try consensus on the simple items, vote on the contested item. Step 6: record the result in minutes. *Follow the thinking:* prepare → discuss → decide → record.

**6. Voting variations.** Australia uses **preferential voting** for the House of Representatives, **proportional voting** for the Senate, and **first-past-the-post** in some smaller contexts. Each system shapes which voices win. *Quick check:* Why does preferential voting encourage broader appeals to voters?

**7. Process legitimacy.** A decision is more legitimate when people see the process as fair: everyone could speak, the rule was clear in advance, and the result was recorded. *Quick check:* Why does "legitimate" depend on the process and not just the outcome?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Local Governance Association runs training in democratic decision-making for councillors. Many Victorian schools elect Student Representative Councils that use voting and consensus.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander decision-making traditions often emphasise consensus, listening, and inclusion of Elders and respected community members. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria uses elected representation to negotiate treaty. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Many community organisations across Melbourne (cultural, faith, service) blend consensus and voting in their governance. Multilingual facilitation helps include all members.

## Memorable takeaways
- Democratic means real say plus rules.
- Consensus listens; voting decides.
- Quorum and procedure matter.
- Voting systems shape who wins.
- Legitimate process beats popular outcome.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Consensus means everyone agrees." It means everyone can live with the decision.
- "Voting is always fair." Only if the rules and access are equal.
- "Loudest voice wins." That is the opposite of democratic.
- "Procedure is bureaucracy." It is what makes decisions trustworthy.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "loudest voice" is not democratic.* It excludes other voices and ignores rules; outcomes reflect intimidation rather than collective will.
2. *Consensus strength/limit.* Strength: builds buy-in and includes minority views. Limit: slow; can stall on hard choices.
3. *Simple vs absolute majority.* Simple: more votes than any other option. Absolute: more than half of all eligible voters.
4. *Why a quorum.* It ensures enough people are present that the decision represents the group, not a small slice.
5. *Why preferential voting encourages broader appeals.* Candidates need to be acceptable as a second or third preference, not just a first.
6. *Why process matters as much as outcome.* People accept results when the process was fair, even if they disagree with the decision.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Class sustainability project.* Step 1: brainstorm options openly. Step 2: clarify pros and cons. Step 3: vote, with the option to call for consensus on simple parts first.
2. *Maribyrnong workshop.* Small-group discussion (consensus per group); show-of-hands shortlist; dot-voting to prioritise items.
3. *Leadership-camp facilitator.* Set ground rules; rotate who speaks; use small-group rounds before big-group votes.
4. *Quieter students' views.* Use written notes or anonymous polls; have small-group rounds before plenary; train facilitators to invite all voices.
5. *Lunar New Year decision.* Step 1: gather options from each language group. Step 2: shortlist by consensus. Step 3: confirm with a simple vote and recorded decision.
