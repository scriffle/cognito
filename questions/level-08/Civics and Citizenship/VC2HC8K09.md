## Core content

**1. What "secular democracy" means.** A **secular** democracy means government does not adopt or favour any particular religion. Citizens can practise any faith (or none). Section 116 of the Constitution forbids the Commonwealth from establishing a state religion. *Quick check:* Why is "secular" different from "anti-religion"?

**2. Pluralism in practice.** **Pluralist** means many communities, traditions and viewpoints share public life. Public schools, hospitals and parliament include people of many backgrounds. **Multi-faith** society means many religious traditions (Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism and others) coexist with secular and non-religious people. *Quick check:* What is one practical sign of a pluralist society in your suburb?

**3. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories and cultures.** First Nations peoples have lived on this continent for at least 65,000 years. Their languages, knowledge, art and governance traditions shape Australia today, from place-names to art, science and law (e.g., native title since Mabo, 1992). The Uluru Statement from the Heart (2017) is a recent significant document. *Quick check:* Why does "deepest layer" capture First Nations cultural contribution?

**4. Christian and Western heritage.** Christian and broader European traditions shaped Australian institutions like parliament, courts, common law and education. Public holidays (Christmas, Easter), the calendar, parts of the legal system and many institutional practices reflect this heritage. *Quick check:* Is the Australian Westminster system of parliament a Christian or a Western political tradition?

**5. Worked example: Melbourne as a multicultural city.** Melbourne's suburbs reflect waves of migration: Italian and Greek heritage in Brunswick and Oakleigh, Vietnamese in Springvale and Footscray, Chinese in Box Hill, Indian in Wyndham, Sudanese and Ethiopian in Footscray and Sunshine, Lebanese in Coburg. Each community has shaped food, festivals, schools and businesses. Lunar New Year, Diwali, Vietnamese Tết, Greek Easter, Ramadan and Eid are public events. *Follow the thinking:* identify a tradition → trace its arrival → describe how it shapes Melbourne now.

**6. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander contributions today.** First Nations peoples lead in many sectors: arts (Aboriginal art is a major Australian cultural export), land management (cool burning, Country care), sport, science and political life. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Wadawurrung, Boon Wurrung and other Victorian peoples actively shape policy through Traditional Owner corporations and the First Peoples' Assembly. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

**7. Tensions and ongoing change.** Pluralism is not always smooth. Racism, religious intolerance and inequality persist. Public debate over the 2023 Voice referendum showed disagreement about how to recognise First Nations peoples. Pluralism is something a society maintains, not a destination it arrives at. *Quick check:* Why is "ongoing work" a better description than "achieved goal"?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria runs Multicultural Affairs and Citizenship through DFFH and the Victorian Multicultural Commission. Federation Square hosts inter-faith events. Acknowledgement of Country is now standard in Victorian public institutions.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Boon Wurrung/Bunurong, Wadawurrung, Gunaikurnai, Taungurung, Yorta Yorta, Dja Dja Wurrung, Eastern Maar, Gunditjmara and other peoples lead programs on cultural revitalisation, language, land management and treaty. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Annual events like Moomba (Melbourne), Antipodes Festival (Greek), Diwali at Federation Square, Vietnamese Lunar New Year in Footscray and Springvale, and Sudanese cultural festivals demonstrate multi-faith pluralism in practice.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Secular means religion is banned." No. It means government does not favour one.
- "Multicultural is recent." No. Migration has shaped Australia for over 200 years.
- "First Nations contribute only to art." No. Land management, science, law, governance and policy all benefit.
- "Pluralism is automatic." No. It needs active institutions and habits.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Secular vs anti-religion.* Secular means no state religion and equal treatment for all faiths. Anti-religion would mean treating religion as bad in general. Australia is the first, not the second.
2. *Pluralist sign in your suburb.* Multilingual signage; restaurants from many traditions; community language schools; multi-faith places of worship within walking distance.
3. *Why "deepest layer".* First Nations cultures predate every other Australian tradition by tens of thousands of years and are still shaping Australia today.
4. *Westminster as Christian or Western.* Primarily Western political tradition with Christian heritage influences, like other British-derived parliaments.
5. *Why "ongoing work".* Tensions, discrimination and inequality persist. Active institutions and habits are needed to maintain pluralism.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Acknowledgement of Country.* It recognises that First Nations peoples are the original custodians of the land we live on; it builds respect; it signals that Australia's institutions include First Nations voices.
2. *Greater Dandenong public spaces.* Multilingual signage; flexible-use community spaces for cultural and religious events; public-art commissions from multiple cultural backgrounds.
3. *Faith-leaders' breakfast.* Halal, kosher and vegetarian food options; respect for prayer times; gender-inclusive seating; clear agenda focused on shared local issues.
4. *Religious holidays in public schools.* For: cultural literacy, inclusion. Against: secular schooling and risk of favouring one tradition. Both sides agree fair representation matters.
5. *Iran new arrival.* Religious freedom is protected; many faiths coexist publicly; you can join a community of your faith or none, and your children can attend public, faith-based or independent schools.

Colonial layer: 19th-century industry along the river. Migrant communities: Greek, Italian and Maltese post-1945; Vietnamese from the 1970s; East African (Sudanese, Ethiopian, Somali) since the 1990s.
