## Core content

**1. National identity is multi-stranded.** Australian national identity is not a single story. It includes First Nations continuity over tens of thousands of years, colonial history, migration waves, and the lived experiences of multiple generations. *Quick check:* Why does "the Australian story" usually mean many stories told together?

**2. Citizenship as legal status and lived experience.** **Citizenship** is the legal status of being recognised as a citizen of a country. It also includes everyday experiences: language, work, participation, sense of belonging. Both matter. *Quick check:* What is one legal right unique to Australian citizens?

**3. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander connections to Country.** **Country** in First Nations contexts is more than land. It includes the seas, skies, rivers, kinship, language, stories, ancestors and responsibilities to care for places. Connection to Country is ongoing, not just historical. *Quick check:* Why is "Country" capitalised when used in this sense?

**4. Worked example: three perspectives on a single suburb.** Imagine Footscray, in Melbourne's west. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Country runs through and is recognised in public spaces and Acknowledgement of Country. Anglo-Australian working-class history shapes terrace houses and trade-union halls. Vietnamese migration since the 1970s shapes food, business and language. Sudanese, Ethiopian and Eritrean communities have added newer threads. Each tells a real Footscray story. *Follow the thinking:* identify the perspective → notice what it foregrounds → see how stories overlap.

**5. Migrant journeys.** **Migration** brings different experiences depending on the era, the reasons (work, family, refuge) and the policies of the time. Italian and Greek post-war migrants, Vietnamese refugees, South Asian skilled migrants, African humanitarian arrivals: each cohort has shaped Australia. *Quick check:* Why do "good migrant" and "bad migrant" frames misrepresent these stories?

**6. Tensions and reconciliation.** Some experiences of citizenship and identity have been negative: dispossession, the White Australia Policy, internment, the Stolen Generations, racial discrimination. Reconciliation Australia, the Uluru Statement from the Heart and the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria are recent responses. *Quick check:* Why does acknowledging hard history matter for shared identity?

**7. Civic identity as a public practice.** National identity is built by what we do publicly: voting, paying tax, serving on juries, volunteering, caring for neighbours and Country, learning about each other. *Quick check:* What is one civic action that includes everyone, regardless of background?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Immigration Museum (Old Customs House, Melbourne) tells migrant histories. The Koorie Heritage Trust in Federation Square shares Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories. Both run school programs.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Boon Wurrung, Wadawurrung, Gunaikurnai and other Victorian peoples maintain language, Country and governance through Traditional Owner corporations. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria is negotiating treaty. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant memoirs, multilingual newspapers, faith communities and cultural festivals carry first-person stories of Australian citizenship across many generations and contexts.

## Watch out for these traps
- "There is one Australian story." No. There are many overlapping stories.
- "First Nations peoples lost their connection to Country." No. Connection continues, even where dispossession has occurred.
- "Migrants assimilate completely." Most migrants integrate, keeping aspects of their heritage and shaping Australia in turn.
- "Acknowledging difficult history divides people." Honesty about history is the basis for genuine unity.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why many stories together.* No single account captures First Nations, colonial and migration experiences over 65,000+ years.
2. *Legal right unique to citizens.* Voting in federal elections is restricted to Australian citizens 18 and over.
3. *Why "Country" is capitalised.* It marks a specific First Nations concept distinct from generic land or country.
4. *Why "good/bad migrant" frames mislead.* They reduce complex stories to simple labels and ignore the structural conditions migrants face.
5. *Why acknowledging hard history matters.* Without it, trust and reconciliation rest on incomplete foundations.
6. *Civic action including everyone.* Voting (for citizens), volunteering, caring for neighbours and shared spaces, learning history together.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Older Australian on national identity.* You may hear about: a major migration journey; a wartime experience; participation in community organisations; views shaped by events you have not lived through.
2. *Greater Dandenong citizenship ceremony.* A Welcome to Country, multilingual programs, and inclusion of migrant elders sharing brief reflections.
3. *Immigration Museum discussion questions.* What stories are missing? Whose voices are loudest? What does the museum ask us to do differently?
4. *Asking for stories.* Issues: consent and emotional impact (some stories carry trauma). Considerations: only invite voluntary sharing; offer support and acknowledge limits.
5. *Yorta Yorta Elder visit.* Prepare students in advance; listen attentively without interrupting; follow up with reflective writing or art that respects what was shared.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Group: Sudanese-Australian community in Melbourne.* Experience: humanitarian migration since the 1990s, building businesses, schools and sports clubs. Tension: experiences of racial profiling and media stereotyping. Strength: leadership in sports (e.g., AFL players, basketball) and community advocacy. Sources cited.
