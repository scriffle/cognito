# VC2HC8S03 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC8S03. Explain the cultural, religious and/or social influences on Australia's democratic institutions.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship (Evaluating democratic institutions and systems).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Institutions did not appear from nowhere.
  - Cultural, religious and social currents shape what they do.

By the end of this you should be able to:

## Core content

**1. What an institution is.** An **institution** in civics is a long-standing organisation or set of rules that shapes public life: parliament, courts, the public service, schools, the Reserve Bank, the ABC. Institutions outlast individuals and governments. *Quick check:* Why is "outlast" part of what makes an institution?

**2. Cultural influences.** **Cultural** influences shape values, language and practices in institutions. The Westminster system of parliament came from Britain. The "fair go" idea, the role of unions and the use of the Westminster English language all draw on cultural inheritance. *Quick check:* What is one Westminster feature visible in Australian parliament?

**3. Religious influences.** Australia is a **secular state** (no official religion), yet religious influences are real: parliamentary prayer, public holidays at Christmas and Easter, oath options on the Bible or affirmation. Many social reforms (welfare, hospitals, schools) involved religious organisations. *Quick check:* Why is "secular state with religious influences" not a contradiction?

**4. Social influences.** **Social** influences include movements (women's suffrage, anti-Vietnam-War protests, LGBTIQ+ rights, environmental campaigns), economic changes, and the slow democratisation of who can participate. The 1967 referendum was driven by First Nations campaigners and broad public support. *Quick check:* What is one social movement that changed an Australian institution?

**5. First Nations influences.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples shape institutions through their continuing law, advocacy and engagement: native title since Mabo (1992), the Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation, the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria. The 2023 Voice referendum proposed an Indigenous body to advise parliament; it did not pass. The Uluru Statement remains a public document. *Quick check:* Why does shaping institutions take long-term work?

**6. Worked example: Australian Sunday trading.** Why was Sunday once a day off for most shops? Religious influence (Christian sabbath), social influence (workers' rest day), cultural influence (British inheritance). Across decades, social and economic changes (mass migration, women's workforce participation, retail competition) loosened those rules. Today, Sunday trading is common, but penalty rates still reflect the social value placed on weekend time. *Follow the thinking:* trace the original layered influence → identify pressures for change → describe the result.

**7. Influence is not control.** Influences shape institutions but do not determine them. Institutions adapt, sometimes slowly, to new pressures. Layered influences make change complex but possible. *Quick check:* Why is layered influence a feature of stable democracies?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian Parliament includes both a prayer and an Acknowledgement of Country at the start of each sitting. The Victorian Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006) was influenced by international human-rights ideas as well as local advocacy.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria is actively shaping Victorian institutions through treaty negotiations. The Koori Court reflects influence on the criminal justice system. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural advisory bodies, multilingual government communications and faith-based community organisations all influence Australian institutions. Major faiths have advisory roles in chaplaincy programs in hospitals, schools and parliaments.

## Memorable takeaways
- Institutions outlast people.
- Influence is cultural, religious, social.
- Australia is secular, not non-religious.
- First Nations influence is ongoing.
- Influence is not control.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Australia is a Christian country." Constitutionally secular; religious influences are real but not official.
- "Institutions never change." They do, often slowly.
- "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander influence is only symbolic." It is also practical, including treaty processes and legislation.
- "Only majority groups shape institutions." Minority advocacy has shaped many Australian reforms.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "outlast" matters.* Institutions persist across changes of leadership, providing stability and continuity.
2. *Westminster feature.* Two houses, a Prime Minister chosen by the majority party, a Speaker, and parliamentary committees.
3. *Why "secular state with religious influences" is not contradictory.* Secular means no state religion. People and traditions still bring religious values into public life.
4. *Social movement changing an institution.* The women's suffrage movement (Victorian women gained the vote in 1908); the 1967 referendum campaign; same-sex-marriage advocacy.
5. *Why shaping institutions takes long-term work.* Rules, habits and networks are slow to shift; sustained advocacy and multiple campaigns are usually needed.
6. *Why layered influence is a feature.* It prevents domination by a single source and allows change to be considered from multiple angles.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Citizenship ceremony.* Australian anthem (cultural and political), Acknowledgement of Country (First Nations), oath or affirmation including option to invoke a religious text (religious and secular together).
2. *Casey council procedures.* Acknowledgement of Country; meeting times that accommodate diverse community schedules; translation support; faith-based dietary considerations for events.
3. *Union influence research.* Hansard records of union-related debates; Trades Hall archives; Auditor-General or government reports on industrial relations.
4. *Parliamentary prayer debate.* For: tradition and continuity, freedom to invoke faith. Against: secular state principles, inclusion of non-religious citizens. Both sides can be reconciled with alternative practices.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian advocacy.* Step 1: identify the relevant council committee and decisions. Step 2: organise a coordinated submission with translated materials. Step 3: attend meetings and follow up through trusted community-organisation channels.
