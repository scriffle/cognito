# VC2HC8S08 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC8S08. Develop an analysis of civics and citizenship issues with knowledge and evidence using multiple methods of communication.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship (Communicating).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Analysis combines claim, evidence and reasoning.
  - Different audiences need different formats.
  - Communication is part of the analysis, not after it.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Literacy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop an analysis** of civics and citizenship issues **with knowledge and evidence**, using **multiple methods of communication**.
- You are learning to make a case people can read, watch or hear.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **analysis**, **format** and **audience** in your own words.
- Construct a short evidence-based argument on a civic issue.
- Adapt the same argument for two different formats.
- Cite at least three reputable sources.

## Core content

**1. What analysis means.** **Analysis** breaks an issue into parts, examines evidence, considers perspectives, and reaches a justified conclusion. It is not a summary or an opinion. *Quick check:* Why is "what I think" weaker than "what the evidence shows"?

**2. Claim, evidence, reasoning.** A solid civic analysis names a claim, supports it with evidence (statistics, court rulings, quotes from reputable sources), and explains the reasoning that links them. *Quick check:* What is missing if you give claim and evidence but no reasoning?

**3. Choosing a format.** Civics analysis can be a written report, a short essay, a poster, an infographic, a podcast, a video or a presentation. The right format depends on audience, length, complexity and access. *Quick check:* Which format suits a 90-second peer-shared message?

**4. Worked example: communicating about a council planning decision.** Issue: a proposed new bike lane in inner Melbourne. Audience 1: peers at school — a 90-second video with three facts and a call to share thoughts. Audience 2: council — a 200-word written submission with two sources cited. Audience 3: a primary-school class — a picture-book page with a simple map and one question. Same analysis, three artefacts. *Follow the thinking:* identify the audience → pick a format → adapt evidence and language → cite sources.

**5. Citing sources.** Cite at least three reputable sources, including at least one primary source (e.g., a council document, court ruling, ABS data). Citation is part of trustworthy analysis. *Quick check:* Why does citing a source strengthen your analysis?

**6. Reasoned conclusions.** A conclusion is not the same as a slogan. It states the position, acknowledges limits or alternatives, and points to next steps if relevant. *Quick check:* Why does naming limits make your conclusion stronger?

**7. Accessibility and inclusion.** Plain English, multilingual versions when relevant, alt text on images, and captions on videos make your analysis available to more people. *Quick check:* Why are captions on videos a civic accessibility issue?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Local Victorian newspapers (e.g., The Age, Herald Sun), independent media (Crikey, The Saturday Paper), public broadcasters (ABC, SBS) and academic-public sites (The Conversation Australia) provide models of civic communication.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander voices in civic communication include NITV (National Indigenous Television), IndigenousX and individual columnists. Cite these sources directly rather than paraphrasing without credit. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** SBS Radio broadcasts in over 60 languages. Multilingual newspapers and community organisations carry civic analysis in many communities; access often improves when materials are produced in community languages.

2. **Civic.** Your council in Knox runs a youth civic essay competition. Suggest three things judges should look for.

## Memorable takeaways
- Analysis is more than opinion.
- Claim, evidence, reasoning.
- Audience first, format second.
- Cite at least three sources.
- Accessibility is part of the message.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "Claim: ___. Evidence: ___ from ___. Reasoning: this supports the claim because ___. Conclusion: ___, with limits ___."
- **Words to keep close.** analysis, claim, evidence, reasoning, format, citation, accessibility.
- **Try this picture.** Build a "communication compass": at the centre the analysis; the points are audience, format, evidence and call to action. Fill it in for one issue.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Take one analysis and produce two artefacts (one written, one visual) on the same issue.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one civic issue. Build a short analysis (claim, two pieces of evidence, reasoning, conclusion) and adapt it for two formats. Cite at least three sources by name and date. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Opinion is analysis." Analysis needs evidence and reasoning, not just a view.
- "Quoting equals analysing." Use quotes sparingly; the reasoning is yours.
- "Citations are decoration." They are the basis for trust.
- "One format suits all audiences." Tailor it.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "what evidence shows" beats "what I think".* Evidence allows others to test the claim; opinion alone cannot be checked.
2. *Missing reasoning.* The argument fails to explain why the evidence supports the claim; the reader is left to guess.
3. *90-second peer message format.* Short video with captions and one call to action.
4. *Why citing strengthens analysis.* It shows your sources, makes claims checkable, and demonstrates that you have read more than one perspective.
5. *Why limits matter.* They build trust and prevent overclaiming.
6. *Why captions are civic.* They make information accessible to deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences and to people watching in noisy environments.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *School-council shade.* Written submission: 200 words with a temperature chart from the school playground and a citation to local heat data. Video: a 60-second walk-through of hot spots, ending with one call to action.
2. *Knox youth essay.* Judges look for: clear claim, named evidence with sources, balanced acknowledgement of counter-arguments.
3. *NGO media kit.* Media release, fact sheet, short video. Avoid hype; keep the tone factual and well-sourced.
4. *Peer experience in a campaign.* Issues: consent and protection of the person. Get clear permission, allow review of the final piece, and provide an opt-out path.
5. *Communicating a Wadawurrung-led finding.* Step 1: co-author with Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: use plain language with named credit. Step 3: review before public release.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: better public transport at a Victorian regional school.* Analysis: claim that current services do not meet demand; evidence from PTV timetables and student survey; reasoning that links scheduling gaps to school access. Adapted to a 200-word council submission and a 60-second video for peers. Sources cited.
