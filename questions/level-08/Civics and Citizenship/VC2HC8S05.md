# VC2HC8S05 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC8S05. Explain how political actors exercise power in democratic institutions.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship (Evaluating democratic institutions and systems).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Power is exercised through specific roles, not just by holding office.
  - Different actors have different powers and limits.
  - Naming the power makes accountability possible.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **explain** how **political actors exercise power** in **democratic institutions**.
- You are learning to read the power moves of real people, not just diagrams.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **political actor**, **power** and **executive** in your own words.
- Identify three different kinds of power exercised in Australia.
- Describe how one actor can use power within and through institutions.
- Justify why "exercise" is a more accurate verb than "hold".

## Core content

**1. Who counts as a political actor.** **Political actors** include elected representatives (MPs, councillors), executive office-holders (Prime Minister, Premier, ministers), public servants, judges, lobby groups, party officials and journalists. Each operates inside institutions. *Quick check:* Why does this list include people who are not elected?

**2. Forms of power.** Common forms in Australia: **legislative power** (passing laws), **executive power** (running government), **judicial power** (interpreting laws), **soft power** (influence through media, expertise, public opinion), and **resource power** (controlling budgets and people). *Quick check:* Which two forms of power are usually held by the Prime Minister?

**3. Power is exercised, not just held.** Holding a position is not the same as using it. The Prime Minister exercises power by setting cabinet agendas, choosing ministers, negotiating with parliament, and speaking publicly. Each act is a use of power. *Quick check:* Why is "exercise" more accurate than "hold"?

**4. Worked example: a federal Treasurer at budget time.** A Treasurer exercises power by: setting the budget priorities (executive), drafting bills with Treasury (resource/legislative), negotiating with caucus and the Opposition (party/political), defending decisions in parliament (legislative), and explaining the budget on television (soft). Each exercise is visible and reviewable. *Follow the thinking:* identify the actor → list the powers → trace one decision through each.

**5. Limits on power.** Constitutional limits (e.g., the High Court can rule a law invalid), institutional checks (parliament can refuse to pass a bill), and political checks (voters, media, party rooms) all limit how power is used. *Quick check:* What is one example of an Australian political actor's power being limited recently?

**6. Soft and hard power.** **Hard power** is formal authority (passing a law, signing a contract). **Soft power** is influence (setting media agendas, mobilising public support). Both matter. *Quick check:* Why is soft power often used before hard power?

**7. Accountability for power.** Every exercise of power can be questioned, recorded and reviewed. Hansard records what MPs say; courts publish judgments; the Auditor-General reviews spending; voters review at elections. *Quick check:* Why does accountability work better when power use is visible?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Premier and ministers exercise executive power across portfolios. IBAC investigates allegations of corrupt use of power. The Victorian Auditor-General reports on executive resource power.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria exercises authority within its mandate and engages directly with the Victorian Government on treaty matters. Traditional Owner corporations exercise power over land management decisions in their Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural advisory bodies, community associations and faith leaders exercise soft power through advice, advocacy and voter mobilisation. Multilingual media shape how political actors are seen by different communities.

2. **Civic.** Your council mayor is criticised by residents at a meeting. Outline three legitimate ways the mayor can respond.
3. **Vocational.** A junior parliamentary researcher tracks how a Victorian bill is shaped before it is introduced. Outline three actors and one type of power each exercises.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates whether private donations to political parties give donors too much power. Identify two ethical considerations.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A community group wants to influence policy without owning a lobbying budget. Outline three ways to exercise soft power.

## Memorable takeaways
- Power is exercised, not just held.
- Many actors, many forms.
- Hard and soft both shape outcomes.
- Limits and checks are part of the system.
- Accountability needs visibility.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Only the elected hold power." Many unelected actors exercise real power (officials, courts, media).
- "Soft power is not real power." It often determines what gets onto the agenda before any vote.
- "Power is bad." Power can be used well or badly. It is normal in democracies.
- "Aboriginal organisations are advisory only." Some exercise statutory and treaty-based power.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why include unelected actors.* They have real influence: courts decide cases, public servants implement policy, journalists shape framing, lobby groups press positions.
2. *PM's two forms of power.* Executive (running government) and legislative influence (leading the majority party that controls the House of Representatives).
3. *Why "exercise" not "hold".* Holding a role does not show what actually happens. Exercising it is observable and reviewable.
4. *Example of power limited recently.* Independent court rulings overturning government decisions; a state premier walking back a policy after sustained public pressure; a federal bill amended after Senate negotiation.
5. *Why soft power comes first.* It shapes the agenda and builds the case before formal votes are needed.
6. *Why accountability needs visibility.* Hidden power use cannot be challenged.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Minister "announced" a policy.* Executive (deciding), soft (media use to set the agenda), and resource (assigning budget and staff).
2. *Mayor responds to criticism.* Address the concerns directly at the meeting; commit to follow-up actions; publish a written response on the council site.
3. *Bill shaping.* Drafting team in the department (resource and executive); minister (executive); parliamentary committee (legislative).
4. *Private donations debate.* For: free political association. Against: undue influence and unequal voice. Both sides agree disclosure rules matter.
5. *Community group soft power.* Build coalitions with other groups; engage media with personal stories; submit to parliamentary committees and brief MPs in person.
