# VC2HC8S03 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Explain the cultural, religious and/or social influences on Australia's democratic institutions.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Institutions outlast people. Influence is cultural, religious, social. Layered, not single-cause.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **explain** **cultural, religious and social influences** on Australia's **democratic institutions**.
- You should be able to trace where institutions get their shape.

## Core content

An **institution** is a long-standing organisation or set of rules that shapes public life: parliament, courts, the public service, schools, the ABC. **Cultural** influences include the Westminster system inherited from Britain, the English language and the "fair go". **Religious** influences include parliamentary prayer, public holidays and oath options, even though Australia is a **secular state**. **Social** influences include movements (suffrage, anti-war, LGBTIQ+ rights, environmental campaigns) and economic change.

First Nations peoples shape institutions through continuing law and advocacy: native title since Mabo (1992), the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria, the Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation. Influence is not control: institutions adapt to layered pressures, sometimes slowly.

**Worked example.** Sunday trading. Originally a religious sabbath day for shops in a British-influenced society — cultural, religious and social influences combined. Mass migration, women's workforce participation and retail competition loosened the rules over decades. Today Sunday trading is common, but penalty rates reflect the social value still placed on weekend time.

**Cultural context.** Victorian Parliament includes both a prayer and an Acknowledgement of Country at the start of each sitting. The Koori Court reflects First Nations influence on the criminal justice system. Multicultural advisory bodies and multilingual government communications continue to shape institutions. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Institutions outlast people.
- Influence is cultural, religious, social.
- Australia is secular, not non-religious.
- Influence is not control.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Australia is a Christian country." Constitutionally secular; religious influences are real but not official.
- "Institutions never change." They do, often slowly.
- "Aboriginal influence is only symbolic." It is also practical, including treaty processes and legislation.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why does "outlast" matter?* Institutions provide continuity across changes of leadership.
- *Westminster feature.* Two houses, PM chosen by the majority party, a Speaker, parliamentary committees.
- *Why is "secular state with religious influences" not contradictory?* Secular means no state religion. People still bring religious values into public life.
- *One social movement that changed an institution.* Women's suffrage (Victorian women voted from 1908); the 1967 referendum; same-sex-marriage advocacy.
- *Short summary of Sunday trading.* Layered religious, cultural and social influences set a day off; later social and economic change loosened the rule; penalty rates carry the social legacy forward.
