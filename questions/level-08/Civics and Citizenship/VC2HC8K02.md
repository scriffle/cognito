# VC2HC8K02 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC8K02. The extent to which Australia's institutions and systems reflect democratic values, such as freedom of speech, association, assembly, religion and movement.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship (Government and democracy).

## What you are learning
- You are learning about the **extent** to which Australia's **institutions and systems reflect democratic values**, such as **freedom of speech, association, assembly, religion and movement**.
- You are learning to judge how well institutions live up to their stated values.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **democratic value**, **freedom of speech** and **freedom of assembly** in your own words.
- Identify three Australian institutions that protect democratic values.
- Discuss one example where a value is balanced against another.
- Explain why "reflects" allows for partial as well as full reflection.

## Core content

**1. What democratic values are.** A **democratic value** is a principle most democracies say they support: freedom, fairness, equality, accountability, participation. Specific values include freedom of speech, association, assembly, religion and movement. *Quick check:* Why is "value" different from "law"?

**2. Where the values sit.** Some are explicit in the Australian Constitution (freedom of religion, s 116). Others are in legislation (anti-discrimination laws), common law (the implied freedom of political communication), or international treaties Australia has signed. *Quick check:* Why does it matter where a freedom is written down?

**3. Freedoms in practice.** **Freedom of speech** lets people criticise government without being jailed. **Freedom of assembly** lets people gather to protest. **Freedom of religion** lets people practise their faith (or none). **Freedom of association** lets people join groups, unions or parties. **Freedom of movement** lets people travel between States. *Quick check:* Which freedom is engaged when a teenager joins a climate-strike rally outside Victorian Parliament?

**4. Worked example: a climate protest in Melbourne.** Imagine students plan a rally in Spring Street to lobby parliament. Freedoms involved: speech (chants and signs), assembly (gathering), association (the organising group), movement (people travelling in). Limits: police can require permits and reroute crowds for safety. Courts can review whether limits go too far. *Follow the thinking:* identify the values → identify limits → identify the institution that balances them.

**5. Reflection is partial.** Institutions reflect democratic values **in part**. Indigenous Australians had no formal political rights until decades after federation. Women could not vote in all elections until 1908. People sleeping rough still face restrictions on assembly in some places. The phrase "extent to which" asks you to assess, not just describe. *Quick check:* Why is "extent to which" different from "whether"?

**6. Values can clash.** Freedom of speech can collide with anti-discrimination protections. Freedom of movement can be limited during a pandemic. Courts, parliament and public debate weigh these tensions. *Quick check:* What is one way these tensions are normally resolved?

**7. International comparisons.** Australia is signatory to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966). It does not have a federal Bill of Rights, unlike the United States or Canada. Victoria's Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006) is the closest state-level analogue. *Quick check:* Why might absence of a national Bill of Rights matter when an institution limits a freedom?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006) requires public authorities to consider human rights. The Victorian Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission investigates complaints. Trades Hall in Carlton hosts assemblies that test freedom of association.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples were not counted in the census until the 1967 referendum changed the Constitution. The Mabo decision (1992) recognised native title. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria leads treaty negotiations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Australia's freedom of religion has been tested in cases involving Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist and Christian communities. Multilingual access to legal services helps protect freedom of speech and assembly for migrant communities in Melbourne's west and south-east.

## Memorable takeaways
- Values guide institutions.
- Constitution protects some; legislation protects more.
- Partial reflection is the norm.
- Values clash; courts and parliament balance.
- Australia lacks a federal Bill of Rights.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Freedom of speech means no consequences." No. Defamation, threats and incitement laws still apply.
- "All Australians have always had these freedoms." No. Many were extended only over recent decades.
- "Limits on freedoms are always bad." No. Some limits protect other people's freedoms.
- "Australia is exactly like the US for free speech." No. The First Amendment is much broader than Australia's implied freedoms.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Value vs law.* A value is a principle people agree should guide action. A law is a specific rule that can be enforced.
2. *Why "where written down" matters.* Constitutional protections are harder to change than legislation. Common-law freedoms can shift through new court decisions.
3. *Climate-strike value.* Freedom of speech, assembly and association (and movement, if travel is required).
4. *Why "extent to which" not "whether".* It invites judgement of degree, allowing partial answers and trade-offs rather than yes/no.
5. *Normal way to resolve clashes.* Through legislation (parliament weighs and writes the rule) and courts (apply the rule to cases).
6. *Why absence of Bill of Rights matters.* Limits on freedoms may be harder to challenge in court without an explicit catalogue of rights.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Say anything without consequences".* The law allows criticism and dissent, but limits apply for defamation, incitement to violence and other harms. Limits protect other people's rights.
2. *Yarra council protest approval.* Safety of participants and bystanders; impact on traffic and emergency services; existing right to peaceful assembly.
3. *VEOHRC complaint review.* Whether the conduct meets the legal definition of discrimination, whether reasonable accommodation was offered, and whether any exemption applies.
4. *Anti-discrimination vs free speech.* For limits: equality requires people not face hate speech in workplaces and public services. Against: broad limits risk chilling legitimate debate. Both sides agree clear rules and fair processes matter.
5. *Sudanese-Australian vigil.* Notify police of the route and time; nominate a clear coordinator; check Victoria Police protest guidelines on safe assembly.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Value: freedom of religion.* Institutions: Constitution s 116 (Commonwealth cannot establish religion), VEOHRC. Current limit: religious-exemption debates in employment law. Challenge case: Williams v Commonwealth (No 2) (2014), which involved Commonwealth funding for chaplaincy programs. Sources cited.
