## Core content

**1. Criminal law.** **Criminal law** covers offences against the state, such as assault, theft, fraud and homicide. The state prosecutes; if convicted, the accused faces penalties (fines, community work, prison). The standard of proof is **beyond reasonable doubt**. *Quick check:* Why does the state prosecute, not the victim?

**2. Civil law.** **Civil law** covers disputes between individuals or organisations, like contracts, negligence, family matters and defamation. A **plaintiff** sues a **defendant** for a remedy, usually damages. The standard is **on the balance of probabilities**. *Quick check:* Why is the standard of proof lower in civil cases?

**3. Standards of proof.** Criminal cases require very high confidence in guilt because the consequences (loss of liberty) are severe. Civil cases require ordinary confidence because the dispute is about who should pay or do what. *Quick check:* What does "balance of probabilities" mean in everyday language?

**4. Worked example: the same event in two courts.** Imagine a car accident in suburban Melbourne. Criminal: police charge the driver with dangerous driving. The state prosecutes. The standard is beyond reasonable doubt. Penalty if convicted: fine, licence loss or jail. Civil: the injured passenger sues the driver for damages to cover medical costs. The standard is on the balance of probabilities. Outcome: a monetary award if the plaintiff succeeds. Both proceedings can run from the same incident. *Follow the thinking:* identify the parties → identify the question → apply the right standard → identify the remedy.

**5. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander customary law.** **Customary law** is a continuing system of law in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, developed over thousands of years. It covers conduct, ceremony, relationships and Country. It is distinct from Australian statutory and common law, with its own authorities and processes. *Quick check:* Why might saying "the law" without qualifying which law be inaccurate in Australia?

**6. Recognition in Australian law.** Australian law recognises some aspects of customary law, including: native title since Mabo (1992); the Native Title Act 1993; and culturally responsive courts like the Koori Court in Victoria. Customary law is not yet fully integrated, and Victorian treaty negotiations are exploring how recognition could deepen. *Quick check:* Why are partial recognition and treaty negotiations different things?

**7. International and emerging types.** Australia is also bound by aspects of international law (through treaties) and adapts to emerging issues like cybercrime, environmental law and human-rights law. These cut across criminal and civil categories. *Quick check:* Why does climate change create both civil and criminal legal questions?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria Police investigate criminal matters; the Office of Public Prosecutions prosecutes serious offences. Civil matters in Victoria are heard in VCAT (Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal) and the higher courts. The Koori Court is a specialist culturally responsive division of the Magistrates' Court.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Victorian Aboriginal Legal Service represents Aboriginal Victorians in courts. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria is engaging in treaty negotiations that may extend recognition of customary law. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Court interpreter services and multilingual legal-aid clinics support people from non-English-speaking backgrounds in both criminal and civil proceedings, especially in Melbourne's south-east and west.

## Memorable takeaways
- Criminal: state vs accused, very high proof.
- Civil: parties dispute, ordinary proof.
- Same event, two cases possible.
- Customary law is its own continuing system.
- Recognition is partial and evolving.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Civil law is less serious." It can have huge financial consequences and serious impact on lives.
- "Customary law is folklore." No. It is a continuing system with authorities and processes.
- "If you win the criminal case, you must also win the civil case." Not always; different parties, different standards.
- "Aboriginal customary law is fully recognised." No. Recognition is partial and evolving.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why the state prosecutes.* Crimes are treated as harm to the public order, not just the individual. The state acts on behalf of society and ensures consistent prosecution.
2. *Why the civil standard is lower.* The consequence is usually monetary, not loss of liberty. Society is willing to resolve disputes on ordinary evidence.
3. *"Balance of probabilities" in everyday language.* More likely than not — roughly more than 50% confidence in the plaintiff's version.
4. *Why "the law" alone can mislead.* Australia has multiple sources and systems of law (statutory, common, customary). Naming which one matters.
5. *Why partial recognition and treaty differ.* Partial recognition is current Australian-law inclusion of some customary-law aspects (e.g., native title). A treaty would be a negotiated agreement that may extend recognition more broadly.
6. *Climate change as civil and criminal.* Civil: negligence and corporate liability claims. Criminal: laws against environmental pollution and offences breaching specific regulations.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Lawsuit against a company.* Civil law. Balance of probabilities. Remedy likely monetary damages or a court order to change behaviour.
2. *Whitehorse community-law night.* Exhibits: VCAT walkthrough; criminal-vs-civil comparison wall; Koori Court explainer (with community-approved content).
3. *Junior paralegal.* Separate court timelines; separate parties (state in one, individuals in the other); separate standards of proof and remedies.
4. *Customary-law recognition debate.* For: respect for continuing First Nations law and self-determination. Against: legal certainty and equal treatment concerns. Both sides need to engage with the views of First Nations themselves.
5. *VCAT tenancy support.* Court interpreter; written information in their language; access to a community legal centre for advice.
