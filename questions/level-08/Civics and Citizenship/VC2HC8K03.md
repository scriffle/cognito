## Core content

**1. What the Constitution does.** A **constitution** is a set of rules for how a country is governed. The Australian Constitution, in force since 1 January 1901, created the federation, set out parliament's powers, and divides responsibilities between the Commonwealth and the States. It is hard to change: changes need a referendum that passes nationally and in a majority of States. *Quick check:* Why is a hard-to-change rule book useful as well as frustrating?

**2. Three branches of government.** The **legislature** (parliament) makes laws. The **executive** (the Prime Minister, Ministers and Departments) carries them out. The **judiciary** (courts) interprets them. The branches check each other. *Quick check:* Which branch would you contact about a new law you wanted, and which about how an existing law applies to your case?

**3. The Commonwealth parliament.** Australia's parliament has two houses: the House of Representatives and the Senate. Laws must pass both. The **Prime Minister** is the leader of the party (or coalition) with majority support in the House of Representatives. Ministers run government departments and report to parliament. *Quick check:* Why does parliament have two houses?

**4. Worked example: a new federal law.** Imagine a proposed law on online safety. A Minister, supported by their department, drafts the **bill**. The House of Representatives debates and votes; the Senate debates and votes. If both pass it, the **Governor-General** gives royal assent and it becomes law. If the High Court is later asked, it can rule whether the law is consistent with the Constitution. *Follow the thinking:* idea → drafting (executive) → debate and vote (legislature) → assent (Governor-General) → application and review (judiciary).

**5. The Governor-General.** The **Governor-General** represents the King of Australia as head of state. In practice, the role is mostly ceremonial and follows the elected government's advice. Reserve powers (like dismissing a government) exist but are rarely used; the 1975 dismissal of Prime Minister Whitlam is the famous example. *Quick check:* Why is "ceremonial role" not the same as "no power"?

**6. State and Territory governments.** Australia is a **federation**: the Commonwealth shares power with six States and two self-governing Territories. States make laws on areas like schools, hospitals and police; the Commonwealth covers defence, immigration and taxation. Some areas overlap, like health and education funding. *Quick check:* Which level of government runs your public school?

**7. The High Court of Australia.** The **High Court** is the final court of appeal and the interpreter of the Constitution. Its decisions, such as Mabo v Queensland (No. 2) in 1992, can reshape Australian society. Mabo recognised native title and overturned the legal fiction of *terra nullius*. *Quick check:* Why does interpreting the Constitution sometimes change the country?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's Parliament sits at Spring Street, Melbourne. Local federal Members of Parliament hold offices in suburbs across the state. The High Court occasionally sits in Melbourne. The Victorian Electoral Commission runs state elections; the Australian Electoral Commission runs federal elections.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Australia's Constitution was written without consultation with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Section 51(xxvi) (the "races power") was changed by the 1967 referendum to allow the Commonwealth to make laws for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. The Uluru Statement from the Heart (2017) and the unsuccessful 2023 Voice referendum are key recent moments. In Victoria, the First Peoples' Assembly is negotiating a treaty with the State Government. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Australia's parliament has grown more diverse, with members from Vietnamese, Indian, Chinese, Greek, Lebanese, Sudanese and many other backgrounds. Multilingual electoral materials and interpreter services reflect that the institutions serve everyone, not only English-speaking citizens.

## Watch out for these traps
- "The Prime Minister is the head of state." No. The King of Australia is, represented by the Governor-General.
- "All laws are made by the Prime Minister." No. Parliament passes laws; the PM leads the government and proposes most bills.
- "The Constitution is just history." No. It actively shapes today's politics through court rulings and referendum debates.
- "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people had no role in shaping Australia's institutions." No. Recent decades have seen significant influence through court cases, advocacy and constitutional change proposals, although recognition remains incomplete.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why hard-to-change rules are useful.* They give stability and protect minority rights from being overridden by passing majorities. They are frustrating when reform is genuinely needed.
2. *Branches to contact.* For a new law, contact a parliamentarian (legislature). For how an existing law applies to your case, contact a court or legal-aid service (judiciary).
3. *Why two houses.* The House of Representatives represents populations; the Senate gives equal representation to each State, balancing power between bigger and smaller States.
4. *Why "ceremonial" is not "no power".* The Governor-General has reserve powers that can be used in constitutional crises, even if rarely.
5. *Public school level.* State government (in Victoria, the Victorian Department of Education).
6. *Why interpreting the Constitution can reshape the country.* New interpretations change what is legally allowed, sometimes recognising rights or invalidating laws.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"PM passed a law" headline.* Parliament passes laws, not the Prime Minister alone. The PM usually leads the government that proposes a bill, but it must be passed by both houses and given royal assent.
2. *Knox mock-parliament.* Speaker (rules the chamber), Minister (proposes a bill), Opposition spokesperson (challenges the bill). Each demonstrates a real role.
3. *Federal MP staffer.* The difference between federal and state matters; the role of the Constitution in setting Commonwealth powers; the limits of what an MP can do for a constituent (advocate, refer, but not override courts).
4. *PM ignoring a court.* Issues: rule of law and separation of powers. Such an act would breach the Constitution and undermine democratic checks; in practice, governments comply or seek to amend the law.
5. *Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung committee meeting.* Step 1: acknowledge Country and invite an opening from a representative. Step 2: agree on protocols for note-taking, recording and public reporting. Step 3: ensure the corporation reviews the committee's report before publication.
