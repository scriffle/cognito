## Core content

**1. What a political party is.** A **political party** is an organised group of people who share broad policy goals and run candidates for election. In Australia, the main parties are the Australian Labor Party, the Liberal Party, the Nationals, the Australian Greens, One Nation, the Liberal Democrats and others. Parties pre-select candidates, fund campaigns and write platforms. *Quick check:* Why is "they agree on everything" a poor description of a party?

**2. Why parties matter for voters.** Parties help voters by grouping policies under a recognisable label, so you can pick a bundle instead of investigating every candidate. They also organise debate inside parliament. *Quick check:* What is one advantage and one cost of bundled policies?

**3. Independents.** An **independent representative** runs without belonging to a party. They make their own decisions on every bill. Recent examples include the "teal" independents elected in 2022 in seats like Kooyong (Monique Ryan) and Goldstein (Zoe Daniel). *Quick check:* What is one strength and one weakness of being independent?

**4. Worked example: forming a federal government.** After a federal election, the House of Representatives has 151 seats. A **majority government** is formed when one party (or a permanent **coalition** like the Liberal-Nationals) wins more than 75 seats. If no group reaches 76, a **minority government** is formed with support from independents and minor parties on a confidence-and-supply basis. The Governor-General invites the leader who commands the confidence of the House to be Prime Minister. *Follow the thinking:* count seats → check for majority → negotiate support → invite the PM.

**5. Balance of power.** **Balance of power** is held by the people whose support a government needs to pass laws. In the Senate, no government has held an outright majority in recent decades, so cross-benchers (Greens, independents) often hold the balance. *Quick check:* Why does balance of power matter even when there is a majority in the House of Representatives?

**6. Coalitions and minority governments.** A **coalition** is a formal alliance, like the Liberals and Nationals. A **minority government** is a single party leading with informal support. Both have governed Australia. The 2010 Gillard government was a minority Labor government supported by independents and the Greens. *Quick check:* Why might a minority government be more responsive to small parties?

**7. What parties do between elections.** Parties keep policy machines running, debate ideas at conferences, hold parliamentary discipline, recruit members and prepare for the next campaign. They are public organisations, not just election vehicles. *Quick check:* Why is "party discipline" both useful and risky?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian seats often shift between parties, including in 2022 when several inner-city seats elected independents or Greens. The Victorian Electoral Commission and Australian Electoral Commission publish detailed results.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander members have served in the Australian parliament since 1971 (Senator Neville Bonner). Linda Burney was the first Indigenous woman in the House of Representatives and served as Minister for Indigenous Australians. In Victoria, the First Peoples' Assembly is a representative body that runs its own elections within the Aboriginal community for treaty negotiations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Diverse parliamentary representation has grown, including members from Vietnamese, Indian, Sudanese, Greek and Lebanese backgrounds. Multilingual electoral materials and interpreter services aim to support all citizens to vote.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Parties never change their policies." No. Platforms evolve with debate and changing circumstances.
- "Independents have no power." No. They can hold the balance of power.
- "The Prime Minister is directly elected." No. Voters elect their local MP; the PM is chosen by the party with majority support.
- "All democracies use the same system." No. Australia uses preferential voting in the House and proportional voting in the Senate.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "they agree on everything" is poor.* Within a party, members debate and sometimes split. Discipline applies on key votes but individual views differ.
2. *Bundled policies cost.* Advantage: easier for voters to make a single choice. Cost: you cannot pick and mix individual policies you disagree with.
3. *Strength/weakness of independents.* Strength: vote on the merits of each bill. Weakness: less collective bargaining power and limited resources.
4. *Why balance of power still matters with majority in House of Reps.* Bills also need to pass the Senate, where the government rarely has a majority.
5. *Why minority governments are responsive to small parties.* Because the government needs their votes to pass legislation, small parties can negotiate concessions.
6. *Why party discipline is useful and risky.* Useful: it lets parties deliver on platforms. Risky: it can suppress dissent and stop MPs voting on conscience.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Parties are all the same".* Show real differences, e.g., climate policy targets, housing measures, tax positions. Reference current platforms or recent legislation.
2. *Mock election.* Provide equal time to all candidates, run preferential voting properly, and brief voters on policies in advance.
3. *Junior staffer.* A party MP usually follows the party line on most votes. An independent decides each vote based on local consultation, conscience or policy detail. Both engage with constituents but with different obligations.
4. *Compulsory voting.* For: high turnout legitimises results and prevents disenfranchisement; against: voting under duress may produce uninformed or coerced choices.
5. *Preferential voting in Vietnamese.* Step 1: number every box from 1 to N in order of preference. Step 2: your vote moves to your next preference if your top choice is eliminated. Step 3: the candidate with majority support after preferences wins.
