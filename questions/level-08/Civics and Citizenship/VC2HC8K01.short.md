# VC2HC8K01 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** The role of political parties and independent representatives in Australian democracy, including elections, the formation of governments and the balance of power.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Parties bundle policies and candidates. Independents vote case by case. 76 seats forms majority government.

## What you are learning
- You are learning about the **role** of **political parties** and **independent representatives** in **Australian democracy**.
- You should be able to explain how votes turn into a government.

## Core content

A **political party** is an organised group running candidates under a shared policy banner. An **independent representative** runs without a party. The House of Representatives has 151 seats; **majority government** needs 76. A **coalition** is a formal alliance (Liberal-Nationals). A **minority government** governs with support from independents or minor parties.

**Balance of power** is held by whoever's vote a government needs to pass laws. In the Senate, no government has held outright majority recently, so cross-benchers (Greens, independents) often hold the balance. Parties also run between elections — policy debates, candidate recruitment, parliamentary discipline.

**Worked example.** Forming a federal government. After an election, count seats in the House. If one party (or permanent coalition) wins more than 75 seats, it forms majority government. If not, the leader who can command the confidence of the House — usually with independent or minor-party support — is invited by the Governor-General to be Prime Minister. The 2010 Gillard government was a minority Labor government supported by independents and the Greens.

**Cultural context.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander members have served in the Australian parliament since 1971 (Senator Neville Bonner). Linda Burney was the first Indigenous woman in the House. In Victoria, the First Peoples' Assembly elects representatives for treaty negotiations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Parties bundle policies and candidates.
- Independents vote case by case.
- 76 seats forms majority government.
- Balance of power is leverage.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Parties never change their policies." No. Platforms evolve with debate.
- "Independents have no power." No. They can hold the balance of power.
- "The PM is directly elected." No. Voters elect their local MP; the PM is chosen by the party with majority support.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why is "they all agree" a poor description of a party?* Members debate; discipline applies on key votes, but individual views differ.
- *Why does balance of power matter with a House majority?* Bills also need the Senate, where the government rarely has a majority.
- *Strength of independents.* They vote on each bill's merits, not party line.
- *Why might minority governments be responsive to small parties?* They need their votes to pass legislation.
- *Short summary of preferential voting.* Number every box in order of preference. Your vote transfers to your next preference if your top choice is eliminated. The candidate with majority after preferences wins.
