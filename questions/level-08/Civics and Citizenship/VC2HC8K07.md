## Core content

**1. What a law is.** A **law** is a general rule that applies to everyone, can be enforced, and was made by a recognised process. Laws apply forward in time (prospective), are publicly known, and apply consistently to similar cases. *Quick check:* Why is "secret law" a contradiction in democracies?

**2. Statutory law (from parliament).** **Statutory law** is law made by parliament. A **bill** is introduced, debated in both houses, voted on, and given **royal assent** by the Governor-General (federal) or Governor (state). Once passed, an **Act** is part of the body of legislation. *Quick check:* Why are debates and votes in both houses important?

**3. Common law (from courts).** **Common law** is law made by judges through their decisions on cases. Judges apply **precedent** — past decisions on similar facts — to reach new ones. Higher-court decisions bind lower courts. Over centuries, principles like the duty of care in negligence developed this way. *Quick check:* What is the role of precedent in keeping law predictable?

**4. Worked example: Donoghue v Stevenson and Australian negligence.** In 1932, a UK case (Donoghue v Stevenson) established a duty of care in negligence: a manufacturer can owe a duty to a consumer they cannot see. Australian courts adopted and developed this in cases like Grant v Australian Knitting Mills (1936). Today this common-law principle is supplemented by statutes like the Civil Liability Act 2002 (Vic), which set rules for negligence claims. *Follow the thinking:* original case → adoption in Australian courts → statute that builds on it → current rule.

**5. How statute and common law interact.** Parliament can override common law by passing a statute. Courts interpret statutes when their words are unclear. Together they form a layered body of law. *Quick check:* Why does parliament sometimes pass a statute that codifies an existing common-law rule?

**6. Characteristics of good laws.** Laws should be clear (people can understand them), workable (they can be applied), and just (they treat similar people similarly). They should also be open to challenge if they fail these tests. *Quick check:* What is one consequence of a law that is too vague?

**7. Federal vs state lawmaking.** The Commonwealth makes laws on matters listed in s 51 of the Constitution (defence, immigration, taxation). States make laws on most other matters (schools, hospitals, traffic, criminal law). Sometimes both legislate on the same area; if there is a clash, federal law usually prevails under s 109. *Quick check:* Which level makes the road rules?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's Parliament publishes bills and Acts at legislation.vic.gov.au. Hansard records debate. The Supreme Court of Victoria publishes its judgments online. Both are essential sources for civics study.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal customary law existed and continues to exist in Aboriginal communities. Australian statutory law has grown to recognise some aspects (e.g., native title since Mabo v Queensland (No. 2) 1992). In Victoria, treaty negotiations are exploring how to recognise customary law and decision-making. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Australian law applies to all residents regardless of background. Interpreters, multilingual legal-information services, and community legal centres in Melbourne's south-east and west help people access statutes and court processes.

## Watch out for these traps
- "All law comes from parliament." No. Common-law principles develop through court decisions.
- "Judges should not make law." Common law develops through judges applying precedent. Parliament can override if it disagrees.
- "Statutes always win." Usually, but courts interpret them, sometimes narrowly.
- "Aboriginal customary law has no place in Australian law." Limited recognition exists, especially since Mabo.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why secret law contradicts democracy.* People cannot follow a rule they cannot see, and no one can hold officials accountable to unknown rules.
2. *Why two-house votes matter.* They prevent rushed laws and give a second chamber the chance to amend or reject.
3. *Role of precedent.* Predictability and equal treatment. Similar facts get similar outcomes, so people can plan and lawyers can advise.
4. *Why parliament codifies common law.* To make the rule clearer, to add detail, or to change parts of it. Codification helps citizens find the rule in one place.
5. *Consequence of vague law.* Inconsistent application and unfair surprises. Vague laws are more likely to be successfully challenged.
6. *Who makes road rules.* Mostly states. Victoria's road rules are state legislation, although some related matters (e.g., heavy-vehicle national rules) involve federal coordination.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *News story on a new Act.* Statutory law. Bills usually start in the House of Representatives (federal) or the Legislative Assembly (state). Before becoming law, the bill must pass both houses and receive assent.
2. *Manningham noise bylaw.* It is statutory law: bylaws are made under power delegated by state legislation.
3. *Junior law clerk tracing precedent.* Look for the highest binding court's decision in the same jurisdiction, the facts most similar to the case, and any later cases that have refined the principle.
4. *Judges making law debate.* For: courts apply general principles to new facts that parliaments cannot anticipate. Against: judges are unelected; major changes should come from parliament.
5. *Native-title claim.* The Mabo decision recognised native title as part of Australian common law. The Native Title Act 1993 codifies how claims are made and resolved. Courts apply both together.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Area: negligence.* Common law: Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) and Grant v Australian Knitting Mills (1936) established duty of care. Statute: Civil Liability Act 2002 (Vic) sets specific rules for negligence claims. The two work together: courts apply common-law principles within statutory limits.
