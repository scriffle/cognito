# VC2HC8S02 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC8S02. Analyse contemporary issues by locating, selecting, explaining and comparing information, data and ideas from a range of sources.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship (Investigating contemporary civics and citizenship issues).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Good analysis uses multiple sources.
  - Selection is judgement, not neutrality.
  - Comparing sources reveals what is contested.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse contemporary issues** by **locating, selecting, explaining and comparing** information, data and ideas from a **range of sources**.
- You are learning to do real research that goes beyond one search result.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **primary source**, **secondary source** and **bias** in your own words.
- Locate three different types of sources on the same issue.
- Compare two sources and explain where they agree and disagree.
- Justify which source carries more weight for a specific question.

## Core content

**1. Locating sources.** Civics sources include parliamentary Hansard, court judgments, government data (ABS, AEC), public-broadcaster reports (ABC, SBS), independent media (The Conversation), academic articles, and community-organisation publications. Different starting points reveal different angles. *Quick check:* Why is a parliamentary Hansard transcript a primary source?

**2. Primary and secondary sources.** A **primary source** is original material from the time or event: speeches, bills, court decisions, statistics, witness accounts. A **secondary source** interprets or summarises: news articles, textbook chapters, analyses. Both are valuable. *Quick check:* Is a video news report on a parliamentary vote a primary or secondary source? Why?

**3. Selecting sources.** Selection is unavoidable; not all sources are equal for your question. Check the publisher, the date, whether there are clear corrections, and what the source claims it is doing (reporting, opinion, advocacy). *Quick check:* What is one risk of relying on a single source?

**4. Explaining sources.** Explaining means summarising the source accurately, naming its position, and showing how it fits your question. Quote sparingly; paraphrase carefully; cite always. *Quick check:* Why is paraphrasing without citing a problem?

**5. Comparing sources.** Comparing reveals where sources agree (likely facts), where they disagree (contested interpretation), and where they have gaps. Disagreements are often clues, not problems. *Quick check:* What does it usually mean when two reputable sources interpret the same data differently?

**6. Worked example: investigating youth voting reform.** Question: should the federal voting age be lowered to 16 in Australia? Locate: the Parliamentary Library brief; an ABC News article; a Youth Affairs Council of Victoria report; an opinion piece in The Conversation; an academic study. Compare: identify shared facts (current voting age, examples from other countries) and contested claims (effects on turnout and democratic engagement). Explain how each source frames the issue. *Follow the thinking:* locate → select → explain → compare → justify.

**7. Recognising bias.** **Bias** is a tendency to favour one side. It can come from funding, audience, ideology or framing. All sources have perspectives. Use a mix to triangulate. *Quick check:* Why is "balanced" not the same as "free of bias"?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian Parliament's Hansard is free online. The Auditor-General of Victoria publishes detailed reports on government programs. The Victorian Multicultural Commission publishes community data and reports.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS publishes guidelines on respectful research with First Nations communities. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria publishes its own communications. Avoid speaking for First Nations communities; cite their own sources directly. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual community media (SBS Radio, ethnic newspapers in Vietnamese, Mandarin, Arabic and other languages) provides voices missed by mainstream sources.

3. **Vocational.** A junior policy researcher gathers sources on rental affordability. Outline three primary and three secondary sources she should consider.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to use anonymous social-media posts as evidence. Identify two ethical considerations.

## Memorable takeaways
- One source is not research.
- Primary records, secondary interprets.
- Selection is judgement.
- Compare to reveal contest.
- Triangulate to manage bias.

## Stretch yourself

## Watch out for these traps
- "Wikipedia is unreliable." Often a useful starting point; check its references and update history before relying on it.
- "Government sources are unbiased." Less likely to fabricate, but still shape framing and choice of statistics.
- "Two opposing views means the truth is in the middle." Not always; sometimes evidence favours one side.
- "If a source is recent, it is best." Recency helps for fast-moving issues; for some questions, older primary sources are essential.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Hansard as primary.* It is the verbatim record of what was said in parliament at the time, made by the institution itself.
2. *News report on a parliamentary vote.* Secondary. A journalist summarises and interprets the event. The Hansard or the bill text is the primary source.
3. *Risk of one source.* You will miss other perspectives, errors and gaps that a single source cannot show.
4. *Paraphrasing without citing.* Misrepresents the work as your own; that is plagiarism, even if unintentional.
5. *Why reputable sources differ.* They may emphasise different parts of the data, use different definitions, or interpret causes differently.
6. *"Balanced" vs "bias-free".* Balanced means presenting multiple sides. Bias-free is rare; framing always shapes how a story is told.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Two headlines, one court case.* Read the judgment summary or full text; cross-check with the court's website; read at least one second news source.
2. *Banyule planning research.* Council planning documents (primary); local-newspaper coverage; academic urban-studies articles.
3. *Rental-affordability sources.* Primary: ABS rent data, lease registers, council planning approvals. Secondary: news articles, academic studies, advocacy reports.
4. *Anonymous social-media evidence.* Issues: verification and consent. Use only as illustrations, not as proof; consider whether the platform's policies allow research use.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian turnout.* AEC turnout data by polling place; SBS Vietnamese coverage; Federation of Australian Vietnamese Communities reports.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: Victorian public transport ticketing reform.* Sources: PTV announcements (primary), Auditor-General report (secondary), The Age coverage, an academic transport study, a passenger-advocacy group submission. Comparison: PTV vs Auditor-General on cost savings of the reform. Verdict: the Auditor-General carries more weight on cost-effectiveness for the specific question. Sources cited.
