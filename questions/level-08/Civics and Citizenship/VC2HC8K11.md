# VC2HC8K11 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC8K11. How groups express their identities, including religious and cultural identity, and how this expression can influence their perceptions of others and others' perception of them.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship (Citizenship, diversity and identity).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Identity has many layers, often expressed in public.
  - Expression shapes how groups see themselves and others.
  - Perception can build connection or breed misunderstanding.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Intercultural Capability; Ethical Capability; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning **how groups express their identities**, including **religious and cultural identity**, and how this expression can **influence perceptions** of others and how others see them.
- You are learning to read identity as a two-way street.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **identity**, **expression** and **stereotype** in your own words.
- Describe three ways groups express identity in Victoria.
- Explain how expression can affect perception positively and negatively.
- Identify one way to challenge an unhelpful stereotype.

## Core content

**1. Identity has many layers.** A person can hold national, cultural, religious, gender, family, occupational and personal identities. Groups share some layers. Both individuals and groups choose which layers to make visible. *Quick check:* Why do most people have multiple identities at once?

**2. Forms of expression.** Identity is expressed through clothing (uniforms, religious attire, traditional dress), food, language, festivals, art, music, place-names, public ceremonies and shared spaces. Expression can be everyday (a hijab at school) or special (Lunar New Year in Footscray). *Quick check:* What is one identity expression you see daily?

**3. Expression shapes self-perception.** Wearing a yarmulke, speaking Greek at home, performing on stage at the Antipodes Festival or attending Friday prayers can strengthen a sense of belonging and continuity. Expression is not just decorative; it builds and maintains identity. *Quick check:* Why do exiled or migrant communities often keep traditions strongly?

**4. Worked example: AFL grand final and Welcome to Country.** At the AFL grand final, multiple expressions sit alongside each other: the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Welcome to Country, the Australian national anthem (now with the line "we are one and free"), team songs, fan colours, multicultural performers. Each expression signals different identities. Together they communicate a national event that includes many groups. *Follow the thinking:* identify the expressions → identify the groups → notice the order and place of each.

**5. Expression shapes how others perceive a group.** Visible identity expression invites perception, accurate or not. Friendly perception leads to connection, curiosity and dialogue. Negative perception can lead to **stereotypes** (oversimplified beliefs about a group) or discrimination. *Quick check:* What is the difference between a stereotype and a description?

**6. Perception is reciprocal.** Groups also form views of others. Newer arrivals interpret the cultures around them, sometimes accurately, sometimes not. Schools, workplaces and media all shape perceptions over time. *Quick check:* Why are first impressions of a group often unreliable?

**7. Challenging unhelpful stereotypes.** Personal contact, accurate information, plural representation in media, and visible everyday respect all reduce the power of stereotypes. Anti-racism education and intercultural programs aim at the same thing. *Quick check:* What is one Australian initiative that aims to build accurate perception across groups?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Public festivals like the Antipodes Festival (Greek), Diwali at Federation Square, Vietnamese Tết in Footscray and Lunar New Year throughout Melbourne demonstrate identity expression in shared public space. SBS Victoria broadcasts in over 60 languages.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Wadawurrung, Boon Wurrung and other Victorian peoples express identity through Welcomes to Country, dual-named places, art (e.g., at NGV and on public buildings), language revitalisation programs and treaty processes. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Identity expression in Melbourne includes religious dress, multilingual signage and broadcasting, community-language schools and faith-based education systems. Friday prayer at the Newport Mosque, Greek Easter at parishes across Melbourne, and Sikh Vaisakhi parades are visible examples.

## Memorable takeaways
- Identity is layered.
- Expression builds belonging.
- Perception is shaped, not fixed.
- Stereotypes simplify; description specifies.
- Contact and accurate information help.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Identity is private; expression is intrusive." Public expression of identity is normal and protected.
- "Stereotypes are just first guesses." Repeated stereotypes shape institutions and behaviour.
- "Only minorities have identities." Everyone has identities, including majority groups.
- "Welcome to Country is just ceremony." It is a culturally significant practice with meaning beyond formality.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why multiple identities at once.* People belong to many groups (family, school, faith, nationality, profession). Each adds a layer to who they are.
2. *Daily identity expression.* School uniform, accents, religious symbols, language at home, sporting-team colours.
3. *Why migrant communities keep traditions strongly.* Traditions provide continuity, comfort and a way to pass identity to the next generation in a new place.
4. *Stereotype vs description.* A description fits a specific person or event. A stereotype generalises a group, often inaccurately or unfairly.
5. *Why first impressions can mislead.* They are based on limited information, often filtered through prior expectations.
6. *Australian initiative for accurate perception.* Reconciliation Australia, the Mapping Social Cohesion survey, and AHRC anti-racism campaigns.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Classmate wearing religious dress.* Ask their name; show interest in the meaning if they want to share; do not stare or treat them as representative of an entire religion.
2. *Brimbank festivals.* Consult community organisations; ensure accessibility for all ages and disabilities; programme across multiple cultures, not single-event tokenism.
3. *Journalist reporting on a festival.* Interview community organisers, use accurate names and terms, check sources and pronunciations; consider the framing implications of imagery.
4. *Religious dress in schools.* For: freedom of religion and expression. Against: uniform consistency. Both sides can be satisfied with reasonable accommodation policies.
5. *Wurundjeri Welcome.* Listen attentively; brief the school community in advance about what a Welcome means; follow up afterwards with thanks and acknowledgement in school communications.

Accurate perception: industrious community contributing to local economy. Stereotype: assumption of "newness" despite multi-generational residence. Initiative: bilingual cultural-orientation programs at the Footscray Community Arts Centre. Sources cited.
