# VC2HC8K06 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** How citizens can participate in and influence lawmaking, including through contact with their elected representatives, use of lobby groups, interest groups, direct action and submissions to parliamentary committees.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Many stages, many openings. Submissions are underused. Coalitions outpunch individuals.

## What you are learning
- You are learning **how citizens can participate in and influence lawmaking**.
- You should be able to map the channels for influencing a bill.

## Core content

A bill passes through drafting, first reading, committee review, debate, votes in both houses, then royal assent. Citizens can influence at almost every stage. Channels: writing to MPs (especially as a constituent), joining a **lobby group** or **interest group**, peaceful **direct action**, and **submissions to parliamentary committees**.

A **parliamentary committee** is a small group of MPs that examines bills and issues. Committees usually call for **submissions** from the public. Submissions are read, summarised, and influence committee recommendations.

**Worked example.** A federal online-safety bill is referred to a Senate committee. The committee opens a public submissions period. You research a specific section, write a 300-word response, and recommend one specific change. The committee report cites your submission. The government may amend the bill before it returns to the chamber.

**Cultural context.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria represents Aboriginal Victorians in treaty negotiations and shapes state-level lawmaking. Multicultural and faith-based organisations file submissions, often translated into multiple languages, coordinated by bodies like the Ethnic Communities' Council of Victoria. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Many stages, many openings.
- Constituent voice carries weight.
- Submissions are underused.
- Coalitions outpunch individuals.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Only experts can write submissions." No. Committees explicitly welcome individual citizens.
- "If a bill passes, you have lost." No. Implementation and follow-up reviews are also influenceable.
- "Lobbying is illegal." No. It is legal and regulated; transparency rules apply.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why does participation between elections matter?* Most policy is shaped after the vote, in committees, regulations and amendments.
- *Why does constituent identity matter?* Representatives are accountable to people in their electorate.
- *What do lobbyists bring that letters cannot?* Sustained relationships, expertise and legislative tracking.
- *Why are submissions stronger than generic letters?* Committees call for them, read them and reference them in reports.
- *Short summary of influencing an online-safety bill.* Research the bill, find the committee, submit a focused response with one recommendation, engage your MP, and join an interest group's coordinated submission.
