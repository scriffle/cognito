# VC2HC8K08 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** The types of law in Australia, including criminal law and civil law, and the place of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander customary law.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Criminal: state vs accused. Civil: parties dispute. Customary law is its own continuing system.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **types of law** in Australia, including **criminal** and **civil law**, and the **place of customary law**.
- You should be able to distinguish kinds of law.

## Core content

**Criminal law** covers offences against the state. The state prosecutes; penalties can include fines, community work or prison. Standard of proof: **beyond reasonable doubt**. **Civil law** covers disputes between people or organisations. A **plaintiff** sues a **defendant** for a remedy, usually damages. Standard: **on the balance of probabilities**.

**Customary law** is a continuing system in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, with its own authorities and processes covering conduct, ceremony, relationships and Country. Australian law recognises some aspects (native title since Mabo, 1992; the Native Title Act 1993; specialist courts like the Koori Court in Victoria). Recognition is partial; Victorian treaty negotiations may extend it.

**Worked example.** A car accident in suburban Melbourne. Criminal: police charge the driver with dangerous driving. The state prosecutes; standard is beyond reasonable doubt; penalty could be fine, licence loss or jail. Civil: the injured passenger sues for damages. Standard is balance of probabilities; outcome is a monetary award. Both can run from the same incident.

**Cultural context.** The Victorian Aboriginal Legal Service represents Aboriginal Victorians in courts. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria is engaging in treaty negotiations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Criminal: state vs accused, very high proof.
- Civil: parties dispute, ordinary proof.
- Same event, two cases possible.
- Customary law is its own continuing system.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Civil law is less serious." It can have huge financial and life impact.
- "Customary law is folklore." No. It is a continuing system.
- "Aboriginal customary law is fully recognised." No. Recognition is partial and evolving.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why does the state prosecute, not the victim?* Crimes are treated as harm to public order; the state ensures consistent prosecution on society's behalf.
- *Why is the civil standard lower?* The consequence is usually monetary, not loss of liberty.
- *"Balance of probabilities" in plain English.* More likely than not — roughly above 50% confidence.
- *Why does saying "the law" alone mislead?* Australia has multiple sources and systems: statutory, common, customary.
- *Short summary of a workplace injury case.* Criminal prosecution by WorkSafe against the employer for safety breach; civil claim by the worker for damages from negligence. Both can run together with different standards and remedies.
