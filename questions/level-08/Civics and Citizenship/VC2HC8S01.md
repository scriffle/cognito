# VC2HC8S01 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC8S01. Develop and refine questions to investigate contemporary political, legal and civic issues.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship (Investigating contemporary civics and citizenship issues).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Good questions narrow a topic into something investigable.
  - Refining questions sharpens the focus.
  - Civic questions name people, places and decisions.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop and refine questions** to investigate **contemporary political, legal and civic issues**.
- You are learning to turn a topic into a specific question someone could investigate.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **investigable question**, **scope** and **stakeholder** in your own words.
- Convert a broad topic into three more specific questions.
- Refine a question by checking scope, time and place.
- Identify when a question needs further refinement before research.

## Core content

**1. Topics vs questions.** A topic is general (e.g., "voting"). A question is specific and answerable (e.g., "Why does Australia have compulsory voting, and how does it compare with neighbouring countries?"). Investigations begin with strong questions. *Quick check:* Why is "I want to study housing" not enough for civics research?

**2. What makes a question investigable.** A good civics question is specific about place, time and people; uses available sources; allows multiple perspectives; and has a clear scope. *Quick check:* Which of these is more investigable: "Are political parties fair?" or "How fairly is the major-party preselection process in Victoria reported by mainstream and independent media in 2024–2025?"

**3. The narrowing funnel.** Start with a topic. Add place (Victoria, Australia, Asia-Pacific). Add time (this year, last decade). Add actor (council, parliament, court). Add measure (compared with what?). Result: a specific question. *Quick check:* What does adding "this year" do for a research question?

**4. Worked example: investigating housing affordability.** Broad topic: housing affordability. Step 1: add place (Melbourne's outer suburbs). Step 2: add time (since 2020). Step 3: add a civic angle (state and local government policy). Step 4: add a measure (rental costs, vacancy rates, planning approvals). Refined question: "How have state and local government planning decisions affected rental affordability in three Melbourne outer suburbs since 2020?" *Follow the thinking:* topic → narrow place → narrow time → name actors → set measures.

**5. Refining for fairness.** Strong civics questions allow multiple perspectives. Replace loaded language ("Why is the government corrupt?") with neutral framing ("What does recent evidence show about transparency in government procurement in Victoria?"). *Quick check:* Why does loaded language weaken a question?

**6. Stakeholders.** A **stakeholder** is anyone affected by an issue. Naming stakeholders shapes who you ask, what sources you use, and how you interpret results. *Quick check:* Who would you name as stakeholders in a question about public-housing policy?

**7. When to refine again.** If your question cannot be answered with sources you can access, or could be answered with a one-line web search, refine again. Questions that lead to research, not a quick lookup, are more useful. *Quick check:* What is one sign that a question is too vague?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Parliament's research papers, the Productivity Commission inquiries and the Auditor-General's reports are good sources for sharper civic questions. Local newspapers and community organisations bring local detail.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Civic questions affecting First Nations communities should follow AIATSIS guidelines on respectful research, with consent and community involvement where the question concerns Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, Country or knowledge. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Civic questions about migrant communities benefit from community sources (multilingual media, local councils' diversity strategies) and from researchers who speak community languages.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to know whether your suburb is well represented in council decisions. Refine this topic into three investigable questions.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Hume wants community questions for a youth-engagement project. Suggest three civic questions that students could pursue.
3. **Vocational.** A junior policy researcher at a Victorian think-tank starts a project on housing. Outline three steps to scope a good question.
4. **Ethical.** A class plans to research a sensitive issue about a faith community. Identify two ethical considerations and how to handle them.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Your class works with a Wadawurrung-led project on land care. Outline three steps to develop questions respectfully.

## Memorable takeaways
- Topic ≠ question.
- Place, time, actor, measure.
- Neutral wording.
- Name stakeholders.
- Refine until it leads to real research.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "Topic: ___. Place: ___. Time: ___. Stakeholder: ___. Question: ___?"
- **Words to keep close.** investigable question, scope, stakeholder, neutral framing, primary source, secondary source.
- **Try this picture.** Build a "question funnel" diagram. At the top: a broad topic. At each narrowing layer: place, time, actor, measure. At the bottom: a refined investigable question.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Take a topic you care about and write three versions of a question — broad, medium, and refined — and explain how each version differs.

## Stretch yourself
Pick a Victorian or Australian civic issue. Write five versions of a question on it, from broadest to most refined, naming what you added at each step. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More words means better question." Not always. Clarity matters more than length.
- "A neutral question has no view." Neutral framing avoids loaded language but still asks a real question.
- "Search engines answer questions instantly." Investigable questions usually need more than one source.
- "Indigenous-related questions are off-limits." They are valid, but need respectful framing, consent and community engagement.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "I want to study housing" is not enough.* It does not name the place, time, people or what you want to find out. Research could go in many incompatible directions.
2. *More investigable.* The second question, which names media types, year range and a specific process.
3. *Adding "this year".* Limits the scope to current events, makes data more available and findings more relevant to recent decisions.
4. *Why loaded language weakens questions.* It assumes a conclusion before the evidence is examined, biasing how you read sources.
5. *Public-housing stakeholders.* Current tenants, applicants on the waiting list, neighbouring residents, local council, state government, advocacy groups.
6. *Sign of vagueness.* You cannot decide which sources to look at, or any source could answer it.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Suburb representation in council.* Q1: How many community submissions has the council received from your suburb in the past two years? Q2: What proportion of council motions reference issues in your suburb? Q3: How does turnout at council elections in your suburb compare with the council average?
2. *Hume youth-engagement.* How could the council better engage Year 8 students in policy decisions? Which transport changes would improve school commutes in Hume? How does the council communicate with multilingual youth?
3. *Think-tank scoping.* Define the place; define the time period; identify the existing data sources before writing the question.
4. *Sensitive faith research.* Issues: respect and consent. Approach: consult community leaders, define the purpose clearly, and ensure participants can decline or remain anonymous.
5. *Wadawurrung-led project.* Step 1: contact the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: invite their leadership in shaping the question. Step 3: ensure community ownership of outputs.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: youth political engagement.* Q1 (broadest): Are young people interested in politics? Q2: How interested are Victorian young people in politics? Q3: How has Victorian Year 9 political interest changed since 2015? Q4: How has Victorian Year 9 political interest changed since 2015, measured by survey data and social media engagement? Q5 (most refined): How has political interest among Year 9 students in Melbourne's western suburbs changed since 2015, comparing survey data with social-media engagement during election years? Each step adds specificity.
