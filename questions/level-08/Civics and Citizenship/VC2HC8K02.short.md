# VC2HC8K02 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** The extent to which Australia's institutions and systems reflect democratic values, such as freedom of speech, association, assembly, religion and movement.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Values guide institutions. Reflection is partial. Tensions between values are normal.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to judge the **extent** to which institutions **reflect democratic values**.
- You should be able to assess how well institutions live up to their stated values.

## Core content

A **democratic value** is a principle most democracies say they support. Key freedoms include speech, assembly, association, religion and movement. Some are explicit in the Constitution (freedom of religion, s 116). Others sit in legislation, common law (the implied freedom of political communication), or international treaties Australia has signed.

Australia signed the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966) but has no federal Bill of Rights. Victoria has a Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006). The phrase "extent to which" asks you to assess, not just describe — institutions reflect democratic values **in part**.

**Worked example.** A climate protest in Spring Street. Freedoms involved: speech (chants, signs), assembly (gathering), association (organising group), movement (people travelling in). Limits: police can require permits and reroute crowds for safety. Courts can review whether limits go too far.

**Cultural context.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples were not counted in the census until the 1967 referendum changed the Constitution. The Mabo decision (1992) recognised native title. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria leads treaty negotiations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Values guide institutions.
- Partial reflection is the norm.
- Values clash; courts and parliament balance.
- Australia lacks a federal Bill of Rights.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Freedom of speech means no consequences." No. Defamation, threats and incitement laws still apply.
- "All Australians have always had these freedoms." No. Many were extended only over recent decades.
- "Limits on freedoms are always bad." No. Some limits protect other people's freedoms.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Value vs law.* A value is a principle. A law is an enforceable rule.
- *Why does "where written down" matter?* Constitutional protections are harder to change than legislation; common-law freedoms can shift with new court decisions.
- *Climate strike — which freedoms?* Speech, assembly, association, sometimes movement.
- *Why "extent to which" not "whether"?* It invites a judgement of degree, allowing partial answers and trade-offs.
- *Short summary of how clashes are resolved.* Parliament writes the rules; courts apply them to specific cases; public debate informs both.
