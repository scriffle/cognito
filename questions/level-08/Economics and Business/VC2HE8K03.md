## Core content

**1. Purposes and goals.** Businesses exist for many purposes: making profit, providing services, building community, achieving social goals, sustaining culture. Some pursue several at once. *Quick check:* What is one business near you whose main purpose is not profit?

**2. Ownership structures.** A **sole trader** is one person who owns and runs a business (e.g., a Melbourne photographer). A **partnership** is two or more owners (e.g., a Brunswick law firm). A **company** is a separate legal entity with shareholders; small (Pty Ltd) or large (Ltd) (e.g., a tech start-up). A **not-for-profit** reinvests surplus into its mission (e.g., a community-health service). *Quick check:* Which structure shields owners' personal assets from business debts?

**3. Types of products.** Businesses sell **goods** (physical products) or **services** (work done for others). Many sell both. *Quick check:* Is a Melbourne café selling coffee and an experience selling a good or a service?

**4. Target market.** A **target market** is the group of customers a business focuses on, defined by age, location, income, interests or other features. Sharper targeting usually improves marketing and product design. *Quick check:* What is the target market for a Vietnamese-language tutoring service in Springvale?

**5. Worked example: a youth-driven sneaker resale business.** A 17-year-old in Box Hill notices peers want rare sneakers but can't find them locally. Purpose: build income and learn business skills. Structure: sole trader. Product: physical goods (sneakers) and service (sourcing). Target market: 14–18 year olds in suburban Melbourne. Opportunity: local supply gap. Trade-offs: time vs school; competition from online giants. *Follow the thinking:* spot opportunity → choose structure → define product and market → assess trade-offs.

**6. Finding market opportunities.** Common methods: identify unmet needs in your community, watch what others do well, ask customers what they wish existed, follow demographic and technology trends. Test cheaply before scaling. *Quick check:* What is one unmet need in your suburb that a small business could address?

**7. Limits and ethics.** Not every gap should be filled. Health, safety, environmental and cultural considerations apply. Ethics is part of business decisions, not separate from them. *Quick check:* What is one ethical consideration for a small business selling food?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Small Business Commission supports new businesses. State and local government grants fund start-up ideas. Markets at Queen Victoria Market, Footscray Market and South Melbourne Market show diverse business structures in action.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Supply Nation lists Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander-owned businesses across Australia. Examples include Clothing the Gaps, Welcome to Country and many in Victoria. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant entrepreneurs run a large share of small businesses in Victoria across hospitality, retail and services. The Ethnic Communities' Council and Multicultural Business Ministerial Council support these enterprises.

## Memorable takeaways
- Purposes vary; structures match purposes.
- Sole trader carries personal risk; companies separate it.
- Goods and services often combine.
- Target market sharpens design.
- Opportunity = unmet need + ability to deliver.

## Watch out for these traps
- "All businesses are about profit." Many serve other purposes alongside or instead of profit.
- "Sole trader is simplest, so it is best." It is also the riskiest for personal liability.
- "Bigger market is better." Sharper targeting often wins over breadth.
- "If others sell it, you can't compete." Niche differentiation often does compete.
