By the end of this you should be able to:

## Core content

**1. What a budget is.** A **budget** is a plan that shows expected **income** and **expenses** over a period (usually a month). Tracking actuals against the plan reveals patterns. *Quick check:* Why is a written budget more useful than a mental one?

**2. Income, expenses, savings.** Income comes in (wages, allowance, gifts). Expenses go out (food, transport, phone, subscriptions). Savings = income − expenses. A common rule of thumb is "pay yourself first": set aside savings before spending. *Quick check:* What is one expense that can sneak up on you?

**3. Goals: short and long term.** A **short-term goal** is within months (e.g., save $300 for a trip). A **long-term goal** is a year or more (e.g., save $5,000 for a vehicle). Specific, measurable, time-bound goals are easier to track. *Quick check:* Why does "I want to save more" usually fail as a goal?

**4. Worked example: a Year 8 student's monthly budget.** Income: $80 part-time work + $40 allowance = $120. Expenses: $20 transport, $30 phone, $20 food, $10 streaming, $10 misc = $90. Savings: $30/month. Two goals: short term $200 for a school trip (pace = 7 months); long term $1,000 for an electric scooter (pace = 33 months). Adjustments: reduce streaming, increase work, save windfalls. *Follow the thinking:* income → expenses → savings → goals → check progress.

**5. Emergency funds.** An **emergency fund** is money saved for unexpected costs. Even small balances ($100–$500 for students) cushion shocks. Adults are advised 3–6 months of expenses. *Quick check:* What is one event that an emergency fund could cover?

**6. Tools.** Spreadsheets, budgeting apps and bank features help track. Free Australian tools include ASIC Moneysmart's budget planner. Pick one and stick with it for at least three months before changing. *Quick check:* Why is "stick with one tool" better than constant switching?

**7. Real life upsets budgets.** Unplanned expenses, lost income, lifestyle changes, holidays, gifts. Build a small buffer; review the budget monthly; adjust without abandoning the plan. *Quick check:* What is one buffer item to include in a Year 8 budget?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ASIC Moneysmart (federal), Consumer Affairs Victoria and Financial Counsellors of Australia provide free Victorian-relevant resources. Schools across Victoria run financial-literacy workshops.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Indigenous Consumer Assistance Network (ICAN) provides culturally appropriate financial counselling. Many First Nations communities practise shared financial planning across extended family. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant families often plan across multiple currencies and remittance commitments. Multilingual financial-counselling services support diverse Melbourne communities.

## Stretch yourself
Build a personal financial plan: monthly budget, one short-term and one long-term goal, an emergency-fund target, and a review schedule. Identify two risks and your response. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I will start saving when I earn more." Saving habits build with current income.
- "Budgeting is restrictive." It is choosing where your money goes.
- "Apps do everything." Apps help; the choices are still yours.
- "Emergency funds are for adults." Even small balances help students.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Written vs mental budget.* Written exposes patterns and forces honesty; mental budgets drift.
2. *Sneaky expense.* Subscriptions, app purchases, school excursions, transport top-ups.
3. *Why "save more" fails.* Vague; no amount, no timeframe, no plan.
4. *Emergency-fund event.* Lost phone, school excursion, bike repair, urgent travel.
5. *Why stick with one tool.* Switching loses history and breaks habits; pick a basic one and use it consistently.
6. *Buffer item.* A small "unplanned" line (say $10–$20 a month) for surprises.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *One-month budget.* Identify your real income and recent spending. Cut one item (e.g., one streaming service) to save $10–$15/month.
2. *Frankston workshop.* Budgeting basics; smart-consumer tips; scam awareness.
3. *First-session financial counsellor.* Listen first; map current income and expenses; agree one specific goal for the next four weeks.
4. *Earlier financial education debate.* For: builds skills before bad habits form. Against: developmental readiness; need for parental partnership. Both sides agree learning by doing matters.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian remittance household.* Include remittance as a fixed expense; agree priorities with extended family; build a small emergency buffer.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Personal plan.* Monthly budget with $120 income and $90 expenses, $30 saved. Short-term goal: $200 for a school trip in 7 months. Long-term goal: $1,000 for a vehicle in 33 months. Emergency fund target: $200. Review on the last day of each month. Risks: unplanned costs (build $20 buffer); income drop (cut streaming first).
