By the end of this you should be able to:

## Core content

**1. Description vs explanation.** A **description** says what is happening; an **explanation** says why. Both use evidence. Strong analysis often combines both: describe the data, explain the cause. *Quick check:* What is the difference between "rents rose 8%" and "rents rose 8% because of...?"

**2. Subject-specific terms.** Use precise terms: scarcity, opportunity cost, supply, demand, equilibrium, profit, revenue, cost, taxation, inflation, CPI, GDP, GST, PAYG, ATO, sole trader, partnership, company, not-for-profit, target market, budget, savings, scams. Define on first use for non-expert audiences. *Quick check:* Why does precise vocabulary build trust?

**3. Audience and format.** Choose format and tone for the audience: a short video for peers, a written report for a teacher or assessor, a public talk for parents. Plain English for general audiences, technical for expert audiences. *Quick check:* What changes between writing for Year 8 peers and a council audience?

**4. Worked example: explaining inflation.** Audience 1: Year 6 students. Format: a 90-second animation with a real example (price of an everyday item now and a year ago). Audience 2: parents at a school evening. Format: a 200-word handout with a chart and one source. Audience 3: a teacher assessor. Format: a 600-word report with multiple cited sources and proper terminology. Same concept; three formats. *Follow the thinking:* identify audience → choose format → adapt language → cite sources.

**5. Citing sources.** Cite by author/organisation, year, where accessed (URL or publication name). Use a consistent style. Cite each significant claim. *Quick check:* Why does citation help your reader, not just the assessor?

**6. Visuals.** Charts, infographics and short videos communicate where words struggle. Use labels, units and a clear takeaway. Cite the data source on the chart itself. *Quick check:* What is one common chart error to avoid?

**7. Quality and integrity.** Avoid plagiarism; quote sparingly; paraphrase carefully; check spelling and units. Integrity beats persuasion. *Quick check:* Why is "long quotes prove the point" usually wrong?

## Memorable takeaways
- Description first; explanation second.
- Use precise terms; define for non-experts.
- Audience and format together.
- Cite by author, year, source.
- Integrity beats persuasion.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Big words sound smart." Plain language sounds clear.
- "Long quotes prove the point." Use sparingly; reasoning is yours.
- "If it looks polished, the source is fine." Polish is not provenance.
- "Citations slow me down." They build trust and let others check.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Description vs explanation.* Description: what is happening (rents rose 8%). Explanation: why (population growth and slow construction).
2. *Why precise vocabulary builds trust.* It signals careful thinking and lets the reader follow your argument exactly.
3. *Year 8 vs council audience.* Council needs more numbers, sources and policy framing; Year 8s need accessibility, examples and clear takeaways.
4. *Why citations help readers.* They allow follow-up reading, demonstrate breadth and signal honesty.
5. *Common chart error.* Cropped axes, missing units, misleading colours, undisclosed sample size.
6. *Why long quotes do not prove a point.* Reasoning, not citation length, is your contribution.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Bank-statement explanation.* Step 1: what each column means. Step 2: how to spot errors. Step 3: how to set up an alert.
2. *Brimbank small-business presentation.* Format: a 5-minute student panel with one infographic and one Q&A. Sources: ABS small-business statistics, council planning documents, a local business interview.
3. *Multilingual GST explainer.* Step 1: agree clear plain-English first. Step 2: translate with community feedback. Step 3: use simple examples (e.g., a $33 purchase: $3 GST).
4. *Class opinion piece.* Issues: factual accuracy and respect for those affected. Approach: cite sources; allow right of reply; avoid stereotypes.
5. *Multilingual community report.* Step 1: co-launch with the community organisation. Step 2: translate into key languages. Step 3: release in trusted community-language media.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: rising electricity prices in Victoria.* Format 1: 90-second TikTok for peers. Format 2: 200-word community-newsletter piece. Format 3: 600-word op-ed with three citations. Each adapts language and emphasis; the underlying claim and evidence stay the same.
