# VC2HE8S04 — Economics and Business

## Snapshot

- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Economics and Business (Interpreting and analysing data and information).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Verifying is more than searching.
  - Source authority varies by claim.
  - Independent triangulation is the gold standard.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse and verify sources of data and information**.
- You are learning to grade sources for use, not just collect them.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **provenance**, **methodology** and **bias** in your own words.
- Apply five criteria to evaluate a source.
- Triangulate two sources on the same claim.
- Justify which source carries more weight for a specific claim.

## Core content

**1. Provenance and method.** **Provenance** is where a source comes from: author, organisation, date, on whose behalf. **Methodology** is how the information was produced: survey, official data collection, observation, model. Both shape what the source can support. *Quick check:* Why does method matter as much as the author?

**2. Five criteria for evaluation.** Authority (who made it), currency (when), method (how), independence (any conflicts), verifiability (can others check). Score each criterion on a simple scale and decide. *Quick check:* Which criterion is most often overlooked?

**3. Bias and conflicts.** **Bias** is a tendency to favour one side. A conflict of interest does not automatically discredit a source but should be disclosed and weighed. *Quick check:* Why does disclosure beat secrecy when there is a conflict?

**4. Worked example: assessing a household-budget claim.** Headline: "Most Australians are running out of money." Source A: a bank's PR survey of 1,000 customers, online sample. Source B: ABS household financial-stress data. Source C: a Productivity Commission research note. Evaluation: B and C are more authoritative; A may be useful for an indicator but limited by sample and conflict. *Follow the thinking:* identify the claim → list candidate sources → score each on criteria → justify weight.

**5. Triangulation.** When sources agree, confidence rises. When they disagree, ask: are they measuring the same thing in the same way? Disagreement is often a clue, not a problem. *Quick check:* What does it mean if two reputable sources reach different conclusions?

**6. Distinguishing strong from weak claims.** Strong claims are specific, sourced and reasoned. Weak claims are sweeping or rely on a single source. Strong analysis uses both kinds of claim carefully. *Quick check:* What is one sweeping phrase to avoid?

**7. Visual sources.** Charts, infographics and videos need the same checks: provenance, method, currency, independence, verifiability. Visuals can mislead through axis design and selective framing. *Quick check:* What is one chart design choice that can mislead?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Auditor-General, ABS, RBA and the Productivity Commission provide verifiable data with documented methods. ABC Fact Check evaluates contested claims.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Cite Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors directly; data on Aboriginal communities should be sourced through community-approved channels (AIATSIS, IBA, Supply Nation). Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual fact-checking and ethnic community council reports complement mainstream sources, especially on community-specific economic and consumer issues.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Government data is always reliable." Usually solid on what it measures; check the measure fits your question.
- "If the influencer is famous, they must know." Fame is not authority.
- "Anonymous = unbiased." Anonymity removes accountability.
- "Newer is always better." Recent data matters; some claims need long series.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why method matters as much as author.* A respected author with weak method can still be misleading; a careful method by an unfamiliar author can be reliable.
2. *Most overlooked criterion.* Methodology; the headline number is often noted without checking how it was produced.
3. *Why disclosure beats secrecy.* Readers can weigh the source themselves; hidden conflicts undermine trust when discovered.
4. *Why reputable sources disagree.* Different methods or definitions; different time windows; honest disagreement on interpretation.
5. *Sweeping phrase to avoid.* "Everyone knows", "always", "never", "the data prove".
6. *Misleading chart design.* Cropped axis, misaligned scales, omitted baseline, dual axes without warning.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Guaranteed 10% return.* Check the licence (financial services); verify identity through ASIC's register; investigate "guaranteed" claims (rarely real).
2. *Casey community survey.* Sample size and method; response rate; question wording; how results were summarised.
3. *Stakeholder submission.* Identify the author and their interests; check methodology; verify key facts independently.
4. *Anonymous online posts.* Issues: verification and consent. Use as illustration, not as proof.
5. *Sudanese-Australian small-business survey.* Step 1: read author and methodology. Step 2: respect community ownership. Step 3: pair with ABS or council data for triangulation.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Claim: "Wage growth is finally beating inflation."* Sources: ABS wage-price index (authoritative, well-documented method); RBA Statement on Monetary Policy (interpretation by central bank); a news article (secondary). Scorecard: ABS and RBA score highest on authority and method; news on currency. Verdict: claim is supported by recent data but with caveats on time-frame and inflation measure used. Sources cited.
