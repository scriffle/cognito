# VC2HE8K01 — Economics and Business

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HE8K01. The basic economic problem of scarcity and how it necessitates economic decision-making based on costs and benefits, incentives and opportunity cost.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Economics and Business (Economic literacy).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Wants are unlimited; resources are not.
  - Every choice has a cost.
  - Incentives change behaviour.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **basic economic problem** of **scarcity** and how it **necessitates economic decision-making** based on **costs and benefits**, **incentives** and **opportunity cost**.
- You are learning to make and evaluate choices like an economist.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **scarcity**, **opportunity cost**, **incentive** and **trade-off** in your own words.
- Explain why scarcity forces choice.
- Calculate the opportunity cost of one decision.
- Identify two incentives that change everyday behaviour.

## Core content

**1. Scarcity is the starting point.** **Scarcity** means there are not enough resources (time, money, materials, labour) to satisfy all wants. Even rich countries face scarcity because wants keep growing. *Quick check:* Why is "I have everything I need" different from "I have everything I want"?

**2. Choice and trade-off.** Because resources are scarce, every choice means giving something up. A **trade-off** is what you accept losing to gain something else. *Quick check:* What is one daily trade-off you made this morning?

**3. Opportunity cost.** **Opportunity cost** is the value of the next-best option you gave up. If you choose to play sport on Saturday, the opportunity cost is the part-time work shift you turned down. *Quick check:* If you spend $50 on a concert ticket, what is the opportunity cost?

**4. Costs and benefits.** Decisions weigh **costs** (what you give up) against **benefits** (what you gain). Both come in many forms: money, time, enjoyment, health, social ties. Honest analysis lists both. *Quick check:* Why do non-money costs and benefits matter?

**5. Worked example: a Year 8 student's Saturday.** Options: (a) work a 5-hour shift at a Werribee café for $80; (b) play in a basketball tournament; (c) help at home with the family business. Costs and benefits for each: money, fun, skills, family relationships. Opportunity cost of (a): missing the tournament and family time. Opportunity cost of (b): the $80 and skills the café offers. Honest decision-making lists trade-offs and chooses with reasons. *Follow the thinking:* list options → cost and benefit → opportunity cost → decide with reasons.

**6. Incentives.** An **incentive** is a reason to act. Higher prices encourage suppliers and discourage buyers; rebates encourage solar installation; school grades encourage study. Some incentives are intentional; others are unintended. *Quick check:* What is one government incentive that changed your family's behaviour?

**7. When opportunity cost is hard to see.** Time costs and lost-experience costs are easy to miss. Listing the next-best option you gave up is the test. *Quick check:* Why is "I had nothing better to do" usually wrong?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Local councils across Victoria make scarcity-driven choices every year (e.g., balancing libraries, parks and roads). The Victorian Auditor-General reviews these decisions.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have long managed scarcity through seasonal cycles, sustainable harvest and shared responsibility for Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant families across Melbourne often manage scarcity through extended-family support, remittances and frugal-cooking traditions. Each generation's choices reflect different costs and benefits.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your family has $200 left over this fortnight. List three options, the trade-offs, and your recommendation.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Knox debates spending $500,000 on either lighting in one park or a new sports court. Outline two costs and benefits for each option.
3. **Vocational.** A junior small-business owner decides whether to hire one full-time worker or two part-timers. Outline three opportunity-cost considerations.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates whether unpaid labour at home counts in economic decisions. Identify two arguments on each side.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Vietnamese-Australian family in Springvale chooses between a child's after-school job and homework time. Outline the trade-offs honestly.

## Memorable takeaways
- Scarcity makes choice unavoidable.
- Every choice has an opportunity cost.
- Costs and benefits are not only money.
- Incentives change behaviour.
- Time is a real resource.

## Watch out for these traps
- "If it is free, it is free." Free in money does not mean free in time or opportunity.
- "I had no choice." Almost every decision is a choice between alternatives.
- "Only money counts." Time, health and relationships are real costs and benefits.
- "Opportunity cost is whatever I imagine." It is the next-best real option you gave up.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"Have everything I need" vs "everything I want".* Needs are limited; wants keep growing as choices and information expand.
2. *Daily trade-off.* Time in bed vs eating breakfast; speed of getting to school vs walking with friends.
3. *Concert opportunity cost.* The best thing you could have done with the $50 (and the time at the concert).
4. *Non-money costs.* Health, family time, sleep and learning are real and often more important than money.
5. *Government incentive.* Solar rebates, free public-transport days, school-attendance allowances.
6. *Why "nothing better to do" is usually wrong.* Some next-best real option always exists, even if it is rest or sleep.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *$200 leftover.* Save for a future need (low fun, high security); pay down debt (low fun, high financial benefit); spend on a family outing (high fun, low future benefit). Recommendation depends on values.
2. *Knox $500,000.* Lighting: safer evening use, lower events impact, longer life. Sports court: more daytime use, attracts juniors, ongoing maintenance. Trade-off depends on community needs and existing assets.
3. *Hiring trade-off.* Full-time: continuity and depth; part-timers: flexibility and lower hours per role. Opportunity costs include training, payroll cost and risk exposure.
4. *Unpaid labour debate.* For: it produces real economic value and shapes choices. Against: it is hard to measure. Both sides agree it matters for honest decision-making.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian after-school job.* Income and skills vs grades and family obligations. The honest trade-off depends on the family's situation and the student's long-term goals.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Decision: a council choice between a new library and extended sports facilities.* Trade-offs: cultural and educational vs physical activity. Opportunity cost: the option not chosen. Incentive at play: state grants for libraries; community demand for sports. Recommendation: depends on existing facilities and community survey data; cite the council's consultation report.
