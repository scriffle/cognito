## Core content

**1. Demand.** **Demand** is how much consumers want to buy at different prices. As price falls, demand usually rises. Demand shifts when income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations or population changes. *Quick check:* Why does demand for ice-cream usually rise in summer?

**2. Supply.** **Supply** is how much producers want to sell at different prices. As price rises, supply usually rises. Supply shifts when costs, technology, weather, taxes or producer numbers change. *Quick check:* Why does supply of fresh tomatoes drop after floods in northern Victoria?

**3. Equilibrium and the price mechanism.** **Equilibrium price** is where supply equals demand. If price is too high, surpluses appear and price falls; too low, shortages appear and price rises. The **price mechanism** is this back-and-forth: prices guide resources toward where they are most needed. *Quick check:* What does a queue at one café and empty seats at another tell you?

**4. Consumer sovereignty.** **Consumer sovereignty** is the idea that consumer choices drive what is produced. When enough people buy plant-based burgers, producers respond. Consumers exercise sovereignty within constraints (income, information, advertising). *Quick check:* Why is "consumer sovereignty" qualified rather than absolute?

**5. Worked example: housing rents in Footscray.** Demand rises with population growth, train upgrades and university expansion. Supply lags because building takes years. Result: rents rise. Higher rents encourage developers to build; renters may move further out. Over time, the market adjusts. Government policy can shift either side. *Follow the thinking:* identify demand shift → identify supply lag → explain price change → trace the response.

**6. Markets work, but imperfectly.** Markets often allocate well in everyday goods. They struggle with goods that are: hard to price (clean air); benefit everyone equally (street lighting); or have effects on others (pollution). Government and community step in. *Quick check:* Why is "leave it to the market" not always the right answer?

**7. Information matters.** Prices only signal accurately when buyers and sellers have reasonable information. Misleading advertising, hidden quality or asymmetric information all distort the signal. *Quick check:* Why do consumer-protection laws exist alongside free markets?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian wholesale markets at Epping (fruit and vegetables), Footscray Bus Markets and farmers' markets across the state are visible examples of supply, demand and price in action.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have practised exchange and reciprocity systems long before colonisation: trade routes, shared management of resources, and seasonal allocation. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant-run small businesses across Melbourne respond to consumer demand for diverse foods, services and cultural goods. Vietnamese, Italian, Lebanese and Indian businesses in Footscray, Carlton, Coburg and Box Hill shape local markets.

## Memorable takeaways
- Prices are signals.
- Supply meets demand at equilibrium.
- Consumer choice shapes production.
- Markets work, but imperfectly.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Demand means how much I want." It means how much I want at a given price, with a real ability to pay.
- "Supply rises when demand rises." Supply rises when price rises (or its costs fall).
- "Consumer sovereignty means consumers control everything." Information, advertising and inequality limit control.
- "Markets always work." Many markets fail or need rules to work fairly.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Ice-cream demand in summer.* Hot weather makes people want cold food; more time outdoors; school holidays add demand.
2. *Tomato supply after floods.* Lost crops and disrupted transport reduce supply; prices rise temporarily.
3. *Café signal.* The popular one offers more value at the price or location; the empty one needs to change price, product or location.
4. *Why "consumer sovereignty" is qualified.* Income, information, advertising and inequality shape what consumers can choose.
5. *Why "leave it to the market" is not always right.* Markets miss external effects (pollution), public goods (clean air) and inequality issues.
6. *Why consumer-protection laws exist.* Prices only signal when buyers have accurate information; protection laws reduce information failures.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Petrol price jump.* Lower supply (refinery issue, OPEC cut), higher demand (school-holiday travel), exchange-rate movements affecting imports.
2. *Vacant-housing tax.* Reduces incentive to leave properties empty (supply increases); rents may ease over time; landlord behaviour and unintended effects need monitoring.
3. *Customer drop-off after price rise.* Cut the rise back; add value at the higher price; segment products (premium vs basic); communicate why prices changed.
4. *Surge pricing in emergencies.* For: signals encourage more supply. Against: harms those who can least afford it. Regulation can cap pricing while protecting basic supply.
5. *Bakery in demand.* Raise prices modestly; expand capacity; introduce pre-orders; consider opening hours.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Market: rental housing in Melbourne.* Demand factors: population growth, university enrolment. Supply factors: construction time, planning rules. Price mechanism: rents rise; some renters relocate; developers may eventually respond. Market failure: distributional impact on low-income renters; need for policy intervention.
