## What you are learning
- You are learning to **locate, select and organise** relevant **data and information** from a **range of sources**.
- You are learning to build a working dataset for your research question.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **primary source**, **secondary source** and **data triangulation** in your own words.
- Identify three reputable sources for one economic question.
- Organise data in a simple table or spreadsheet with labels and units.
- Justify which sources to include and exclude.

## Core content

**1. Locating sources.** Economic sources include the ABS (Census, labour force, inflation), Reserve Bank of Australia, ATO, Productivity Commission, Treasury, State Treasury, councils, peak industry bodies, NGOs, peer-reviewed academic articles, news outlets and community-language media. Start with primary sources where possible. *Quick check:* Why is the ABS a strong starting point for many economic questions?

**2. Primary and secondary sources.** A **primary source** is original data: ABS Census, company financial reports, government budget papers. A **secondary source** interprets: news articles, textbook chapters, opinion columns. Both have uses. *Quick check:* Is a news article about an ABS release primary or secondary?

**3. Selecting sources.** Selection is unavoidable. Criteria: authority (who made it), currency (when), method (how), independence (any conflicts), verifiability (can others check it). Discard sources that fail these checks. *Quick check:* What is one sign of low authority?

**4. Worked example: investigating youth unemployment in Melbourne.** Question: How has youth (15–24) unemployment in Greater Melbourne changed between 2019 and 2024? Sources: ABS Labour Force release; Jobs and Skills Australia briefings; The Conversation analyses; an academic study; a youth-advocacy group report. Triangulate across at least three. *Follow the thinking:* identify the question → list candidate sources → score them → keep the strongest → record citations.

**5. Organising data.** Use a spreadsheet with consistent columns: date, value, units, source, notes. Cite each row's source. Save raw and cleaned versions. *Quick check:* What is one risk of overwriting the raw data?

**6. Triangulation.** **Triangulation** is using multiple independent sources on the same point. Agreement increases confidence; disagreement reveals contested ground. *Quick check:* Why is triangulation more than "more sources"?

**7. Ethics in source use.** Cite by author/organisation, year and where accessed. Quote sparingly; paraphrase carefully; avoid using anonymous social-media posts as evidence. Respect copyright. *Quick check:* Why is "I found it online" not a citation?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABS, the Victorian Department of Treasury and Finance, the Victorian Auditor-General and the Productivity Commission publish accessible primary sources. Local councils publish ratepayer reports.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS ethical guidelines apply when using data on First Nations communities. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria publishes its own data and reports. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** SBS, ethnic community councils and multilingual research bodies provide data on community-specific economic conditions in Melbourne.

## Memorable takeaways
- One source is not research.
- Primary records; secondary interprets.
- Organise with labels and units.
- Triangulate to manage bias.
- Cite by author, year, source.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Google says" is not a citation.
- "Government data is unbiased." It is more reliable on some questions; not on all.
- "Recent is best." Often, but older primary data still matters for trends.
- "Anonymous sources are convenient." They are often unverifiable; rarely strong evidence.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why ABS is strong.* Authority, independence, methodology disclosed, regular release schedules, wide coverage.
2. *News article on ABS release.* Secondary. The journalist interprets primary data. The ABS release is the primary source.
3. *Sign of low authority.* Anonymous author, no methodology, no citations, hostile or sensationalist tone.
4. *Risk of overwriting raw data.* You lose the ability to re-check; errors become permanent.
5. *Why triangulation is more than "more sources".* It requires sources to be independent and to address the same point; mere repetition is not triangulation.
6. *Why "I found it online" is not a citation.* Citations identify author, year and access information so others can check.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Melbourne rents.* Primary: REIV rental data; ABS Census housing data. Secondary: The Age coverage; Tenants Victoria reports.
2. *Whittlesea small-business research.* Council planning documents; ABS small-business statistics; ABR/ASIC business registers.
3. *Spreadsheet conventions.* Consistent column headers with units; one source per row; save raw and cleaned files separately.
4. *Anonymous social-media data.* Issues: verification and consent. Use only as illustrations, not as evidence.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian Springvale small business.* ABS business statistics; Federation of Vietnamese-Australian Business Associations; Vietnamese-language media reports.
