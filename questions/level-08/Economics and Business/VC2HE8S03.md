## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct, interpret and draw conclusions** from **graphs, models and text** to identify and describe **economic and business issues, trends and cause-and-effect relationships**.
- You are learning to read economic graphs like an economist would.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **trend**, **correlation** and **cause** in your own words.
- Build a labelled line graph from a small dataset.
- Read a supply-and-demand diagram and describe one cause-and-effect chain.
- Distinguish correlation from causation in an economic example.

## Core content

**1. Constructing a graph.** Choose the right chart for the question: line for time trends, bar for category comparison, scatter for relationships. Label both axes with name and unit. Cite your source. *Quick check:* What axis is usually time on a line graph?

**2. Reading a graph.** Look at axes first, then the trend, then specific points. Describe with direction (up, down, flat), magnitude (how much) and timeframe (over what period). Note any obvious anomalies. *Quick check:* Why is "the line is up" a weak description?

**3. Trends.** A **trend** is a pattern of change over time. Short-term fluctuations are normal; longer time periods reveal the underlying direction. Smoothing (e.g., 12-month moving average) helps. *Quick check:* Why is a single-year change a fragile signal?

**4. Cause and effect.** **Correlation** is two variables moving together. **Cause** requires a mechanism plus evidence that change in one drives change in the other. Many economic relationships are correlations with underlying causes we cannot fully separate. *Quick check:* Why do economists distrust "this caused that" claims?

**5. Worked example: petrol prices and car-use in Melbourne.** Data: monthly average petrol prices and monthly car-trip volumes, 2019–2024. Construct a line graph with two axes. Observe: when prices rose sharply (2022), car trips dipped slightly. Possible causes: higher prices reduce travel; remote work; new infrastructure. Correlation observed; causation requires more analysis. *Follow the thinking:* build graph → describe trend → note correlation → name possible causes → flag uncertainty.

**6. Reading text alongside graphs.** Reports and news often interpret graphs. Cross-check the chart against the text: does the text describe what the chart actually shows? Headlines sometimes overclaim. *Quick check:* What is one common headline overclaim?

**7. Models simplify.** Economic models (supply-demand, circular flow, cost-benefit) are simplifications. Use them to organise thinking, not to claim certainty. *Quick check:* What is one important factor a supply-demand diagram leaves out?

## Memorable takeaways
- Line for time, bar for category, scatter for relationships.
- Direction, magnitude, timeframe.
- Correlation hints; cause needs mechanism.
- Cross-check text against the chart.
- Models simplify on purpose.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Correlation = cause." Not without a plausible mechanism.
- "The line went up, so things are better." Depends on what is going up.
- "Charts cannot lie." Axes, labels and scales can mislead.
- "Models are reality." They are tools for thinking.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Time axis.* Usually the x-axis.
2. *Why "the line is up" is weak.* No direction reference, no magnitude, no timeframe.
3. *Why a single-year change is fragile.* Year-to-year noise can hide or fake a trend; longer windows are more reliable.
4. *Why economists distrust "caused".* Many factors change at once; isolating cause needs controls or natural experiments.
5. *Headline overclaim.* "Inflation crisis" when CPI grew 0.5%; "house prices surge" on a one-month change.
6. *Factor a supply-demand diagram leaves out.* Time lags, regulation, network effects, environmental costs.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Online-shopping chart.* Check the source and date; ask which retailers are included; describe direction, magnitude, timeframe; note any pandemic-era anomaly.
2. *Yarra employment chart.* Are categories the same year-over-year? Is part-time included? What is the source and confidence?
3. *Junior data analyst labelling.* Axes named with units; clear title; source citation; consistent colour scheme; baseline at zero unless justified.
4. *Selective trend showing debate.* Issues: cherry-picking misleads. Address: show the full series with the highlighted part marked.
5. *Unemployment across language groups.* Step 1: check sample sizes. Step 2: avoid implying cause from category differences. Step 3: include community-led commentary.
