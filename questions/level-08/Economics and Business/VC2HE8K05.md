# VC2HE8K05 — Economics and Business

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HE8K05. The reasons people work and the different ways Australians can derive an income.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Economics and Business (Work).
- **Big ideas:**
  - People work for many reasons beyond money.
  - Income comes from more than wages.
  - Work shapes identity, community and society.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Personal and Social Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **reasons people work** and the **different ways Australians can derive an income**.
- You are learning to see work as more than a pay packet.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **wage**, **salary**, **self-employed income** and **transfer payment** in your own words.
- List five reasons people work.
- Identify three different income sources for Australians.
- Explain why unpaid work matters in economic life.

## Core content

**1. Why people work.** Reasons include income, meaning, identity, social connection, skill development, contributing to community, supporting family, building toward a goal. Different reasons can shape what work people choose. *Quick check:* Why is "people work only for money" too simple?

**2. Wages and salaries.** A **wage** is paid by the hour or day; a **salary** is paid as a fixed annual amount. Most employed Australians earn one or the other, plus superannuation. Award rates set minimum pay for many roles. *Quick check:* What is one difference between a wage and a salary?

**3. Self-employed income.** **Self-employed income** is earned by people running their own business as sole traders, contractors or partners. Income is variable; the person manages tax, super and business risk themselves. *Quick check:* Why is income often less stable for self-employed people?

**4. Other income sources.** **Transfer payments** from government (e.g., Centrelink payments, family tax benefits, age pension). **Investment income** from interest, dividends or rent. **Royalties** for creative work. **Trust distributions** in family arrangements. *Quick check:* Why are government transfers part of the income picture for many Australians?

**5. Worked example: a Vietnamese-Australian family in Footscray.** One parent earns a salary as a primary teacher. The other runs a small grocery as a sole trader. A grandparent receives the age pension. A teenager has a part-time café wage. The family includes paid work, self-employed work, transfer payments and youth wages, plus unpaid caring at home. *Follow the thinking:* identify each income source → name the work behind it → discuss security and risk.

**6. Unpaid work.** Unpaid work includes caring, parenting, volunteering and homemaking. It produces real economic value (counted at billions of dollars annually in Australian studies) but does not show up in GDP. *Quick check:* What is one consequence of not counting unpaid work in economic measures?

**7. Work, identity and community.** Work shapes who we are and our place in community. Loss of work (unemployment, retirement, illness) often affects identity and mental health, not just income. *Quick check:* Why do communities celebrate work milestones like apprenticeship completions or long-service awards?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Australian Bureau of Statistics publishes Victorian labour-force data monthly. The Fair Work Ombudsman protects worker rights in Victoria, as it does nationally.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander work includes land management, ranger programs, cultural education, arts, health and many sectors. Indigenous Business Australia and the Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation support Aboriginal employment and enterprise. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant work patterns vary by community and arrival pathway. Skilled-migration arrivals often work in their trained field; humanitarian arrivals often start in entry-level work and progress over time. Settlement services support work pathways.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Salary = high status, wage = low." Both can vary widely; status depends on the role.
- "If you are not paid, it is not work." Unpaid work is essential to households and communities.
- "Self-employment is freedom." It is also risk; many self-employed earn less than employees.
- "Transfer payments are not earned." They reflect contribution to society and are funded by taxes.

A "household income tree": at the trunk, the household; the branches are different income sources; the leaves are reasons each person works.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "only for money" is too simple.* Meaning, identity, social ties and skill development all motivate people. Many would still work if money were not needed.
2. *Wage vs salary.* Wages: paid per hour or day, often shift-based. Salary: fixed annual amount, often professional roles.
3. *Why self-employed income is less stable.* Demand varies; sick or holiday days are unpaid; tax and super are managed by the worker.
4. *Why government transfers matter.* They support people in periods of need (childhood, retirement, illness, unemployment) and stabilise the economy.
5. *Not counting unpaid work.* Carers and parents do essential work that is missing from headline economic numbers, undervaluing their contribution.
6. *Why work milestones are celebrated.* They mark identity, skill, and contribution to community.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Three adults, three reasons.* You might hear: pay, meaning and skill; family obligation; community service; identity and continuity. Different reasons reflect different roles and life stages.
2. *Wyndham volunteer awards.* Care for elders; environmental volunteering; cultural-heritage transmission.
3. *Full-time work or further study.* Long-term earnings, skill-building, family situation; balance short-term income vs long-term opportunity.
4. *Unpaid internships debate.* For: skill-building and networks. Against: financial exclusion and exploitation. Both sides agree clear rules matter.
5. *Sudanese-Australian household budget.* Essential needs first; emergency fund; savings for children's education and caring needs.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Sector: aged care.* Incomes: wages for personal care workers; salaries for registered nurses; transfer payments to many older clients. Reasons: meaning, family precedent, work availability. Challenge: pay rates and workforce shortages. Source: ABS health-and-care workforce data.
