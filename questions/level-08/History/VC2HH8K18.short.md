# VC2HH8K18 — History (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Interpretations of the significance of an ancient society and/or individual and their legacies.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, History.
- **Big ideas:** Significance is judged, not found — and judgements change over time. A legacy is what survives and shapes later societies. Different people weigh the same past differently, for reasons.

## What you are learning
- You are learning **interpretations of the significance** of an ancient society and/or individual, and their **legacies**.
- You should be able to treat significance as an argument built on criteria, not a fact to look up.

## Core content

**Significance is judged, not found.** People decide significance using **criteria** — for example, whether something was *remarkable*, *remembered*, *resonant*, *resulting in change*, and *revealing* about its time — and they can disagree.

**Interpretation.** An **interpretation** is an argued account of what the past means, built from evidence. Interpretations differ because of the sources available, the questions asked, and the values of the person asking. A different interpretation is not automatically wrong.

**Legacy.** A **legacy** is the lasting influence of a society or individual: laws, languages, technologies, ideas, institutions. Roman law shaped European legal systems; Latin shaped English, French, Spanish and Italian; Roman roads and concrete shaped later building. Legacies can be material (a road) or cultural (an idea of citizenship).

**Worked example: interpreting Augustus.** To some, he is the wise founder who ended civil war and brought peace; to others, the man who destroyed the Republic and concentrated power. Same evidence, different criteria — one weighs *stability*, the other *liberty*.

**Interpretations change over time.** The "legacy of Rome" was praised by empire-builders who wanted to copy it, then questioned by later writers concerned with conquest and slavery. **Historiography** is the study of how interpretations themselves change.

**Cultural context.** First Nations communities hold their own interpretations of significance and legacy across Deep Time, determined by community. Greek, Italian, Chinese and Egyptian-Australian families in Victoria often hold strong, living interpretations of their ancestral societies. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Significance is judged with criteria, not found.
- Interpretations differ for reasons — sources, questions, values.
- A legacy is what survives and shapes later societies.
- Judgements of the past change over time (historiography).

## Watch out for these traps
- "Significance is a fact you look up." It is an argument built on criteria.
- "A different interpretation is just wrong." Interpretations can differ legitimately.
- "Legacy is always positive." Conquest and slavery are legacies too.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why historians rank the same event differently.* They apply different criteria and value different outcomes.
- *One material and one cultural legacy of Rome.* Material: roads and concrete engineering. Cultural: ideas of law and citizenship.
- *Which value each Augustus interpretation puts first.* One puts stability first; the other puts liberty.
- *Why views of "greatness" change.* Later generations ask new questions, such as about conquest and slavery.
- *Short summary.* Significance and legacy are interpretations built on criteria and evidence; they differ between people and shift over time, so a fair account names its criteria and a rival view.
