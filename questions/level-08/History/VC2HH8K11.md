## What you are learning
- You are learning **changing evidence and interpretations** of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples as **the world's oldest continuous cultures**.
- You are learning to read history as evolving knowledge.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **continuous culture**, **interpretation** and **historiography** in your own words.
- Identify three pieces of evidence supporting continuous culture.
- Describe how interpretations have changed since the 19th century.
- Discuss the ethical and political significance of "oldest continuous".

## Core content

**1. What "continuous" means.** A **continuous culture** has unbroken transmission of language, lore, kinship and practice across generations, on Country. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples meet this definition over 65,000+ years. *Quick check:* Why is "oldest" not the only point — "continuous" matters too?

**2. Evidence supporting deep continuity.** Archaeological dating (Madjedbebe, Lake Mungo, Devil's Lair); genetic studies; oral accounts aligning with sea-level rise; intact languages, lore and ceremonial practice today. *Quick check:* Why is each line of evidence individually limited and stronger together?

**3. 19th-century interpretations.** Settler-colonial science framed First Nations as "vanishing", "primitive" or "Stone Age". These were wrong and harmful, fuelling Stolen Generations and dispossession. *Quick check:* Why is naming earlier narratives important even when we reject them?

**4. 20th-century shifts.** Mungo Man and Mungo Lady (1968, 1974); growing recognition of Aboriginal scholarship; the 1967 referendum; Mabo (1992); Native Title Act 1993. *Quick check:* How did Mabo reshape interpretation?

**5. 21st-century shifts.** Madjedbebe (65,000 years); Aboriginal-led research; *Dark Emu* (Pascoe 2014); cultural-burning policy; the Uluru Statement from the Heart (2017); Yoorrook Justice Commission. *Quick check:* Why does Aboriginal-led research change interpretation?

**6. Worked example: changing wheat-belt prehistory.** Earlier narratives said Aboriginal Australia had "no agriculture". New evidence — Budj Bim aquaculture, native-grain cropping at Brewarrina and elsewhere — has rewritten textbooks. *Follow the thinking:* evidence reshapes interpretation; interpretation reshapes policy and rights.

**7. Ethical and political significance.** Recognising deep continuity supports Native Title, treaty, cultural heritage and self-determination. Misrepresentation continues to do real harm. *Quick check:* Why does interpretation matter for present-day rights?

## Stretch yourself
Pick one example where evidence has reshaped interpretation. Describe the evidence, the older interpretation, the newer interpretation and the consequences for rights or policy. Cite reputable sources. 300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Old textbooks were objective." They reflected colonial assumptions.
- "The story is settled." It evolves with evidence and voice.
- "All First Nations are ancient and unchanging." Continuity includes adaptation.
- "Interpretation is just academic." It shapes lives.

2. *Why evidence is stronger together.* Archaeology, genetics, oral history and lived practice triangulate.
3. *Why naming earlier narratives.* It shows how power and assumptions shape "facts".
4. *Mabo's effect.* Recognised native title in Australian law and overturned terra nullius.
5. *Why Aboriginal-led research changes interpretation.* It brings authority, depth and accountability.
6. *Why interpretation matters for rights.* Native Title, cultural heritage and self-determination rest on it.
