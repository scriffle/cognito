## Core content

**1. From foraging to farming.** Most humans foraged for over 95% of human history. From about 12,000 years ago, **agriculture** emerged independently in several regions — the Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, the Sahel and the New Guinea Highlands. *Quick check:* Why was agriculture a turning point?

**2. Technologies.** Polished stone tools; pottery; weaving; metallurgy (copper, bronze, iron); writing; the wheel; irrigation. Different societies developed different combinations. *Quick check:* Why do we say "the wheel was not universal"?

**3. Trade.** Early trade networks moved obsidian, salt, copper, shells, textiles and ideas across long distances. The Silk Roads linked China, Central Asia, India, the Middle East and the Mediterranean. *Quick check:* What does trade reveal about distant societies' contact?

**4. Social groups.** Many societies stratified into rulers, priests, warriors, farmers and slaves. Others remained more egalitarian. Gender roles varied. *Quick check:* Why is "social structure" a useful comparison tool?

**5. Religious beliefs.** Polytheistic, animist, monotheistic and ancestor-focused belief systems shaped art, law and politics. *Quick check:* Why are temples, tombs and rock-art sites such rich evidence?

**6. Worked example: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander societies.** Across Sahul, peoples developed sophisticated technologies (stone tools, fish-traps such as Budj Bim and Brewarrina, polished hatchets), fire and land management, complex kinship and lore, trade routes (pearl-shell, ochre, stone-axe heads), languages and seasonal calendars. *Follow the thinking:* deep technology, ecology, society and law in continuous relationship.

**7. Worked example: Mesopotamia.** In Sumer (modern Iraq) from about 4000 BCE, cities grew around irrigated farming; writing (cuneiform) supported trade and law (e.g., Code of Hammurabi); religion centred on ziggurats. *Quick check:* Why is the Code of Hammurabi a useful primary source?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Budj Bim Cultural Landscape (Gunditjmara Country) is World Heritage-listed for its eel-aquaculture system going back at least 6,600 years. Museums Victoria collections.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Across Country, sophisticated economies are documented in works such as Bruce Pascoe's *Dark Emu* and academic agronomy. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant communities bring living connections to early societies — Greek, Egyptian, Chinese, Indian, Mesopotamian and Andean heritage all enrich Victorian classrooms.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Agriculture meant progress." It brought benefits and new harms (disease, hierarchy).
- "Only literate societies are sophisticated." Orality is its own information system.
- "Indigenous economies were hunter-gathering only." Many also farmed, aquacultured and traded.
- "Religion is just belief." It shaped law, art and politics.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why agriculture was a turning point.* Food surplus enabled larger settlements, stratification and specialised work.
2. *Why "wheel not universal".* Many societies used sledges, canoes or carry-baskets and developed other technologies first.
3. *What trade reveals.* Long-distance exchange of goods and ideas.
4. *Why social structure helps comparison.* It reveals who held power and how labour was organised.
5. *Why temples and tombs are rich evidence.* They concentrate beliefs, art and inscriptions.
6. *Why Hammurabi is useful.* Codified laws give a window into values and disputes.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Budj Bim features.* Stone fish-traps; weirs; village foundations; trade items.
2. *Glenelg Shire promotion.* Gunditjmara leadership; cultural-tourism standards; school programs.
3. *Sumerian seal label.* Material; date; uses; meaning of imagery.
4. *"Civilisation" debate.* For: writing enables record. Against: it excludes oral cultures. Both sides accept multiple-criteria definitions.
5. *Gunditjmara–Mesopotamia pairing.* Step 1: Gunditj Mirring contact. Step 2: agreed comparison framework. Step 3: shared classroom presentation.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Societies: Gunditjmara (Budj Bim) and Sumer (Uruk).* Technology: stone fish-traps vs irrigation. Economy: eel aquaculture and trade vs grain agriculture and trade. Social: clan-based with lore vs city-state with king and priesthood. Beliefs: lore and ancestors vs polytheism. Continuities: trade endured; lore and ritual endured. Changes: scale and political organisation. Sources cited.
