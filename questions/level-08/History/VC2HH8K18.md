## What you are learning
- You are learning **interpretations of the significance** of an ancient society and/or individual, and their **legacies**.
- You are learning that significance is an argument supported by criteria, not a fact to be looked up.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **significance**, **interpretation**, **legacy** and **historiography** in your own words.
- Identify criteria people use to judge significance.
- Describe two different interpretations of the same society or figure.
- Discuss why interpretations of significance change over time.

## Core content

**1. Significance is judged, not found.** Nothing is significant by itself. People decide significance using **criteria** — and they can disagree. A useful set of criteria asks whether something was *remarkable*, *remembered*, *resonant* (it still speaks to people), *resulting in change*, and *revealing* about its time. *Quick check:* Why can two historians rank the same event differently?

**2. Interpretation.** An **interpretation** is an argued account of what the past means, built from evidence. Interpretations differ because of the sources available, the questions asked, and the values of the person asking. A different interpretation is not automatically "wrong." *Quick check:* Name two reasons interpretations of the same figure can differ.

**3. Legacy — what survives and shapes the future.** A **legacy** is the lasting influence of a society or individual on later times: laws, languages, technologies, ideas, institutions, stories. *Follow the thinking:* Roman law shaped European legal systems; Latin shaped English, French, Spanish and Italian; Roman roads, concrete and aqueduct engineering shaped later building. A legacy can be material (a road) or cultural (an idea of citizenship). *Quick check:* Give one material and one cultural legacy of ancient Rome.

**4. Worked example: interpretations of Augustus.** To some ancient and modern writers, Augustus is the wise founder who ended civil war and gave Rome peace. To others, he is the man who destroyed the Republic and concentrated power in one person. *Follow the thinking:* same evidence, different criteria — one writer weighs *stability*, another weighs *liberty*. Both interpretations use real sources; they prioritise different values. *Quick check:* Which value does each interpretation put first?

**5. Interpretations change over time.** How a society or figure is judged shifts as later generations ask new questions. The "legacy of Rome" was praised by empire-builders who wanted to copy it, and questioned by later writers concerned with conquest and slavery. **Historiography** is the study of how historical interpretations themselves change. *Quick check:* Why might views of an ancient empire's "greatness" change between 1850 and today?

**6. Whose legacy, and who decides.** Legacies are also claimed and contested. Different communities may value, mourn or critique the same ancient society. Deciding what an ancient society "left us" is itself an act of interpretation, often shaped by who is telling the story. *Quick check:* Why is "everyone agrees on the legacy of X" usually false?

**7. Building a fair interpretation.** A fair interpretation states its criteria, uses evidence, acknowledges at least one rival view, and explains *why* it weighs the evidence as it does. It does not pretend to be the only possible reading. *Quick check:* What four features make an interpretation fair?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Museum and the Hellenic and Chinese Museums display competing interpretations of ancient societies; the State Library of Victoria holds nineteenth-century works that praised "classical civilisation," letting you see interpretation change over time; university classics and history departments debate these legacies today.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations communities hold their own interpretations of significance and legacy across Deep Time, and these are determined by community rather than by outside historians. Whose past counts as "significant," and how a legacy is described, is itself a question of power and respect. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Greek, Italian, Chinese, Egyptian and Indian-Australian communities in Victoria often hold strong, living interpretations of the significance and legacy of their ancestral societies — sometimes differing from textbook accounts.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A documentary calls Rome "the greatest civilisation." Identify the criteria it is using and one criterion it ignores.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Yarra plans a plaque on an ancient society's legacy. Suggest three wordings and explain which is most balanced.
3. **Vocational.** A museum curator must label a Qin dynasty artefact for a diverse audience. Outline three steps to present more than one interpretation.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates whether ancient slavery should change how we judge a society's "greatness." Give two arguments on each side.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Two students disagree about the legacy of an ancient society. Outline three respectful steps to compare their interpretations.

## Memorable takeaways
- Significance is judged with criteria, not found.
- Interpretations differ for reasons — sources, questions, values.
- A legacy is what survives and shapes later societies.
- Judgements of the past change over time (historiography).
- A fair interpretation names its criteria and a rival view.

## Stretch yourself
Choose one ancient society or individual. In 300 words, present two genuinely different interpretations of its significance, naming the criteria and values behind each. Identify one material and one cultural legacy, and explain how interpretations of that legacy have changed over time. State your own judgement and the evidence behind it, and acknowledge the rival view.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Significance is a fact you look up." It is an argument built on criteria.
- "A different interpretation is just wrong." Interpretations can differ legitimately.
- "Legacy is always positive." Conquest and slavery are legacies too.
- "Everyone agrees on the legacy of X." Legacies are claimed and contested.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why historians rank the same event differently.* They apply different criteria and value different outcomes.
2. *Two reasons interpretations differ.* Different sources are available, and the historian's questions and values differ.
3. *One material and one cultural legacy of Rome.* Material: roads and concrete engineering. Cultural: ideas of law and citizenship.
4. *Which value each Augustus interpretation puts first.* One puts stability first; the other puts liberty (republican self-government) first.
5. *Why views of an empire's "greatness" change.* Later generations ask new questions — for example, about conquest and slavery.
6. *Why "everyone agrees on the legacy" is false.* Different communities value and contest the same past differently.
7. *Four features of a fair interpretation.* States its criteria, uses evidence, acknowledges a rival view, and explains its weighting.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Greatest civilisation."* Criteria used: scale, engineering, lasting influence. Ignored: the experience of the conquered and enslaved.
2. *Yarra plaque.* The most balanced wording names both achievement and cost, and credits more than one perspective.
3. *Qin artefact label.* Step 1: present achievement and cost. Step 2: quote two interpretations. Step 3: invite the visitor to weigh them.
4. *Slavery and "greatness" debate.* For: moral cost must shape judgement. Against: we should understand a society in its own terms first. Both accept honest, costs-included history.
5. *Comparing interpretations.* Step 1: name each interpretation's criteria. Step 2: check each against evidence. Step 3: agree on what is shared and where values differ.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Society: Ancient Rome.* Interpretation A: significant for stability, law and engineering (values order and lasting influence). Interpretation B: significant but troubling, built on conquest and slavery (values liberty and justice). Material legacy: roads and aqueduct engineering. Cultural legacy: Roman law and ideas of citizenship. Over time, nineteenth-century admiration gave way to more critical readings. Own judgement stated with evidence, rival view acknowledged.
