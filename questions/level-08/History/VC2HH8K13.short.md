- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, History.
- **Big ideas:** Ancient societies were structured by class, law and belief. Different groups held different power and perspectives. Studying who had voice — and who did not — reveals how societies change.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **features and organisation of ancient societies**, including significant groups and their roles in influencing change.
- You should be able to analyse social structures critically.

## Core content

Every ancient society had rules about who could own land, speak in public, serve in the military and worship where. These rules created a **social structure**.

**Worked example: Ancient Rome (c. 753 BCE – 476 CE).** Roman society divided into **patricians** (wealthy Senate families), **plebeians** (ordinary citizens), **freedpersons** and **enslaved people**. Women of all classes had restricted public roles. Birth, wealth and legal status determined what a person could do, own and say.

**The Conflict of the Orders (c. 494–287 BCE).** Plebeians demanded and won greater rights — tribunes to veto Senate decisions, written law (the Twelve Tables, c. 450 BCE) and access to consulship. Writing laws down meant they could no longer be interpreted only by patricians. This is one of the clearest ancient examples of organised pressure from below changing a political system.

**Enslaved people** were captured in war, born into slavery or sold for debt. They had no vote, no legal marriage and could be punished without trial. Their experience challenges any "Golden Age" narrative of Rome.

**First Nations social organisation.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples developed complex kinship systems, moiety structures and law — including skin-name systems regulating marriage, ceremony, land responsibility and trade. Authority was distributed through Elders and knowledge holders rather than a single ruler. These are the longest-running social organisations known. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Social structure shaped every ancient life.
- Different groups saw the same society differently.
- Pressure from below could change the rules.
- First Nations kinship systems are the world's longest-running social organisation.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Ancient societies were all the same." Each had distinct structures and laws.
- "Only rulers matter." Enslaved people, women and ordinary citizens shaped societies too.
- "Kinship systems are primitive." First Nations governance is complex, sophisticated and enduring.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why laws reveal organisation.* Laws set enforceable rules about rights and roles; myths may idealise.
- *Why written laws helped plebeians.* Unwritten laws could be interpreted by those who controlled the courts; written laws created accountability.
- *Why enslaved experience challenges "Golden Age" narratives.* Rome's wealth depended on forced labour and legal violence.
- *Why kinship systems are complex.* They regulate marriage, ceremony, land, trade and authority across multiple dimensions simultaneously.
- *Short summary.* Ancient societies were structured by hierarchy, law and custom; different groups experienced them differently, and pressure from below could reshape the rules over time.
