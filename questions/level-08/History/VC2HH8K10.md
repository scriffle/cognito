## Core content

**1. Why protocols matter.** Protocols translate respect into reliable practice. They protect cultural knowledge, sacred sites and community authority. *Quick check:* Why is "good intentions" not enough?

**2. Country and Place protocols.** Welcome and Acknowledgement; permission for access to sites; cultural-heritage assessments before development; protecting rock art and ceremonial places. *Quick check:* Why is a cultural-heritage assessment legally required for many Victorian projects?

**3. Ancestral remains.** Returning remains to community is the norm. The Return of Cultural Heritage program and museum policies support repatriation. Australian and overseas institutions hold remains; many are now being returned. *Quick check:* Why is repatriation a high priority?

**4. Cultural artefacts.** Documentation, conservation, restricted access where required, repatriation. Museums consult Traditional Owners on display, storage and loan. *Quick check:* Why might an artefact need restricted access?

**5. Artworks.** Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) protections; consent for reproduction; correct attribution to artist, Country and community; fair payment. *Quick check:* What is one risk of buying "Aboriginal-style" art without provenance?

**6. Shared responsibility.** Traditional Owners hold authority. Governments, institutions and individuals all have roles — councils protect places, museums care for objects, schools teach respectfully, the public follows protocols. *Quick check:* Why is "shared" not the same as "equal"?

**7. Worked example: Victorian Aboriginal Heritage Act 2006.** The Act protects Aboriginal places and objects across Victoria. Cultural Heritage Management Plans are required for many works. Registered Aboriginal Parties (RAPs) speak for Country. *Follow the thinking:* protocols are written into law.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Aboriginal Heritage Act 2006; First Peoples — State Relations; Heritage Victoria; Registered Aboriginal Parties (RAPs) across the State.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS Code of Ethics; ICIP guidance from Terri Janke and Co; Maiam nayri Wingara Data Sovereignty principles. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant heritage organisations (e.g., Italian Australian Institute, Chinese Museum Melbourne) work with overlapping protocols of consent and care.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Protocols are red tape." They are how respect happens.
- "Old objects don't need protection." Many are highly significant.
- "If it's on the internet it's free to use." Permissions still apply.
- "One community can speak for all First Nations." Each Country has its own authority.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why good intentions aren't enough.* Without protocols, harm can occur even with respect.
2. *Why cultural-heritage assessment is legal.* Sites and objects are protected by law.
3. *Why repatriation matters.* Cultural respect, community authority and law.
4. *Why restricted access.* Some objects are sacred and not for public viewing.
5. *Risk of buying "Aboriginal-style" art.* It may be inauthentic, fail to credit artists or breach ICIP.
6. *Why "shared" isn't "equal".* Traditional Owners hold authority; others have supporting roles.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Stone tool on a beach.* Don't move; report; contact local Aboriginal Corporation or Heritage Victoria.
2. *Glen Eira training.* Welcome to Country; RAP partnerships; staff cultural-safety training.
3. *Museum review.* Provenance check; consult Traditional Owners; consider repatriation.
4. *Repatriation debate.* For: cultural respect. Against: research access. Both sides accept Traditional Owner authority.
5. *Art class referencing Wurundjeri designs.* Step 1: invitation. Step 2: agreed brief. Step 3: credit and pay.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Example: a rock-art site on Dja Dja Wurrung Country.* Protocols: consult Dja Dja Wurrung Clans Aboriginal Corporation; do not photograph without consent; restrict access if directed.
