# VC2HH8K15 — History (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Significant events or turning points in an ancient society and their contribution to continuity and change.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, History.
- **Big ideas:** Turning points shift a society's direction. They produce both continuity and change. Significance is judged by scale, duration and consequence.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to identify **turning points** in an ancient society and explain how they contributed to continuity and change.
- You should be able to evaluate what makes an event significant enough to be called a turning point.

## Core content

A **turning point** is an event after which things cannot return to the way they were. Historians judge significance by asking: did it affect many people, did it last, and could it have gone differently?

**The founding of the Roman Republic (c. 509 BCE)** ended monarchy. Elected magistrates and a Senate replaced royal authority. Continuity: the Senate survived. Change: power was distributed among officials and assemblies.

**The Twelve Tables (c. 450 BCE)** gave Rome its first written law code. Before this, law was unwritten and interpreted by patrician magistrates. Written law created accountability. This change shaped Western legal traditions for millennia.

**The Punic Wars (264–146 BCE)** transformed Rome from a regional Italian power into a Mediterranean empire. The crisis of Hannibal's invasion (218–201 BCE) nearly destroyed Rome but ultimately strengthened it. Military expansion, however, undermined the Republic from within.

**The assassination of Julius Caesar (44 BCE)** aimed to restore the Republic but triggered civil wars that ended it. Augustus established the Empire (27 BCE). Continuity: the Senate survived in name. Change: real power concentrated in one person.

**First Nations turning points** include the flooding of land bridges (c. 14,000–8,000 years ago), the development of Budj Bim eel aquaculture (at least 6,600 years ago), and the intensification of trade networks — understood within a framework of continuity and custodial responsibility rather than empire-building.

## Memorable takeaways
- Turning points change direction — they produce both continuity and change.
- Not only wars: legal, social and environmental changes are turning points too.
- Unintended consequences are common.
- "Turning point" means different things in different traditions.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Only wars are turning points." Legal and social changes matter equally.
- "Turning points always mean progress." Some led to collapse or oppression.
- "Aboriginal history has no turning points." It has many, understood through continuity and Country.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why a short battle can be a turning point.* Its consequences may reshape society for centuries.
- *Why writing law down matters.* It transfers interpretive power from a few to many and creates lasting accountability.
- *Why Caesar's assassination backfired.* It removed a strongman without fixing the structural problems that created him — civil war followed.
- *One test for a turning point.* "Would what came next have been significantly different without this event?"
- *Short summary.* Rome's turning points (Republic, Twelve Tables, Punic Wars, Caesar) show how political, legal and military events create both continuity and change — sometimes with consequences opposite to what was intended.
