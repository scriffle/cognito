# VC2HH8K15 — History

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HH8K15. Significant events or turning points in an ancient society and their contribution to continuity and change.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, History (Investigation: Ancient societies 10,000 BCE – 600 CE).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Turning points are moments when a society's direction shifts.
  - Not all significant events are sudden — some turning points unfold over decades.
  - Turning points produce both continuity and change simultaneously.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Intercultural Capability; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to identify **significant events or turning points** in an ancient society and to explain how they contributed to continuity and change.
- You are learning to evaluate what makes an event a "turning point" rather than just another event.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **turning point**, **significance** and **causation** in your own words.
- Identify three turning points in one ancient society and explain their consequences.
- Describe how an event contributed to both continuity and change.
- Discuss the criteria historians use to judge whether something is a turning point.

## Core content

**1. What makes a turning point?** A turning point is an event, decision or development after which things cannot return to the way they were. Historians judge significance by asking: did it affect many people? Did it last? Did it change what came next? Could things have gone differently? *Quick check:* Why might a battle be considered a turning point even if it was short?

**2. Worked example: The founding of the Roman Republic (c. 509 BCE).** The overthrow of Rome's last king, Tarquinius Superbus, ended monarchy and established a republic with elected magistrates, a Senate and popular assemblies. This turning point shaped Roman politics for nearly 500 years. Continuity: the Senate, originally a royal council, survived and grew more powerful. Change: political authority shifted from one ruler to elected officials. *Follow the thinking:* one event creates both continuity (the Senate persists) and change (power is distributed differently).

**3. The Twelve Tables (c. 450 BCE).** Rome's first written law code was a turning point for ordinary citizens (plebeians). Before this, law was unwritten and interpreted by patrician magistrates. Written law created accountability and limited arbitrary power. Continuity: Roman legal principles persisted into the empire and influenced modern law. Change: plebeians gained a tool to challenge elite interpretation. *Quick check:* Why is writing law down a turning point and not just an administrative choice?

**4. The Punic Wars (264–146 BCE).** Rome's three wars with Carthage transformed it from a regional Italian power into a Mediterranean empire. The Second Punic War (Hannibal's invasion, 218–201 BCE) nearly destroyed Rome but ultimately strengthened its military and political reach. Continuity: Roman military culture endured. Change: empire replaced republic in practice long before Augustus. *Quick check:* Why might a war you nearly lose be a bigger turning point than one you win easily?

**5. The assassination of Julius Caesar (44 BCE).** Caesar's murder by senators aiming to restore republican government actually accelerated the collapse of the Republic. Civil wars followed. Within two decades, Augustus established one-man rule — the Empire (27 BCE). Continuity: institutions like the Senate survived in name. Change: real power concentrated in the emperor. *Quick check:* Why did the assassination produce the opposite of what the conspirators intended?

**6. Evaluating turning points.** Not every event is a turning point. Historians ask: would history have unfolded differently without it? How many people were affected? How long did consequences last? Some events look dramatic but change little; others look quiet but shift everything. *Quick check:* What is one question you could ask to test whether an event is truly a turning point?

**7. Comparison: First Nations turning points and Deep Time.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples' histories include significant turning points — the flooding of land bridges (c. 14,000–8,000 years ago), the development of eel aquaculture at Budj Bim (at least 6,600 years ago), and the intensification of trade networks. These events shaped Country and society without the "empire-building" pattern common in Mediterranean history. The concept of "turning point" applies differently in societies that emphasise continuity and custodial responsibility. *Quick check:* Why might a First Nations historian define "turning point" differently from a Roman historian?

## Memorable takeaways
- Turning points change a society's direction.
- They produce both continuity and change.
- Significance is judged by scale, duration and consequence.
- Unintended consequences are common.
- "Turning point" means different things in different traditions.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Only wars are turning points." Legal, religious and social changes can be equally significant.
- "Turning points always mean progress." Some led to collapse, oppression or loss.
- "One event explains everything." Turning points sit within larger patterns of cause and consequence.
- "Aboriginal history has no turning points." It has many — but they are understood within a framework of continuity and Country.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why a short battle can be a turning point.* Its consequences — territory gained, leaders killed, alliances broken — may reshape a society for centuries.
2. *Why writing law down is a turning point.* It transfers interpretive power from an elite minority to a broader public and creates lasting accountability.
3. *Why a near-loss is a bigger turning point.* The crisis forces deep adaptation — military reform, political change, national identity — that an easy victory would not require.
4. *Why the assassination backfired.* It removed a strongman without resolving the structural problems (wealth inequality, military power, weak institutions) that created him. Civil war filled the vacuum.
5. *One question to test a turning point.* "Would what came next have been significantly different without this event?"
6. *Why a First Nations historian might define "turning point" differently.* Emphasis on custodial responsibility and continuity means change is understood relationally — as adaptation within an ongoing system, not as a rupture.
