- You are learning to separate what a person *did* from what *changed* because of them.

## Core content

**1. Role, contribution, achievement — three questions.** *Role* asks what position or function a person or group held (general, pharaoh, council, guild). *Contribution* asks what they added (a law, a road network, a reform). *Achievement* asks what was actually accomplished and lasted. Keep them separate. *Quick check:* What is the difference between a contribution and an achievement?

**2. Agency and conditions.** **Agency** is the capacity to act and cause change. But people act inside conditions they did not create — wars, trade routes, technology, beliefs. The strongest historical answers weigh *both* the person and the moment. *Quick check:* Why is "one great leader changed everything" usually too simple?

**3. Worked example: Augustus and the Roman Empire (63 BCE – 14 CE).** After decades of civil war, Octavian — later titled **Augustus** — turned the Roman Republic into a system of one-man rule (the Principate). *Follow the thinking:* role (first citizen, then effectively emperor) → contributions (ended civil wars, reorganised the army, reformed taxation, built roads and aqueducts, sponsored a census) → achievements (roughly 200 years of relative stability called the *Pax Romana*; a model of empire copied for centuries). He did not act alone: he relied on the army, the Senate, allies like Agrippa, and a population exhausted by war. *Quick check:* Name one contribution and one condition that made it possible.

**4. Worked example: a group, not an individual.** Around 508 BCE the reforms attributed to **Cleisthenes** reorganised Athens into local units (demes) and widened participation, but the *change* was carried by thousands of ordinary citizens who took up the new assembly and council. Groups — guilds, councils, armies, religious orders — change societies as surely as named individuals. *Quick check:* Why might a group's contribution be harder to see in the sources than an individual's?

**5. Worked example: Qin Shi Huang and early imperial China (259–210 BCE).** The first Qin emperor unified warring states, standardised script, currency, weights and cart-axle widths, and began the network later known as the Great Wall. Contributions were vast; so were the human costs (forced labour, censorship). Significance is not the same as approval. *Quick check:* Can a figure be historically significant and harshly judged at the same time?

**6. Sources and bias.** Much of what we know about ancient individuals comes from sources written by supporters, enemies, or much later. Augustus partly shaped his own record (the *Res Gestae*). Always ask: who wrote this, when, and what did they want? *Quick check:* Why treat an autobiography of a ruler with caution?

**7. Judging significance fairly.** A fair judgement names the person's role, gives specific contributions, distinguishes achieved change from intended change, weighs the conditions, and notes the costs as well as the gains. *Quick check:* What four things should a fair judgement include?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Museum's ancient-civilisations galleries; the Hellenic Museum and Chinese Museum in Melbourne; the State Library of Victoria's collections on Roman and Chinese history; classics and ancient-history programs at the University of Melbourne and Monash University.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations societies also remember significant individuals and groups — leaders, knowledge-holders, and resistance figures — but who counts as significant, and how their story may be told, is determined by community. Significance is not only a Western or "great man" idea. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Greek, Italian, Chinese and Egyptian-Australian communities in Victoria carry living memory of these ancient societies, and often hold their own family interpretations of significant ancestors and figures.

## Memorable takeaways
- Role, contribution and achievement are three separate questions.
- Agency always works inside conditions.
- Groups change societies, not only individuals.
- Significance is not the same as approval.
- Always ask who wrote the source, and why.

## Stretch yourself
Choose one significant individual or group from an ancient society. In 300 words, set out their role, three specific contributions, and the change that actually lasted. Weigh at least two conditions that made their action possible, and name one cost or limitation. Cite reputable sources and note where your evidence comes from a biased source.

## Watch out for these traps
- "One great leader changed everything." Conditions and supporters always matter.
- "Significant means good." Significance measures impact, not virtue.
- "If a source survives, it must be true." Surviving sources often serve the powerful.
- "Only individuals make history." Groups change societies too, often invisibly in the record.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Contribution vs achievement.* A contribution is what a person adds; an achievement is the change that actually resulted and lasted.
2. *Why "one leader changed everything" is too simple.* Leaders act inside conditions and rely on others to carry the change.
3. *Augustus — contribution and condition.* Contribution: army and tax reform. Condition: a population exhausted by civil war and ready for stability.
4. *Why a group's contribution is hard to see.* Sources tend to name leaders, not the many ordinary people who carried out change.
5. *Significant and harshly judged.* Yes — Qin Shi Huang changed China enormously and is also judged for forced labour and censorship.
6. *Why treat a ruler's autobiography with caution.* It is written to shape the ruler's own reputation.
7. *Four things in a fair judgement.* Role, specific contributions, achieved (not just intended) change, and conditions/costs.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Single-handedly."* He relied on the army and allies; conditions (war-weariness) made reform possible.
2. *Dandenong display criteria.* Lasting impact; reliable evidence; balance of cultures and groups.
3. *Qin Shi Huang panel.* Achievements: unification, standardisation, the Wall. Cost: forced labour, censorship. Note debate over the sources.
4. *Celebrate cruelty debate.* For: impact is real and worth understanding. Against: celebration can excuse harm. Both accept honest, costs-included history.
5. *Non-European example steps.* Step 1: consult community or expert advisers. Step 2: use published, approved sources. Step 3: credit the society and its scholars.
