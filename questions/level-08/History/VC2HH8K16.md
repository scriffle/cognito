# VC2HH8K16 — History

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HH8K16. Causes and consequences of contacts and conflicts within and/or with other societies, resulting in developments such as the conquest of other lands, the expansion of trade and peace treaties.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, History (Investigation: Ancient societies 10,000 BCE – 600 CE).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Contact between societies was driven by trade, curiosity, migration and ambition.
  - Conflict arose from competition for resources, territory, honour and security.
  - Both contact and conflict produced lasting consequences — political, cultural and economic.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Intercultural Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **causes and consequences of contacts and conflicts** within ancient societies and between them.
- You are learning to analyse why contact sometimes led to trade and exchange, and sometimes to war and conquest.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **contact**, **conflict**, **conquest**, **trade network** and **peace treaty** in your own words.
- Identify three causes of conflict or contact in one ancient society.
- Describe the consequences of one major contact or conflict event.
- Discuss why the same contact could produce both exchange and violence.

## Core content

**1. Why societies made contact.** Trade (desire for goods unavailable locally — metals, spices, textiles, stone). Migration (population pressure, drought, opportunity). Diplomacy (marriage alliances, embassies). Curiosity and exploration. Religious mission. Not all contact was peaceful, but not all was violent either. *Quick check:* Why might a society that lacked tin seek contact with a distant neighbour?

**2. Why conflicts arose.** Competition for fertile land, water, trade routes and strategic positions. Defence against raiders. Dynastic ambition. Religious difference. Honour and revenge. Internal conflicts — civil wars, rebellions, succession disputes — could be as destructive as external wars. *Quick check:* Why might a city on a trade crossroads face more conflict than one in a remote valley?

**3. Worked example: Rome and Carthage — the Punic Wars (264–146 BCE).** Cause: both Rome and Carthage sought control of the western Mediterranean, especially Sicily and its grain. Consequence of the First Punic War (264–241 BCE): Rome gained Sicily — its first overseas province. Consequence of the Second (218–201 BCE): Hannibal's invasion devastated Italy but Rome ultimately conquered Spain and weakened Carthage permanently. Consequence of the Third (149–146 BCE): Rome destroyed Carthage entirely. *Follow the thinking:* cause (trade and territorial competition) → escalating conflict → conquest → empire. The consequences reshaped the Mediterranean for centuries.

**4. Trade as peaceful contact.** The Silk Road (from c. 200 BCE) connected China, Central Asia, Persia, India and the Mediterranean. Goods moved: silk, spices, glassware, horses, ideas, religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam later). Trade routes also carried disease and cultural exchange. The Indian Ocean trade network linked East Africa, Arabia, India and South-East Asia. *Quick check:* Why might trade spread religion as well as goods?

**5. Peace treaties and diplomacy.** The earliest known peace treaty — between the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II and the Hittite king Hattusili III (c. 1259 BCE) — ended decades of conflict and established borders, prisoner exchanges and mutual defence. Diplomacy was a tool of statecraft alongside war. *Quick check:* Why might both sides benefit from a peace treaty even if neither "won"?

**6. Internal conflicts.** Civil wars and rebellions shaped ancient societies as much as foreign wars. The Roman Republic's civil wars (88–30 BCE) ended republican government. The Warring States period in China (475–221 BCE) ended with Qin unification. Internal conflict often drove political innovation — new laws, new institutions, new distributions of power. *Quick check:* Why might a civil war change a society more deeply than a foreign war?

**7. Comparison: First Nations contact and exchange networks.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples maintained extensive trade and contact networks across the continent for tens of thousands of years — exchanging stone, ochre, shells, songs, ceremonies and knowledge. The Pituri trade route crossed thousands of kilometres. Contact involved negotiated protocols, kinship obligations and ceremonial exchange. Conflict occurred but was governed by lore and resolution processes. These networks demonstrate that contact, exchange and diplomacy are not uniquely "civilised" inventions. *Quick check:* What does the scale of First Nations trade networks tell you about ancient Australian society?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Museum's ancient civilisations galleries; Hellenic Museum; Chinese Museum (Silk Road connections); State Library of Victoria; University of Melbourne's Centre for Classics and Archaeology.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Trade networks crossing south-eastern Australia connected Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung, Bunurong/Boon Wurrung, Dja Dja Wurrung, Yorta Yorta and Gunaikurnai Country — moving greenstone from Mt William (Lancefield), shell from the coast, and ceremony across the region. These were governed by kinship obligation and lore. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Greek, Italian, North African, Chinese, Indian and Middle Eastern communities in Victoria carry family connections to ancient trade routes and conflict legacies that shaped migration to Australia.

## Memorable takeaways
- Contact was driven by trade, migration, diplomacy and ambition.
- Conflict arose from competition — but also from honour, religion and internal disputes.
- Consequences include empires, trade networks, cultural exchange and destruction.
- Peace treaties are as historically significant as battles.
- First Nations trade networks show sophisticated, long-distance exchange governed by lore.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Ancient trade was simple barter." Long-distance networks were sophisticated, multi-stage and governed by agreements.
- "Conflict is always about land." Religion, honour, succession and internal politics also drove wars.
- "Contact means conquest." Many contacts were peaceful, diplomatic or mutually beneficial.
- "Aboriginal Australia was isolated." Continent-wide trade and contact networks operated for tens of thousands of years.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why seek contact for tin.* Bronze requires tin; if your region lacks it, trade is essential for military and economic power.
2. *Why crossroads cities face more conflict.* Multiple societies compete for control of the same strategic, profitable location.
3. *Why trade spreads religion.* Merchants carry beliefs, build temples in foreign ports, and create communities that attract converts.
4. *Why both sides benefit from a peace treaty.* War is expensive — peace allows trade, rebuilding and security without ongoing military cost.
5. *Why civil war changes a society deeply.* It forces internal reckoning — new constitutions, new leadership, new distributions of power.
6. *What First Nations trade networks show.* Complex society, long-distance organisation, diplomatic skill and sustained inter-group cooperation over millennia.
