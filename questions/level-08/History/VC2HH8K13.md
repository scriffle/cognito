# VC2HH8K13 — History

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HH8K13. Features and organisation of ancient societies, significant groups, their perspectives and their roles in influencing and changing society.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, History (Investigation: Ancient societies 10,000 BCE – 600 CE).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Ancient societies were structured by class, law and belief.
  - Different groups held different perspectives and different power.
  - Studying who had voice — and who did not — reveals how societies change.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Intercultural Capability; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **features and organisation of ancient societies**, including significant groups, their perspectives and their roles in influencing and changing society.
- You are learning to analyse social structures critically rather than accepting them as natural or inevitable.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **social hierarchy**, **civic participation** and **social mobility** in your own words.
- Identify three significant groups in one ancient society and describe their roles.
- Describe how different groups held different perspectives on the same events.
- Discuss how the actions of specific groups contributed to change over time.

## Core content

**1. What "organisation" means.** Every ancient society had rules — written or unwritten — about who could own land, who could speak in public, who could serve in the military and who could worship where. These rules created a social structure. *Quick check:* Why might an ancient society's laws tell you more about its organisation than its myths do?

**2. Worked example: Ancient Rome (c. 753 BCE – 476 CE).** Roman society was divided into **patricians** (wealthy landholding families who dominated the Senate), **plebeians** (ordinary citizens — farmers, traders, labourers), **freedpersons** (formerly enslaved people with limited rights) and **enslaved people** (who had no legal personhood). Women of all classes had restricted public roles. *Follow the thinking:* birth, wealth and legal status determined what a Roman could do, own and say.

**3. Patricians and the Senate.** Patricians controlled Rome's early political institutions. The Senate, composed mostly of patricians, advised on law, finance and foreign affairs. Their perspective centred on preserving aristocratic privilege and military reputation. *Quick check:* Why might patricians resist sharing power?

**4. Plebeians and the Conflict of the Orders.** Between about 494 BCE and 287 BCE, plebeians demanded and won greater rights — including tribunes to veto Senate decisions, written law (the Twelve Tables, c. 450 BCE) and access to consulship. This struggle is one of the clearest ancient examples of organised pressure from below changing a political system. *Quick check:* Why was writing laws down a victory for plebeians?

**5. Enslaved people in ancient societies.** In Rome, enslaved people were captured in war, born into slavery, or sold for debt. They worked on farms, in mines, in households and as gladiators. They had no vote, no legal marriage and could be punished without trial. Some were freed (manumission) and became freedpersons — but still faced restrictions. *Quick check:* Why does studying enslaved people's experience challenge a "Golden Age" narrative of Rome?

**6. Women in ancient societies.** Roman women could not vote, hold office or represent themselves in court. Elite women sometimes wielded influence through family networks. In other ancient societies (e.g., ancient Egypt), women had somewhat broader legal rights, including property ownership. *Quick check:* Why is it important to ask "whose perspective?" when reading ancient sources?

**7. Comparison: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander social organisation.** First Nations societies across Sahul developed complex kinship systems, moiety structures and law (including skin-name systems that regulated marriage, ceremony, land responsibility and trade). Authority was distributed through Elders, knowledge holders and ceremonial leaders rather than a single ruler. These systems have operated continuously for tens of thousands of years — the longest-running social organisations known. *Quick check:* Why might a kinship-based system be described as complex rather than simple?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Hellenic Museum (Melbourne) for Greek and Roman collections; Melbourne Museum's ancient civilisations displays; State Library of Victoria's classical texts; Koorie Heritage Trust for First Nations social organisation.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Kinship, moiety and skin-name systems are living social structures, not historical relics. Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung and Bunurong/Boon Wurrung governance structures continue to operate in the Melbourne region through Corporations and the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Greek, Italian, Egyptian, Chinese, Indian and Iraqi communities in Victoria carry living connections to ancient societies studied in this descriptor. Family stories often link ancient social patterns to contemporary experience.

## Stretch yourself
Choose one ancient society. Identify three significant social groups and describe their roles, perspectives and power. Explain one moment when a group challenged the existing social order. Compare briefly with one feature of First Nations social organisation. Cite reputable sources. 300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Ancient societies were all the same." Each had distinct structures, laws and relationships.
- "Hierarchy is natural." It is constructed and contested.
- "Only rulers matter." Enslaved people, women and ordinary citizens shaped societies too.
- "Kinship systems are primitive." First Nations governance structures are complex, sophisticated and enduring.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why laws reveal organisation.* Laws set enforceable rules about rights and roles; myths may idealise.
2. *Why patricians resist sharing power.* They benefited from exclusive access to political office and land.
3. *Why written laws helped plebeians.* Unwritten laws could be interpreted in favour of those who controlled the courts; written laws created accountability.
4. *Why enslaved experience challenges "Golden Age" narratives.* It reveals that Rome's wealth and culture depended on forced labour and legal violence.
5. *Why "whose perspective?" matters.* Most ancient sources were written by elite men; asking the question opens up missing voices.
6. *Why kinship systems are complex.* They regulate marriage, ceremony, land, trade and authority across multiple dimensions simultaneously.
