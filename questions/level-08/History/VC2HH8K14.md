## Core content

**1. Beliefs are not just "religion".** A society's beliefs include ideas about the natural world, the afterlife, justice, honour, hospitality and the proper relationship between people and gods or spirits. Values — what a society prizes — show up in laws, stories, art and burial. *Quick check:* Why might a society's burial customs reveal its deepest values?

**2. Worked example: Ancient Greece (c. 800 BCE – 146 BCE).** Greek city-states shared a common pantheon (Zeus, Athena, Apollo, Artemis and others) but each polis emphasised different deities. Athens honoured Athena; Sparta honoured Artemis and Ares. The Olympic Games (from 776 BCE) were a religious festival honouring Zeus at Olympia. Temples like the Parthenon were not just beautiful — they were acts of devotion and civic pride. *Follow the thinking:* shared gods + local emphasis + competitive festivals = a religious system woven into politics and identity.

**3. Everyday life and belief.** Greeks poured libations before meals, consulted oracles (most famously at Delphi) before major decisions, and observed festivals throughout the year. Theatre began as worship of Dionysus. Philosophy — including Socrates, Plato and Aristotle — challenged traditional beliefs but did not replace them for most people. *Quick check:* Why might theatre and philosophy both be described as forms of belief inquiry?

**4. Death and funerary customs.** Greeks believed the dead crossed the River Styx to the underworld. Bodies were washed, anointed and buried or cremated with grave goods — a coin for the ferryman Charon, pottery, jewellery, weapons. Elaborate tombs (e.g., Mycenaean shaft graves) showed wealth and status. Funerary customs reveal beliefs about the afterlife, honour and family obligation. *Quick check:* What can grave goods tell you about a society's values?

**5. Sacred places.** Temples, sanctuaries and oracles were gathering points for worship, pilgrimage and political decision-making. Delphi was consulted by city-states across the Greek world. Olympia drew competitors and spectators from rival cities. These places were considered sacred ground — violating their peace was a serious offence. *Quick check:* Why might a sacred site also be a political site?

**6. Change over time.** Greek beliefs shifted: archaic hero cults gave way to classical temple worship; Hellenistic culture (after Alexander the Great, 323 BCE) blended Greek, Egyptian and Persian traditions; and eventually Christianity supplanted the old gods across the Roman-Greek world by the fourth century CE. Change was often gradual, driven by contact, conquest and internal questioning. *Quick check:* Why does contact with other cultures often change religious practice?

**7. Comparison: First Nations belief and practice.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples hold Dreaming (or Creation) narratives that connect law, land, ceremony and identity. Sacred sites across Country carry ongoing spiritual significance — they are not "historical" but living. Ceremony, song, dance and art maintain connection to Country across tens of thousands of years. This continuity contrasts with the pattern of replacement seen in many Mediterranean societies. *Quick check:* Why might continuity of belief over millennia be as significant as change?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Hellenic Museum (Melbourne) — Greek pottery and sculpture; Melbourne Museum ancient civilisations galleries; NGV classical collection; State Library of Victoria's classical texts.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Dreaming narratives and sacred sites are living systems, not past curiosities. Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung and Bunurong/Boon Wurrung maintain ceremony and connection to sacred sites across greater Melbourne. The Yoorrook Justice Commission has heard evidence on the ongoing significance of these connections. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Greek Orthodox, Coptic, Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh, Jewish and Islamic communities in Victoria maintain ancient religious traditions in contemporary Australia. Family connections to ancient sacred places are living, not distant.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Ancient people were superstitious." Their beliefs were systematic and purposeful.
- "All Greeks believed the same things." City-states, classes and eras differed significantly.
- "Beliefs are separate from politics." In ancient societies, religion and governance were deeply entwined.
- "Aboriginal beliefs are extinct." Dreaming and ceremony are living and ongoing.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why burial customs reveal values.* People invest their deepest beliefs in how they farewell the dead — afterlife, status, family duty.
2. *Why theatre and philosophy are belief inquiry.* Both ask fundamental questions about justice, fate, the gods and human nature.
3. *What grave goods reveal.* Status, wealth, gender roles, beliefs about the afterlife and trade connections.
4. *Why a sacred site is also political.* Pilgrimage, consultation and festival brought together people, resources and decisions.
5. *Why contact changes religion.* Exposure to new ideas, intermarriage and trade introduce new practices and challenge existing ones.
6. *Why continuity is significant.* Sustained belief over millennia demonstrates adaptive strength and deep connection to Country.
