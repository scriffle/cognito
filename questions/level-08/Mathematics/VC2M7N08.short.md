# VC2M7N08 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Compare, order and solve problems involving addition and subtraction of integers.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Number).
- **Big ideas:** Integers extend the number line in both directions. Adding a negative is subtracting; subtracting a negative is adding. Temperature, altitude and finance all depend on integer reasoning.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **compare, order, add and subtract integers** — positive, negative and zero.
- You should be able to solve real-world problems that involve negative numbers.

## Core content

**Integers** are whole numbers: … −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3 … On the **number line**, right is greater. So −2 > −8 and 3 > −5.

**Adding integers — same sign:** add the absolute values and keep the sign. (−4) + (−6) = −10. **Different signs:** subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger and take the sign of the larger. (+8) + (−3) = +5. (−9) + (+4) = −5.

**Subtracting integers:** change to adding the opposite. a − b = a + (−b). So (+3) − (−4) = (+3) + (+4) = +7. And (+6) − (+9) = (+6) + (−9) = −3.

**Worked example — temperature change.** Mount Hotham, 6 am: −4°C. Rises 11 degrees by 2 pm: (−4) + 11 = +7°C. Drops 9 degrees by midnight: 7 + (−9) = −2°C. Overall change: (−2) − (−4) = +2°C.

**Integers in real life.** Bank accounts: deposits positive, withdrawals negative. Altitude: above sea level positive, below negative. History timelines: BCE dates as negative numbers.

**Victorian context.** The Bureau of Meteorology records sub-zero temperatures across the Victorian Alps. Gunaikurnai knowledge of frost patterns in Gippsland links cold-season observations to land management — in Western measurement these involve temperatures below zero. ## Memorable takeaways
- Integers include positives, negatives and zero.
- On the number line, right is greater — even −2 > −8.
- Adding a negative = subtracting. Subtracting a negative = adding.
- Temperature, altitude and finance all use integer reasoning.

## Watch out for these traps
- Thinking −3 > −1 because 3 > 1 — the signs flip the comparison.
- Confusing 5 − (−3) = 8 with 5 − 3 = 2 — the double negative matters.
- Rushing through signs in multi-step problems — write out each step.
