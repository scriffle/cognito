# VC2M8A03 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Use mathematical modelling to solve applied problems involving linear relations, including financial contexts involving profit and loss; formulate problems with linear functions, and choose a representation; interpret and communicate solutions in terms of the context, and review the appropriateness of the model.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Mathematical modelling turns a real problem into algebra. Linear models work when the rate of change is roughly constant. Every model has limits — review honestly.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **model applied problems** with linear functions, especially involving **profit and loss**.
- You should be able to formulate, solve, interpret and review a linear model.

## Core content

**Mathematical modelling** follows a cycle: Problem → Formulate → Solve → Interpret → Review. You translate a real situation into a linear rule, solve, then check whether the answer makes sense in context.

**Revenue** = price × quantity sold. **Cost** = fixed costs + variable costs. **Profit** = Revenue − Cost. If profit is negative, the business makes a **loss**.

The **break-even point** is where Revenue = Cost (profit = 0). Below break-even: loss. Above: profit.

**Choosing a representation.** Tables show specific values. Graphs show trends and break-even visually. Equations let you solve precisely. Choose based on your audience.

**Worked example — school sausage sizzle.** Costs: $120 (supplies). Revenue: $3.50 per sausage. Model: *P = 3.5n − 120*. Break-even: *3.5n = 120 → n ≈ 34.3*, so sell 35 to profit. At 80 sold: *P = $160*. *Follow the thinking:* define variables → write rules → find break-even → communicate in context.

**Reviewing the model.** Is the rate truly constant? Are there hidden costs? Does it apply for all values? A model is useful, not perfect — state its limitations.

**Cultural context.** Aboriginal community enterprises like Budj Bim Tours and Worn Gundidj use business modelling to balance cultural tourism with sustainability. Queen Victoria Market stallholders, Lygon Street cafés and Melbourne food trucks all use linear cost-revenue models for daily decisions.

## Memorable takeaways
- Modelling cycle: Formulate → Solve → Interpret → Review.
- Profit = Revenue − Cost.
- Break-even: Revenue = Cost (profit = 0).
- Every model has limitations — name them.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Profit is always positive." It can be negative (a loss).
- "Break-even means success." It means zero profit — costs covered, nothing earned.
- "The model works for all values." Real constraints exist: you cannot sell negatives, and demand has a ceiling.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *"Interpret" in modelling.* Translate the maths answer into real-world meaning.
- *Revenue $800, costs $650.* Profit: $150.
- *Break-even importance.* Minimum sales to avoid a loss — a critical planning figure.
- *Sausage sizzle break-even.* 35 sausages (since 34.3 rounds up to the next whole number).
- *Model limitation.* Demand has a ceiling; bulk costs may decrease per unit; volunteer time is not costed.
