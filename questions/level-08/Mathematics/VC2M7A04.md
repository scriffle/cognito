# VC2M7A04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7A04. Investigate, interpret and describe relationships between variables represented in graphs of functions developed from authentic data.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A graph makes the relationship between two variables visible.
  - Authentic data grounds algebra in real situations.
  - Reading a graph means describing what happens as one variable changes.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Sustainability; Intercultural Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **investigate, interpret and describe** relationships between variables shown in **graphs of functions** built from **authentic data**.
- You are learning to move between a table, a graph and a verbal description of the same relationship.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Plot data points on a Cartesian plane with correctly labelled axes.
- Identify whether a graph shows a linear or non-linear relationship.
- Describe the trend — increasing, decreasing, constant or changing rate.
- Interpret what the graph means in the context of the data.

## Core content

**1. Variables and axes.** Every graph of a function shows how a **dependent variable** (vertical axis) changes as the **independent variable** (horizontal axis) changes. Choose which variable goes on which axis based on what depends on what. Time almost always goes on the horizontal axis. *Quick check:* If you graph the temperature in Melbourne across a day, which variable goes on which axis?

**2. Tables to graphs.** Start with a table of paired values. Plot each pair as a point *(x, y)* on the **Cartesian plane**. Connect the points if the data is continuous (like temperature over time) or leave them as dots if discrete (like number of students per class). *Quick check:* Should you connect the dots for "number of goals scored per round"?

**3. Linear relationships.** If the points lie roughly along a straight line, the relationship is **linear**. A linear graph has a constant rate of change — every equal increase in *x* gives the same increase (or decrease) in *y*. Example: a phone plan charging $0.10 per text produces a straight-line cost graph. *Quick check:* What does "constant rate of change" look like on a graph?

**4. Non-linear relationships.** If the graph curves, the rate of change is not constant. Example: the height of a ball thrown upward rises quickly, slows, stops, then falls — a curve, not a straight line. *Quick check:* Name one real-world situation that produces a curved graph.

**5. Reading the story of a graph.** Describe what happens in plain language: "As time increases, temperature rises steadily until noon, levels off, then drops in the afternoon." Look for key features: starting value, highest or lowest point, flat sections, steep sections. *Quick check:* What does a flat section of a graph tell you?

**6. Worked example — Birrarung (Yarra River) water level.** Melbourne Water publishes daily water-level data for the Birrarung at various gauging stations. A table shows hourly readings over 24 hours. Plotted on a graph: time (hours) on the horizontal axis, water level (metres) on the vertical axis. The graph shows a gradual rise during a rain event (steep section), a peak around hour 14, then a slow decline. *Follow the thinking:* label axes with units → plot points → connect (continuous data) → describe the trend and its real-world meaning.

**7. Comparing two data sets on one graph.** You can plot two functions on the same axes to compare. Example: daily maximum temperature in Melbourne vs Mildura over a week — two lines on the same graph, same axes, different colours. This makes differences and similarities visible at a glance. *Quick check:* Why is it important to use the same scale when comparing two data sets?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Water's Birrarung gauging data, Bureau of Meteorology weather stations across Victoria, PTV patronage statistics and VicRoads traffic-count data all provide authentic data sets for graphing. The Melbourne Museum and ACMI feature data visualisations in their exhibits.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung knowledge of Birrarung seasonal flow patterns represents long-held understanding of how water levels change across seasons — a relationship between time and water that has been observed and managed for tens of thousands of years. Gunaikurnai knowledge of Gippsland Lakes water cycles and Eastern Maar knowledge of volcanic-plain water systems encode similar variable relationships. AIATSIS publishes research on Indigenous environmental observation systems. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Data literacy is valued across cultures. Students from farming families understand seasonal patterns intuitively; students from urban migrant backgrounds bring experience of tracking prices, exchange rates and travel times — all graphable relationships.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You record the temperature outside your school every hour from 8 am to 4 pm. Describe what the graph would probably look like and identify the peak.
2. **Civic.** Whittlesea City Council publishes monthly recycling tonnage data. Sketch a possible graph over 12 months and describe any seasonal patterns.
3. **Vocational.** A barista tracks the number of coffees sold each hour during a shift. Describe the expected shape of the graph and identify the busiest period.
4. **Ethical.** A news article shows a graph of social-media screen time vs sleep hours for teenagers. The graph slopes downward. Discuss what this means and whether the graph proves one variable causes the other.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Gunaikurnai ranger records water levels in the Gippsland Lakes weekly for a year. Describe what seasonal patterns the graph might show and why this data matters for land management.

## Memorable takeaways
- A graph makes the relationship between two variables visible.
- Label axes, include units, choose the right scale.
- Linear means straight line and constant rate of change.
- Non-linear means the rate of change varies.
- Always describe the graph in the context of the real data.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "As ___ increases, ___ (increases / decreases / stays the same). The graph shows that ___. The steepest section is at ___ because ___."
- **Words to keep close.** variable, dependent, independent, Cartesian plane, linear, non-linear, rate of change, trend, axes, scale.
- **Try this picture.** Draw a blank Cartesian plane. Label the horizontal axis "Time (hours)" and the vertical axis "Temperature (°C)." Plot five made-up temperature readings and connect them with a smooth line.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Collect data on something that changes over a week (e.g., minutes of exercise, daily high temperature) and create your own graph with a written description.

## Stretch yourself
Collect or look up two authentic Victorian data sets that share a common independent variable (for example, daily maximum temperature and daily ice-cream sales over two weeks, or monthly rainfall and dam level over a year). Create a table for each, plot both on the same Cartesian plane with a clear legend, and write a paragraph describing the relationship. Is it linear or non-linear? Is there a connection between the two data sets? Could one variable influence the other, or is the link coincidental? Justify your reasoning. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "The graph goes up, so the situation is good." A rising graph only tells you the variable is increasing — whether that is good or bad depends on context.
- "Correlation means causation." Two variables moving together does not prove one causes the other.
- "All graphs are straight lines." Many real-world relationships are curved.
- "You can ignore the axis labels." Without labels and units, a graph is meaningless.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Temperature graph axes.* Time (hours) on the horizontal axis, temperature (°C) on the vertical axis — temperature depends on time of day.
2. *Goals per round — connect the dots?* No — the data is discrete (you cannot score half a goal between rounds), so use separate dots.
3. *Constant rate of change on a graph.* A straight line — equal steps along the horizontal axis produce equal steps along the vertical axis.
4. *Curved-graph situation.* A ball thrown upward; population growth; water filling a curved container — any situation where the rate of change is not constant.
5. *Flat section meaning.* The dependent variable is not changing — it stays constant while the independent variable continues to increase.
6. *Same scale for comparing.* Different scales would distort the comparison, making one data set look larger or more variable than it actually is.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *School temperature.* Graph rises from cool morning (8 am) to a peak around 1–2 pm, then drops toward 4 pm. Shape: roughly a hill or inverted U.
2. *Recycling tonnage.* Likely higher in summer (more packaging, holiday waste) and December (Christmas), lower in winter. Seasonal wave pattern.
3. *Barista coffees.* Peaks around 7–9 am (morning rush) and possibly 12–1 pm (lunch). Dips mid-morning and mid-afternoon.
4. *Screen time vs sleep.* The downward slope suggests that as screen time increases, sleep hours decrease. However, correlation does not prove causation — other factors (stress, schedule) may drive both.
5. *Gippsland Lakes water levels.* Higher after winter–spring rains, lower in summer–autumn. Seasonal pattern matters for managing waterbird habitat, fishing and cultural sites.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Data sets: Melbourne daily max temperature and daily ice-cream sales over two weeks. Both plotted on the same axes (day on horizontal, temperature and sales on two vertical scales or normalised). Trend: both rise and fall together — positive correlation. Likely non-linear on hot days (sales spike). Connection: temperature probably influences sales, but other factors (weekends, holidays) also matter. Correlation observed; causation plausible but not proven from this graph alone.
