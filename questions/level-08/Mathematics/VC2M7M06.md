# VC2M7M06 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7M06. Use mathematical modelling to solve practical problems involving ratios of lengths, areas and volumes; formulate problems, interpret and communicate solutions in terms of the situation, justifying choices made about the representation.

## Core content

**1. What is mathematical modelling?** Mathematical modelling is the process of translating a real-world problem into mathematics, solving it, and then interpreting the result back in the real world. The cycle has four stages: **formulate** (identify variables, assumptions, relationships), **solve** (do the mathematics), **interpret** (what does the answer mean in context?), **communicate** (explain your reasoning and justify your choices). *Quick check:* Name the four stages of the modelling cycle.

**2. Ratios of lengths.** A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind. If a model car is built at a scale of 1 : 24, every length on the model is 1/24 of the real length. A real car 4.8 m long becomes a model 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.2 m = 20 cm long. *Quick check:* A map has a scale of 1 : 50 000. Two towns are 3 cm apart on the map. What is the real distance?

**3. Ratios of areas.** When every length scales by factor k, areas scale by **k²**. If the model car is 1 : 24, its windscreen area is 1/24² = 1/576 of the real windscreen area. This is because area depends on two length dimensions (length × width), and both are scaled. *Quick check:* A garden plan is drawn at 1 : 100. A lawn on the plan has area 6 cm². What is the real lawn area?

**4. Ratios of volumes.** When every length scales by factor k, volumes scale by **k³**. The model car's engine bay volume is 1/24³ = 1/13 824 of the real engine bay. Volume depends on three length dimensions. *Quick check:* A scale model of a water tank is 1 : 10. The model holds 0.5 L. How much does the real tank hold?

**5. The modelling cycle in action.** Not every problem hands you a formula. You may need to decide what shape to approximate an object with, what measurements to use, and what to ignore. State your assumptions clearly — for example, "I modelled the water tank as a cylinder" or "I assumed the walls have negligible thickness." *Quick check:* Why is stating assumptions an important part of modelling?

**6. Worked example — scaling a community garden bed.**
A primary school in Footscray has a rectangular garden bed that is 2 m × 1 m. They want to build a new bed that is similar in shape but with all lengths doubled.

*Follow the thinking:*
- **Formulate:** Scale factor k = 2. Original area = 2 × 1 = 2 m². Original volume of soil (depth 0.3 m) = 2 × 1 × 0.3 = 0.6 m³.
- **Solve:** New area = k² × original = 4 × 2 = 8 m². New volume = k³ × original = 8 × 0.6 = 4.8 m³.
- **Interpret:** The new bed covers four times the area and needs eight times the soil. Doubling the lengths does not just double the resources.
- **Communicate:** "Doubling each dimension quadruples the planting area but requires eight times the soil. The school should budget for 4.8 m³ of soil, not 1.2 m³."

**7. Communicating and justifying.** A good modelling response includes: the assumptions you made, the mathematical setup, the solution with units, an interpretation in plain language, and a justification for why your model is reasonable. If your answer seems unreasonable, revisit your assumptions. *Quick check:* You calculate that a scaled-up fish pond needs 800 000 litres. The school budget is for 2 000 litres. What should you check first?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Scale models are used across Victorian industries: architects at the Melbourne School of Design build physical and digital models; the Eureka Tower scale model at Eureka Skydeck demonstrates length, area and volume scaling; water authorities like Melbourne Water use scaled models to plan reservoir capacity. CSIRO uses mathematical modelling for climate, water and agriculture.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The engineered eel traps at Budj Bim on Gunditjmara Country involve careful proportioning of channels, pools and stone walls — a practical application of scaling relationships between lengths, areas and water volumes refined over at least 6,600 years. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students from farming and regional backgrounds bring practical experience of scaling — paddock areas, water-tank volumes, fertiliser rates per hectare. In urban Melbourne, families involved in cooking, tailoring or construction use ratio reasoning daily.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You are doubling a cake recipe. The original uses a round tin with diameter 20 cm. You want to use a tin with diameter 30 cm instead. The scale factor for length is 1.5. By what factor does the base area increase? Will the doubled recipe fill the larger tin to the same depth?
2. **Civic.** Moreland City Council is designing a new public pool. A 1 : 50 scale model holds 12 litres. Estimate the volume of the real pool in litres and then convert to megalitres. State your assumptions.
3. **Vocational.** A landscaping apprentice is quoting for paving. A paved area on the plan (scale 1 : 200) measures 3 cm × 5 cm. What is the real area in square metres? If pavers cost $45 per m², what is the materials cost?
4. **Ethical.** A company advertises a "double-size" drink bottle. The bottle is twice as tall but otherwise the same shape. Is the volume really doubled? Explain using the k³ rule and discuss whether the advertising is fair.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Many cultures use body-based units (handspan, cubit, foot). Choose two body-based units and measure the same object. Set up a ratio between the two measurements. Discuss why standardised units matter for scaling.

## Memorable takeaways
- Scale length by k → area scales by k² → volume scales by k³.
- The modelling cycle: formulate, solve, interpret, communicate.
- Doubling every length gives four times the area and eight times the volume.
- Always state your assumptions and check your answer against reality.
- Units matter — include them at every step.

## Stretch yourself
A Victorian water authority is planning a new cylindrical water tank to replace an old one. The old tank has radius 2 m and height 3 m. The new tank must hold exactly three times the volume. The authority wants the new tank to be geometrically similar to the old one (same proportions). Calculate the required scale factor k (hint: k³ = 3, so k = ∛3). Find the new radius and height to two decimal places. Then calculate the ratio of the surface areas (old to new). A farmer suggests simply tripling the height instead of scaling all dimensions. Calculate the volume of the farmer's design and explain why it does not produce a similar shape. Discuss which design is better and why, stating your assumptions. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Thinking area doubles when length doubles.** Area scales by k², not k. Doubling lengths quadruples area.
- **Thinking volume doubles when length doubles.** Volume scales by k³. Doubling lengths gives eight times the volume.
- **Forgetting to interpret.** A number without context is not a solution. Always say what the number means in the real situation.
- **Ignoring assumptions.** If you modelled a hill as a cone, say so — and acknowledge what that simplification misses.

A note on certainty. The scaling relationships k, k², k³ are exact mathematical consequences of similarity. In practice, real objects may not be perfectly similar — walls have thickness, shapes are approximate, materials are not uniform. Good modelling acknowledges these gaps between the model and reality.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Four stages of modelling.* Formulate, solve, interpret, communicate.
2. *Map distance.* 3 cm × 50 000 = 150 000 cm = 1 500 m = 1.5 km.
3. *Real lawn area.* Scale factor = 100, so area factor = 100² = 10 000. Real area = 6 × 10 000 = 60 000 cm² = 6 m².
4. *Real tank volume.* Scale factor = 10, so volume factor = 10³ = 1 000. Real volume = 0.5 × 1 000 = 500 L.
5. *Why state assumptions?* So others can check whether your model is reasonable and understand its limitations.
6. *Garden bed.* New area = 8 m², new soil volume = 4.8 m³ (worked through in example).
7. *Check first.* Whether the scale factor was applied correctly — you may have cubed when you should not have, or used the wrong scale.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Cake tin.* Area factor = 1.5² = 2.25. The doubled recipe (2× batter) in a tin with 2.25× base area will be slightly shallower than the original — about 2 ÷ 2.25 ≈ 0.89 of the original depth.
2. *Public pool.* Volume factor = 50³ = 125 000. Real volume = 12 × 125 000 = 1 500 000 L = 1.5 ML. Assumption: the model is geometrically similar to the real pool.
3. *Paving.* Plan area = 15 cm². Real area = 15 × 200² = 15 × 40 000 = 600 000 cm² = 60 m². Cost = 60 × $45 = $2 700.
4. *"Double-size" bottle.* Only height is doubled (k = 2 for height, k = 1 for width). Volume = π r² × 2h = 2 × original, so the volume is doubled. But if it were truly scaled by k = 2 in all dimensions, volume would be 8× original. The ad is technically correct only if the width stays the same.
5. *Body-based units.* Answers vary. Key point: different people have different hand spans, so the ratio between units varies — standardised units remove this ambiguity.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Old volume = π × 2² × 3 = 12π m³. New volume = 36π m³. k³ = 3, so k = ∛3 ≈ 1.44. New radius = 2 × 1.44 = 2.88 m. New height = 3 × 1.44 = 4.33 m. Surface area ratio = k² = (∛3)² = 3^(2/3) ≈ 2.08. Farmer's design: radius stays at 2 m, height triples to 9 m. Volume = π × 4 × 9 = 36π m³ — correct volume but not similar (ratio height : diameter changes from 3 : 4 to 9 : 4). The similar design is structurally sounder and easier to maintain; the farmer's tall, narrow tank is less stable and harder to clean. Assumption: wall thickness is negligible relative to dimensions.
