# VC2M8N01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8N01. Recognise irrational numbers in applied contexts, including π and numbers that develop from the square root of positive real numbers that are not perfect squares, and recognise that irrational numbers cannot develop from the division of integer values by natural numbers.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Number and Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Not all numbers can be written as fractions — those that cannot are called irrational.
  - π and square roots of non-perfect-squares are irrational: their decimals never terminate and never repeat.
  - Rational numbers are exactly those that can be expressed as one integer divided by a natural number.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **recognise irrational numbers** in applied contexts and to understand **why they cannot be written as fractions**.
- You are learning to distinguish irrational numbers from rational numbers.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define irrational number and give examples (π, √2, √3, √5).
- Explain why square roots of non-perfect-squares are irrational.
- Distinguish rational numbers (expressible as integer ÷ natural number) from irrational numbers.
- Identify irrational numbers when they arise in measurement and geometry.

## Core content

**1. Rational numbers — a recap.** A **rational number** is any number that can be written as a fraction p/q, where p is an integer and q is a natural number (q ≠ 0). Examples: 3/4, −7/2, 5 (which is 5/1), 0.75 (which is 3/4), 0.333... (which is 1/3). Terminating and repeating decimals are always rational. *Quick check:* Is 0.125 rational? Write it as a fraction.

**2. What makes a number irrational?** An **irrational number** cannot be written as any fraction of integers. Its decimal expansion goes on forever without ever settling into a repeating pattern. You cannot find any integers p and q such that the number equals p/q. *Quick check:* A number has a decimal that goes on forever but never repeats. Is it rational or irrational?

**3. π is irrational.** π ≈ 3.14159265358979... The digits continue forever with no repeating block. This was proven mathematically in 1761 by Johann Lambert. We use approximations like 3.14 or 22/7, but these are not exact — π itself cannot be captured by any fraction. *Quick check:* Is 22/7 equal to π? Why or why not?

**4. Square roots of non-perfect-squares are irrational.** A **perfect square** is an integer whose square root is also an integer: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100... The square root of any positive integer that is *not* a perfect square is irrational. √2 ≈ 1.41421356..., √3 ≈ 1.73205080..., √5 ≈ 2.23606797... — none of these terminate or repeat. *Quick check:* Is √49 irrational? What about √50?

**5. Why √2 is irrational — the idea.** Suppose √2 could be written as a fraction p/q in simplest form. Then p² = 2q², which means p² is even, so p must be even. Write p = 2k. Then 4k² = 2q², so q² = 2k², meaning q is also even. But that contradicts "simplest form" (both p and q cannot be even). This **proof by contradiction** shows no such fraction exists. *Quick check:* What does "proof by contradiction" mean in plain language?

**6. Worked example — identifying irrational numbers in a measurement.**
You use Pythagoras' theorem on a right-angled triangle with both legs equal to 1 m. The hypotenuse = √(1² + 1²) = √2 m.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Is 2 a perfect square? No (1² = 1, 2² = 4 — nothing gives exactly 2).
- Therefore √2 is irrational.
- On a calculator: √2 ≈ 1.41421356... The digits do not terminate or repeat.
- In practical use, we round (≈ 1.414 m), but the exact length cannot be expressed as a fraction.

**7. Where irrational numbers appear.** In circles (π in circumference and area formulas). In right-angled triangles (√2, √3, √5 through Pythagoras). In the diagonal of a square (side × √2). In the height of an equilateral triangle (side × √3 / 2). Whenever you see a square root of a non-perfect-square or π in a calculation, the exact answer is irrational. *Quick check:* A square has side length 6 cm. Is its diagonal rational or irrational?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Engineers at the Australian Synchrotron in Clayton work with π daily in circular-path calculations. Architects designing the Melbourne Rectangular Stadium calculated diagonal measurements involving √2. AMSI education materials explore the number system from natural numbers to irrationals. The State Library of Victoria's dome involves π in every dimension.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The concept of number systems as formal categories is a Western mathematical framework. First Nations mathematical thinking emphasises relationships, patterns and practical reasoning — which can accommodate concepts like "a length that does not fit neatly into a counting system" without needing the same formal apparatus. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The discovery and proof of irrational numbers has a rich cross-cultural history. Ancient Indian mathematicians (Sulba Sutras, c. 800 BCE) approximated √2 for altar construction. The Greek discovery that √2 is irrational reportedly caused a philosophical crisis in Pythagorean thought. Students can explore how different cultures responded to numbers that "don't fit."

## Try these scenarios

2. **Civic.** A council is designing a circular roundabout with diameter 20 m. The exact circumference is 20π. Explain why the circumference is irrational and what this means for practical measurement.
3. **Vocational.** A tiler needs to cut a square tile diagonally. The tile is 30 cm × 30 cm. Calculate the exact diagonal length. Is it rational or irrational? What value would the tiler use in practice?
4. **Ethical.** A textbook says "π = 22/7." Is this statement true or misleading? Explain the difference between an approximation and an exact value.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research the Sulba Sutras approximation of √2 (c. 800 BCE India). Write two sentences describing the approximation and explain how close it is to the actual value.

## Memorable takeaways
- Rational = can be written as a fraction (integer ÷ natural number). Irrational = cannot.
- Irrational decimals never terminate and never repeat.
- π is irrational. √2, √3, √5, √7, √(any non-perfect-square) are irrational.
- √4 = 2 (rational), √9 = 3 (rational) — perfect squares give rational roots.
- In practice we round irrational numbers, but the exact value cannot be captured by any fraction.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Thinking all square roots are irrational.** √4 = 2, √9 = 3, √25 = 5 — perfect squares have rational roots.
- **Thinking 22/7 = π.** 22/7 ≈ 3.142857... (repeating) while π ≈ 3.141592... (non-repeating). They are close but not equal.
- **Thinking long decimals must be irrational.** 1/7 = 0.142857142857... goes on forever but repeats, so it is rational.
- **Thinking irrational means "unreasonable."** In maths, "irrational" means "not a ratio" — it is a technical term, not a judgement.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Is 0.125 rational?* Yes. 0.125 = 1/8.
2. *Never-ending, never-repeating decimal.* Irrational.
3. *Is 22/7 equal to π?* No. 22/7 is a rational approximation (≈ 3.142857..., repeating). π is irrational (≈ 3.141592..., non-repeating).
4. *√49 and √50.* √49 = 7 (rational, since 49 is a perfect square). √50 is irrational (50 is not a perfect square).
5. *Proof by contradiction.* Assume the opposite of what you want to prove. Show that this leads to an impossibility. Conclude the opposite must be true.
6. *Hypotenuse of 1, 1 triangle.* √2 — irrational because 2 is not a perfect square.
7. *Diagonal of a square with side 6.* Diagonal = 6√2. Since √2 is irrational, 6√2 is also irrational.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Diagonal of 1 m square.* √2 is proven irrational — its decimal never repeats, so no fraction p/q can ever equal it exactly. 1 and 5/12 ≈ 1.4167, which is close but not exact.
2. *Roundabout circumference.* 20π is irrational because π is irrational and multiplying by a non-zero rational (20) preserves irrationality. In practice, builders round to a workable decimal.
3. *Tile diagonal.* √(30² + 30²) = √1800 = 30√2 ≈ 42.43 cm. Irrational. The tiler would measure to the nearest mm: 424 mm.
4. *"π = 22/7."* Misleading. 22/7 is an approximation accurate to two decimal places. π is irrational and cannot equal any fraction.
5. *Sulba Sutras.* The approximation is √2 ≈ 1 + 1/3 + 1/(3×4) − 1/(3×4×34) ≈ 1.4142156... Actual √2 ≈ 1.4142135... — accurate to five decimal places, remarkably precise for c. 800 BCE.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Triangle with legs 1 and √3: c² = 1 + 3 = 4, c = 2 (rational). Triangle with legs √2 and √2: c² = 2 + 2 = 4, c = 2 (rational). Yes, it is possible for two irrational legs to give a rational hypotenuse — whenever their squares sum to a perfect square. These examples show that irrational numbers can combine to produce rational results — irrationality does not "carry through" addition or square-rooting in predictable ways. The key is whether the sum of the squares is a perfect square.
