# VC2M7ST03 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Plan and conduct statistical investigations for issues involving discrete and continuous numerical data, and data collected from primary and secondary sources; analyse and interpret distributions of data and report findings in terms of shape and summary statistics.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** A statistical investigation follows a cycle: question → plan → collect → analyse → report. Data can come from primary or secondary sources. Reporting means connecting shape and summary statistics back to the question.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan and conduct a statistical investigation** from question to report.
- You should be able to analyse distributions and communicate findings using shape and summary statistics.

## Core content

Every investigation follows a cycle: **pose a question → plan → collect data → analyse → report**. A good statistical question expects variability (e.g. "How long do Year 7 students take to travel to school?" — not "How long does one student take?").

**Primary data** is data you collect (surveys, measurements). **Secondary data** is collected by others (ABS Census, Bureau of Meteorology). Primary gives you control; secondary saves time and covers larger groups.

**Planning** means deciding: What variable? Discrete or continuous? Who to sample? How many? Watch for **bias** — will your method favour certain values? Surveying only friends may not represent the whole group.

**Analysing** means creating a display (dot plot, stem-and-leaf) and describing **shape** (symmetric, skewed), **centre** (mean, median, mode) and **spread** (range, outliers).

**Worked example.** A Year 7 class surveys travel times. 30 responses range from 5 to 60 minutes. Stem-and-leaf plot shows positively skewed shape — most students travel 10–30 min, with a tail to 60 min. Median = 24 min. Mean = 25.3 min. The longer commutes pull the mean above the median.

**Reporting** connects analysis to the question. State the question, method, display, statistics, interpretation and **limitations** (e.g. "Our sample was one class only").

**Victorian context.** The ABS Census, Bureau of Meteorology and CensusAtSchool Australia provide rich secondary data. First Nations organisations like the Lowitja Institute emphasise **data sovereignty** — community control over how data is collected and reported.

## Memorable takeaways
- The cycle: question → plan → collect → analyse → report.
- Primary data: you collect it. Secondary data: already collected.
- Check for bias in your method and errors in your data.
- Report shape, centre and spread — connect them to the question.
- State limitations honestly.

## Watch out for these traps
- Vague questions — specify what you measure, for whom and how.
- Convenience sampling — surveying only friends may not represent the population.
- Confusing correlation with causation — moving together does not prove one causes the other.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Rewrite "What is the weather?" as a statistical question.* "What are the daily maximum temperatures in Melbourne during January?"
- *BoM rainfall data — primary or secondary?* Secondary.
- *Surveying only friends — why biased?* They may have similar habits, so the sample may not represent the whole year level.
- *Height recorded as 17.2 m.* Likely 1.72 m — a decimal-place error.
- *Why mention limitations?* So the reader knows how far findings can be generalised.
