# VC2M7ST03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7ST03. Plan and conduct statistical investigations for issues involving discrete and continuous numerical data, and data collected from primary and secondary sources; analyse and interpret distributions of data and report findings in terms of shape and summary statistics.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Statistics and Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A statistical investigation follows a cycle: pose a question, collect data, analyse, interpret, report.
  - Data can come from primary sources (you collect it) or secondary sources (someone else collected it).
  - Reporting means connecting shape and summary statistics back to the original question.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; ICT Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan and conduct a statistical investigation** from question to report.
- You are learning to analyse distributions and communicate findings using shape and summary statistics.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Pose a statistical question that can be answered with data.
- Plan a data-collection method using primary or secondary sources.
- Analyse the distribution (shape, centre, spread, outliers).
- Report findings that answer the original question with evidence.

## Core content

**1. The statistical investigation cycle.** Every investigation follows a cycle: **pose a question → plan → collect data → analyse → interpret and report**. The question drives everything — if the question is vague, the investigation will be too. A good statistical question expects variability in the answer (e.g. "How long do Year 7 students at our school take to travel to school?" rather than "How long does Priya take?"). *Quick check:* Rewrite "What is the weather?" as a statistical question that expects variability.

**2. Primary and secondary data.** **Primary data** is data you collect yourself — surveys, experiments, observations, measurements. **Secondary data** is data collected by someone else — the ABS, Bureau of Meteorology, school records, published reports. Primary data gives you control over what is collected and how; secondary data saves time and often covers larger populations. *Quick check:* You use Bureau of Meteorology rainfall data for a project. Is this primary or secondary data?

**3. Planning the collection.** Decide: What variable will you measure? Is it discrete or continuous? Who or what will you sample? How many observations do you need? How will you record the data? A plan should also consider **bias** — will your method systematically favour some values over others? *Quick check:* You survey only your friends about homework time. Why might this introduce bias?

**4. Collecting and organising data.** Record data in a table or spreadsheet with clear headings and units. Check for errors — values that seem impossible or entries that were accidentally duplicated. For large data sets, software (spreadsheets, online tools) speeds up both organisation and display. *Quick check:* You record a student's height as 17.2 m. What has likely gone wrong?

**5. Analysing the distribution.** Create an appropriate display (dot plot, stem-and-leaf plot, histogram). Describe: **shape** (symmetric, skewed, clusters, gaps), **centre** (mean, median, mode), **spread** (range, outliers). Calculate summary statistics. Look for patterns and surprises. *Quick check:* A distribution of test scores has a cluster around 70–80 with a gap at 50–60 and one score at 15. Describe the shape.

**6. Worked example — investigating travel time to school.**
A Year 7 class wants to know how long students take to travel to school.

*Follow the thinking:*
- **Question:** "What is the distribution of travel times (in minutes) for Year 7 students at our school?"
- **Plan:** Survey all Year 7 students. Variable: travel time (continuous, measured in minutes). Record in a spreadsheet.
- **Collect:** 30 responses: 5, 8, 10, 10, 12, 12, 15, 15, 15, 15, 18, 20, 20, 20, 22, 25, 25, 25, 28, 30, 30, 30, 35, 35, 40, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60.
- **Analyse:** Stem-and-leaf plot created. Median = 24 min (average of 22 and 25). Mean = 25.3 min. Mode = 15 min. Range = 60 − 5 = 55 min. Shape: positively skewed — most students travel 10–30 min, with a tail of longer commutes up to 60 min.
- **Report:** "Most Year 7 students travel between 10 and 30 minutes. The median travel time is 24 minutes. The distribution is positively skewed, with a few students travelling 45–60 minutes. These longer travel times pull the mean (25.3 min) above the median."

**7. Reporting findings.** A good report connects the analysis back to the question. It includes: the question, a description of the data source and method, an appropriate display, summary statistics, a written interpretation of shape and centre, and any limitations (e.g. "Our sample was only one class — results may differ across the whole school"). *Quick check:* Why should a report mention limitations?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS Census provides secondary data on Victorian communities — population, household size, income, transport. CensusAtSchool Australia runs student data-collection projects. The Bureau of Meteorology and VicHealth publish data sets ideal for secondary-source investigations. ACMI supports digital data-literacy programs.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations communities and organisations such as the Lowitja Institute emphasise the importance of community control over data collection and reporting — often called **data sovereignty**. When using data about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, consider who collected it, for what purpose, and whether the community was involved. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students can investigate questions about their diverse school communities — languages spoken, countries of heritage, food preferences — using primary data collection that respects cultural sensitivity and consent.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to know whether Year 7 students spend more time on homework or on screens after school. Plan a statistical investigation: write the question, decide on a primary or secondary source, and describe how you would collect and display the data.
2. **Civic.** Hobsons Bay City Council wants to know how residents travel to the local shopping centre. Design an investigation using a survey. Identify one source of bias and how you would reduce it.
3. **Vocational.** A café owner wants to predict how many iced coffees to make each day in summer. Suggest a secondary data source (e.g. past sales records and temperature data) and describe how the owner could analyse the relationship.
4. **Ethical.** A school survey asks students to report their exam marks. Some students may exaggerate. Discuss how this could bias the data and suggest a way to get more accurate results.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Design an investigation to compare the number of languages spoken at home across two Victorian schools — one in inner Melbourne and one in regional Victoria. State your question, data source (primary or secondary), and how you would report the findings.

## Memorable takeaways
- The cycle: question → plan → collect → analyse → report.
- Primary data: you collect it. Secondary data: someone else collected it.
- Check for bias in your method and errors in your data.
- Report shape, centre and spread — then connect them back to the question.
- Always state limitations honestly.

## Stretch yourself
Design and carry out a statistical investigation to answer the question: "Do students who live further from school spend more time travelling?" Collect primary data from at least 15 students (distance and travel time). Create an appropriate display for each variable. Calculate summary statistics for both. Write a report that includes your question, method, displays, summary statistics, interpretation of the distributions, and limitations of your investigation. Discuss whether your findings support the idea that distance and travel time are related. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Vague questions.** "Is homework bad?" is not a statistical question. Specify what you will measure, for whom, and how.
- **Convenience sampling bias.** Surveying only friends or only one class may not represent the whole population.
- **Confusing correlation with causation.** If two variables move together, it does not prove one causes the other — there may be other factors.
- **Ignoring limitations.** Every investigation has limitations. Acknowledging them strengthens your report, not weakens it.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Statistical question.* "What are the daily maximum temperatures in Melbourne during January?" — this expects variability across days.
2. *BoM rainfall data.* Secondary data — someone else collected it.
3. *Surveying only friends.* Your friends may have similar habits, so the sample may not represent the whole year level.
4. *Height of 17.2 m.* A recording error — likely 1.72 m (the decimal point is in the wrong place).
5. *Test score distribution.* Negatively skewed or multimodal — a main cluster at 70–80, a gap at 50–60, and a low outlier at 15.
6. *Travel time investigation.* Question, method, display, statistics and interpretation all provided in the worked example.
7. *Why mention limitations?* So the reader knows how far the findings can be generalised and where caution is needed.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Homework vs screen time.* Primary source: survey asking for estimated minutes on each. Display: paired dot plots or back-to-back stem-and-leaf. Compare medians and shapes.
2. *Council survey.* Possible bias: surveying only during work hours misses commuters. Reduce by surveying at multiple times across the week.
3. *Iced coffee prediction.* Past sales records (secondary) paired with BoM temperature data. Plot sales against temperature; look for a pattern.
4. *Exam mark exaggeration.* Self-report bias inflates scores. Use school records (secondary data) instead, or make the survey anonymous.
5. *Languages comparison.* Question: "How does the number of languages spoken at home compare between School A and School B?" Primary: survey both schools. Report: compare medians, shapes and spreads using dot plots or stem-and-leaf.
