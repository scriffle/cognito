# VC2M7N06 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7N06. Use the 4 operations with positive rational numbers, including fractions and decimals, to solve problems using efficient mental and written calculation strategies.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Number).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The four operations — addition, subtraction, multiplication and division — work together to solve multi-step problems.
  - Fractions, decimals and whole numbers are all rational numbers and follow the same rules.
  - Choosing the right operation and the most efficient strategy is as important as getting the arithmetic right.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Numeracy; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **combine addition, subtraction, multiplication and division with fractions and decimals** to solve problems that need more than one step.
- You are learning to choose efficient strategies — mental, written or digital — depending on the complexity of the numbers.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify which operations a problem requires and in what order.
- Apply order of operations (BODMAS/BIDMAS) correctly with fractions and decimals.
- Solve multi-step problems involving a mix of whole numbers, fractions and decimals.
- Estimate answers before calculating and check results for reasonableness.

## Core content

**1. Positive rational numbers.** A **rational number** is any number that can be written as a fraction a/b where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. This includes whole numbers (5 = 5/1), fractions (3/4) and terminating or repeating decimals (0.75, 0.333…). At Level 7, you work with positive rational numbers. *Quick check:* Is 2.5 a rational number? Write it as a fraction.

**2. Adding and subtracting fractions (review).** Find a **common denominator**, convert, then add or subtract the numerators. 2/3 + 1/4: common denominator 12 → 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12. For decimals, line up the decimal points and add column by column. *Quick check:* Calculate 3/5 − 1/4.

**3. Order of operations with rational numbers.** **BODMAS** (Brackets, Orders, Division and Multiplication left to right, Addition and Subtraction left to right) applies to fractions and decimals exactly as it does to whole numbers. In 1/2 + 3/4 × 2/3, do the multiplication first: 3/4 × 2/3 = 1/2. Then add: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. *Quick check:* Calculate 0.5 + 0.25 × 4.

**4. Multi-step problems — choosing operations.** Read the problem. Identify the quantities and what you need to find. Decide which operations to use and in what order. Write the expression, then calculate step by step. A good habit: estimate first, calculate second, check third. *Quick check:* "A student buys 2.5 kg of apples at $3.80 per kg and pays with a $20 note." What two operations do you need to find the change?

**5. Mixed fractions and decimals in one problem.** Sometimes a problem mixes fractions and decimals. Convert to a common form — usually decimals for money and measurement, fractions when exact values matter. For example, ½ of $7.60 can be done as 0.5 × 7.60 = $3.80, or as 7.60 ÷ 2 = $3.80. *Quick check:* Calculate ¾ of $12.40 using a decimal method.

**6. Worked example — school fundraiser.**
A school sells 150 sausages at $2.50 each and 80 drinks at $1.75 each. Ingredients cost $185.50 and drinks cost $52.00.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Sausage income: 150 × $2.50 = $375.00.
- Drink income: 80 × $1.75 = $140.00.
- Total income: $375.00 + $140.00 = $515.00.
- Total costs: $185.50 + $52.00 = $237.50.
- Profit: $515.00 − $237.50 = $277.50.
- Profit per item sold (230 items): $277.50 ÷ 230 ≈ $1.21 per item.

**7. Estimation and reasonableness.** Before calculating, round to friendly numbers. 150 × $2.50 ≈ 150 × $2.50 = $375 (exact here). 80 × $1.75 ≈ 80 × $2 = $160 (actual is $140 — close enough to confirm the calculation is in the right range). If your final answer is wildly different from the estimate, recheck. *Quick check:* Estimate 4.9 × 3.1 mentally, then calculate exactly. How close is your estimate?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You have $25.00. You buy a sandwich for $6.75 and a drink for $3.50. Your friend owes you 1/4 of a $12.00 movie ticket. After buying lunch and receiving the money, how much do you have?
2. **Civic.** The City of Whittlesea budgets $840 000 for three community programs. Program A gets 2/5, Program B gets 1/3, and Program C gets the rest. Calculate each program's share in dollars. Check that the three amounts add to $840 000.
3. **Vocational.** A baker in Geelong makes 12 batches of bread. Each batch uses 1.75 kg of flour and 0.35 kg of butter. Flour costs $2.40/kg and butter costs $8.50/kg. Calculate the total ingredient cost for all 12 batches.
4. **Ethical.** Two students split a bill of $47.50. One ate 3/5 of the food. Should they split equally or proportionally? Calculate both ways and discuss which is fairer.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A multicultural food stall at a school fair sells dumplings ($1.20 each), empanadas ($2.00 each) and samosas ($1.50 each). They sell 45 dumplings, 30 empanadas and 60 samosas. Ingredients cost 2/5 of total revenue. Calculate total revenue and total profit.

## Memorable takeaways
- The four operations work the same way for fractions and decimals as for whole numbers.
- BODMAS applies — multiplication and division before addition and subtraction.
- Convert to a common form (all fractions or all decimals) when a problem mixes both.
- Estimate first, calculate second, check third.
- Multi-step problems are just chains of single-step problems — identify each step before you start.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Ignoring BODMAS with fractions.** In 1/2 + 1/3 × 3/4, you must multiply first (1/3 × 3/4 = 1/4), then add (1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4). Doing the addition first gives the wrong answer.
- **Rounding too early.** In multi-step problems, keep full precision until the final step. Rounding partway through compounds the error.
- **Forgetting to check reasonableness.** If a lunch bill comes out to $347.50 when you spent about $15, something is wrong. Always compare with your estimate.
- **Mixing up "of" and "off."** "1/4 of $20" means 1/4 × $20 = $5. "1/4 off $20" means $20 − $5 = $15. The words matter.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Is 2.5 rational?* Yes. 2.5 = 5/2.
2. *3/5 − 1/4.* Common denominator 20: 12/20 − 5/20 = 7/20.
3. *0.5 + 0.25 × 4.* Multiply first: 0.25 × 4 = 1. Then add: 0.5 + 1 = 1.5.
4. *Apple change.* Multiplication (2.5 × $3.80 = $9.50) then subtraction ($20 − $9.50 = $10.50).
5. *¾ of $12.40.* 0.75 × 12.40 = $9.30.
6. *Estimate 4.9 × 3.1.* Estimate: 5 × 3 = 15. Exact: 15.19. Very close.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Lunch and movie money.* Spend: $6.75 + $3.50 = $10.25. Receive: 1/4 × $12.00 = $3.00. Balance: $25.00 − $10.25 + $3.00 = $17.75.
2. *Whittlesea programs.* A: 2/5 × $840 000 = $336 000. B: 1/3 × $840 000 = $280 000. C: $840 000 − $336 000 − $280 000 = $224 000. Check: $336 000 + $280 000 + $224 000 = $840 000.
3. *Geelong baker.* Flour per batch: 1.75 × $2.40 = $4.20. Butter per batch: 0.35 × $8.50 = $2.975. Per batch: $7.175. Total (12 batches): 12 × $7.175 = $86.10.
4. *Bill split.* Equal: $47.50 ÷ 2 = $23.75 each. Proportional: 3/5 × $47.50 = $28.50 and 2/5 × $47.50 = $19.00. Proportional is fairer if portions were unequal.
5. *Food stall.* Revenue: 45 × $1.20 + 30 × $2.00 + 60 × $1.50 = $54 + $60 + $90 = $204. Costs: 2/5 × $204 = $81.60. Profit: $204 − $81.60 = $122.40.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Accommodation: 28 × $18.50 × 3 = $1554. Meals: 28 × $12.75 × 3 = $1071. School subsidy: 1/3 × $1554 = $518. Net accommodation: $1036. Net meals: $1071 − $485.60 = $585.40. Total student costs: $1036 + $585.40 = $1621.40. Per student (28): $1621.40 ÷ 28 = $57.91 (rounded). With 3 unable to pay: total still $1621.40, split among 25 students: $1621.40 ÷ 25 = $64.86 (rounded).
