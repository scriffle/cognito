# VC2M8P01 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Recognise that complementary events have a combined probability of one; use this relationship to calculate probabilities in applied contexts.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Complementary events cover all outcomes — one must happen. P(A) + P(A′) = 1. Using complements is often the most efficient calculation strategy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that **complementary events** have a combined probability of **one**.
- You should be able to use *P(A′) = 1 − P(A)* to calculate probabilities efficiently.

## Core content

Two events are **complementary** if exactly one must occur — they cover all outcomes with no overlap. "Rain" and "no rain" are complementary. "Rolling a 6" and "not rolling a 6" are complementary.

The **complement rule**: *P(A′) = 1 − P(A)*. This works because all probabilities in a sample space sum to 1.

**Why complements help.** Sometimes it is easier to calculate what you do *not* want. P(at least one head in 3 tosses) = 1 − P(no heads) = 1 − (1/2)³ = 7/8. The complement "none" is one calculation; "at least one" would require counting many cases.

**Key language pairs.** "At least one" ↔ complement "none." "Not all" ↔ complement "all." "Fewer than 3" ↔ complement "3 or more."

**Worked example — Melbourne weather.** P(rain) = 0.4. P(no rain) = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6. A school uses this: 60% dry is acceptable for an excursion with an indoor backup. *Follow the thinking:* identify event → apply complement rule → interpret in context.

**Applied contexts.** Quality control: 2% defective → 98% non-defective. Medical: 95% accurate → 5% incorrect. At scale, small complementary probabilities still matter.

**Cultural context.** Aboriginal knowledge systems assess likelihood through environmental indicators — if certain birds arrive, rain is highly likely; if not, unlikely. This is complementary reasoning applied to ecology. Bureau of Meteorology forecasts, AFL win probabilities and quality-control data all use complementary probability daily.

## Memorable takeaways
- Complementary events are exhaustive and mutually exclusive.
- P(A) + P(A′) = 1, always.
- Use complements for "at least one" problems — calculate "none" and subtract from 1.
- Small complementary probabilities matter at scale.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Any two events sum to 1." Only complementary pairs do.
- "P(A) + P(B) = 1 for any A, B." Only if B = A′.
- "Small probabilities don't matter." At thousands of trials, even 1% is significant.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Even and odd on a die — complementary?* Yes — every roll is one or the other, no overlap.
- *P(no rain) if P(rain) = 0.35.* 0.65.
- *P(not a heart) from a deck.* 1 − 1/4 = 3/4.
- *500 items, 3% defect rate.* 500 × 0.97 = 485 non-defective.
- *At least one head in 3 tosses.* 1 − (1/2)³ = 7/8.
