# VC2M7A03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7A03. Solve one-variable linear equations of increasing complexity with natural number solutions; verify equation solutions by substitution.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A linear equation is a statement that two expressions are equal.
  - Solving means finding the value of the variable that makes the equation true.
  - Substitution is the check — plug the answer back in to verify.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Sustainability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **solve one-variable linear equations** with increasing complexity, where the solutions are **natural numbers** (1, 2, 3, …).
- You are learning to **verify** your solution by substituting it back into the original equation.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Recognise a linear equation and identify the unknown.
- Use inverse operations to isolate the variable.
- Solve equations involving multiple steps.
- Verify every solution by substitution.

## Core content

**1. What is a linear equation?** An **equation** says two things are equal: *3x + 5 = 20*. It is **linear** because the variable (*x*) has no power higher than 1. A **solution** is the value that makes both sides equal. *Quick check:* Is *x² + 1 = 10* a linear equation? Why or why not?

**2. One-step equations.** Use the **inverse operation** to isolate the variable. *x + 7 = 12* → subtract 7 from both sides → *x = 5*. *4n = 28* → divide both sides by 4 → *n = 7*. *Quick check:* Solve *m − 9 = 15*.

**3. Two-step equations.** Work outward from the variable. *2x + 3 = 17* → subtract 3 → *2x = 14* → divide by 2 → *x = 7*. Think of undoing the operations in reverse order. *Quick check:* Solve *5y − 4 = 31*.

**4. Equations with brackets.** Expand first, then solve. *3(n + 2) = 21* → *3n + 6 = 21* → *3n = 15* → *n = 5*. Alternatively, divide both sides by 3 first: *n + 2 = 7* → *n = 5*. Either method works. *Quick check:* Solve *4(x − 3) = 20* using both methods.

**5. Equations with variables on one side and multiple operations.** *2(3x + 1) + 4 = 30* → *6x + 2 + 4 = 30* → *6x + 6 = 30* → *6x = 24* → *x = 4*. Take it step by step — there is no need to rush. *Quick check:* Solve *3(2m − 1) + 5 = 20*.

**6. Worked example — Melbourne Aquarium tickets.** A school group visits SEA LIFE Melbourne Aquarium. Student tickets cost $n each. The school buys 24 tickets and pays a $30 booking fee. The total bill is $582. Equation: *24n + 30 = 582*. Solve: *24n = 552* → *n = 23*. Each ticket costs $23. *Follow the thinking:* translate the problem → write the equation → isolate the variable → answer in context.

**7. Verifying by substitution.** Always substitute your answer back. For the aquarium: *24(23) + 30 = 552 + 30 = 582*. It matches the total, so *n = 23* is correct. If the check fails, re-do your working. *Quick check:* Why should you verify every solution, even when you feel confident?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** SEA LIFE Melbourne Aquarium, Melbourne Zoo entry pricing, PTV myki fare calculations and AFL match-day ticket bundles all generate one-variable equations when you know the total and need to find a per-unit cost.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung and Bunurong/Boon Wurrung seasonal calendars encoded timing relationships — when certain plants flowered, certain fish ran, and certain ceremonies were held. While not expressed in Western algebraic notation, these are quantitative relationships with knowns and unknowns. AIATSIS publishes research on Indigenous systems of measurement and reasoning. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students from diverse backgrounds may bring different methods for solving equations — arithmetic reasoning, guess-and-check, bar models. All valid approaches can be connected to the formal inverse-operations method.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You save $15 per week and already have $40. After how many weeks will you have $160? Write and solve the equation.
2. **Civic.** Brimbank City Council hires a hall for *h* hours at $45 per hour plus a $60 cleaning fee. The invoice is $375. Solve for *h*.
3. **Vocational.** A baker makes *b* trays of lamingtons. Each tray holds 12. She adds 8 extra lamingtons for samples. Total lamingtons: 80. Write and solve the equation.
4. **Ethical.** A charity says "every $20 feeds one family." They raise $F and feed 35 families. Write the equation. Discuss whether this formula oversimplifies the charity's costs.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Koorie Heritage Trust workshop group splits into equal teams of *t* students, with 3 students left to help the facilitator. There are 27 students. If there are 4 teams, write and solve for *t*.

## Memorable takeaways
- An equation states that two expressions are equal.
- Inverse operations undo what was done to the variable.
- Work step by step — subtract or add first, then multiply or divide.
- Always verify by substitution.
- Natural number solutions are positive whole numbers.

## Stretch yourself
A community sports club in Werribee charges a seasonal registration fee of $R plus $12 per match. A player pays $204 for a season of 15 matches. Write and solve the equation to find *R*. The club then adds a $25 equipment levy. Write a new equation for the total cost *C* in terms of matches *m* and find how many matches a player can attend for $300 (with the levy and the same registration fee). Show all steps, name the inverse operations used, and verify both answers by substitution. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Do the same operation twice." If you subtract 3, then subtract again, you have gone too far. Each inverse operation is used once to undo its partner.
- "Forget to do both sides." Whatever you do to one side of the equation, you must do to the other.
- "Skip the check." Verification catches arithmetic errors before they reach your final answer.
- "Natural numbers include zero." In this context, natural numbers are 1, 2, 3, … (positive whole numbers).

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Is x² + 1 = 10 linear?* No — the variable is squared, so it is quadratic.
2. *Solve m − 9 = 15.* Add 9: *m = 24*.
3. *Solve 5y − 4 = 31.* Add 4: *5y = 35*. Divide by 5: *y = 7*.
4. *Solve 4(x − 3) = 20.* Expand: *4x − 12 = 20 → 4x = 32 → x = 8*. Or divide first: *x − 3 = 5 → x = 8*.
5. *Solve 3(2m − 1) + 5 = 20.* Expand: *6m − 3 + 5 = 20 → 6m + 2 = 20 → 6m = 18 → m = 3*.
6. *Why verify?* Substitution catches arithmetic errors and confirms the solution satisfies the original equation.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Savings.* *15w + 40 = 160 → 15w = 120 → w = 8* weeks. Check: 15(8) + 40 = 160.
2. *Hall hire.* *45h + 60 = 375 → 45h = 315 → h = 7* hours.
3. *Lamingtons.* *12b + 8 = 80 → 12b = 72 → b = 6* trays.
4. *Charity.* *20 × 35 = F → F = $700*. Discussion: costs include logistics, staff and distribution, so the $20-per-family figure is a simplification.
5. *Workshop teams.* *4t + 3 = 27 → 4t = 24 → t = 6* students per team.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Registration: *R + 12(15) = 204 → R + 180 = 204 → R = 24*. Check: 24 + 180 = 204. With levy: *C = 24 + 12m + 25 = 49 + 12m*. For $300: *49 + 12m = 300 → 12m = 251 → m = 20.9…* — not a whole number, so the player can attend 20 matches (paying $289) with $11 left over. Inverse operations named; both answers verified.
