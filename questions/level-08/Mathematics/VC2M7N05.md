# VC2M7N05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7N05. Multiply and divide fractions and decimals using efficient mental and written strategies, and digital tools.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Number).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Multiplying fractions means finding a fraction of a fraction.
  - Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal.
  - Decimals are fractions written in base-ten disguise — the same rules apply.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Numeracy; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **multiply and divide fractions and decimals** using strategies that are efficient, not just correct.
- You are learning to choose between mental shortcuts, written methods and digital tools depending on the numbers involved.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Multiply fractions by fractions, whole numbers by fractions, and decimals by decimals.
- Divide fractions by fractions and decimals by decimals.
- Simplify before multiplying to keep numbers manageable.
- Estimate answers mentally and verify with a calculator when needed.

## Core content

**1. Multiplying fractions — the rule.** Multiply the numerators together and the denominators together: a/b × c/d = (a × c) / (b × d). For example, 2/3 × 4/5 = 8/15. *Quick check:* Calculate 3/4 × 2/7.

**2. Simplifying before you multiply.** Cross-cancel common factors first to keep numbers small. In 4/9 × 3/8, the 4 and 8 share a factor of 4, and the 3 and 9 share a factor of 3. Cancel: 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6. *Quick check:* Simplify and then multiply 5/6 × 3/10.

**3. Multiplying mixed numbers.** Convert to improper fractions first. 2½ × 1⅓ becomes 5/2 × 4/3 = 20/6 = 10/3 = 3⅓. *Quick check:* Calculate 1¾ × 2⅖.

**4. Dividing fractions — keep, flip, multiply.** To divide by a fraction, multiply by its **reciprocal** (flip the second fraction). 3/4 ÷ 2/5 becomes 3/4 × 5/2 = 15/8 = 1⅞. Why does this work? Dividing asks "how many groups of 2/5 fit in 3/4?" — and multiplying by the reciprocal answers that question. *Quick check:* Calculate 5/6 ÷ 1/3.

**5. Multiplying decimals.** Ignore the decimal points and multiply as whole numbers. Then count the total decimal places in both factors and place the point in the answer. 0.4 × 0.3: multiply 4 × 3 = 12; two decimal places total, so 0.12. For larger calculations, estimate first: 2.5 × 3.4 ≈ 2.5 × 3 = 7.5, then calculate exactly: 8.50. *Quick check:* Calculate 1.2 × 0.05.

**6. Dividing decimals.** Make the divisor a whole number by multiplying both numbers by the same power of ten. 3.6 ÷ 0.4: multiply both by 10 to get 36 ÷ 4 = 9. For 0.72 ÷ 0.08: multiply both by 100 to get 72 ÷ 8 = 9. *Quick check:* Calculate 4.5 ÷ 0.15.

**7. Worked example — scaling a recipe.**
A recipe serves 4 and needs 2¾ cups of flour. You want to serve 6 — that is 6/4 = 3/2 = 1½ times the recipe.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Convert 2¾ to an improper fraction: 11/4.
- Multiply: 11/4 × 3/2 = 33/8 = 4⅛ cups.
- Decimal check: 2.75 × 1.5 = 4.125 cups. Matches.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Queen Victoria Market sells produce by fractional and decimal quantities — half a kilogram of cheese, 1.5 kg of tomatoes. CSIRO's food-science division uses fractional scaling when adjusting commercial recipes. The Victorian Curriculum explicitly links fraction fluency to financial literacy — understanding GST at 1/11 of the total price.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Sharing and distribution are central to many First Nations social systems. When Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung or Yorta Yorta communities share a harvest — dividing a catch of fish equally among families — this is practical fraction work: dividing a whole into equal parts and reasoning about what each group receives. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural kitchens across Melbourne scale recipes daily — doubling a banh mi filling, halving a curry paste quantity. Market traders in Footscray and Springvale calculate prices per fraction of a kilogram. Fraction and decimal fluency is a shared practical skill across all communities.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A pizza is cut into 8 slices. You eat 3/8 and your friend eats 2/8. You then split one remaining slice equally. What fraction of the whole pizza does each of you end up eating? Show your multiplication and addition.
2. **Civic.** The City of Darebin allocates 3/5 of its parks budget to maintenance. Of that maintenance share, 2/3 goes to mowing. What fraction of the total budget goes to mowing? If the total budget is $450 000, how much is that?
3. **Vocational.** A carpenter in Bendigo needs 4.8 m of timber cut into pieces each 0.6 m long. How many pieces can she cut? Write it as a decimal division.
4. **Ethical.** A student uses a calculator for every fraction question and gets 100%. Another student does them mentally and gets 90%. Discuss whether efficiency is just about getting the right answer, and identify one benefit of mental strategies.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A school fundraiser sells samosas at $1.50 each and spring rolls at $0.75 each. If 2/3 of the 120 items sold are samosas and the rest are spring rolls, calculate the total revenue using both fraction and decimal methods.

## Memorable takeaways
- Multiply fractions: tops × tops, bottoms × bottoms.
- Divide fractions: keep the first, flip the second, multiply.
- Cross-cancel before multiplying to keep numbers small.
- For decimals: count decimal places (multiplication) or equalise the divisor (division).
- Always estimate first — it catches place-value errors.

## Stretch yourself
A community café in Collingwood orders supplies weekly. This week they need 3½ kg of flour at $2.40 per kg, 1¾ litres of olive oil at $12.80 per litre and 2⅔ kg of sugar at $1.95 per kg. Calculate the cost of each item using fraction-to-decimal conversion and multiplication. Find the total cost. If the café gets a 15% discount (multiply by 0.85), calculate the discounted total. Show all working with both fraction and decimal methods where possible. Verify your final answer with estimation. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Adding instead of multiplying.** "2/3 of 3/4" means multiply, not add. The word "of" signals multiplication.
- **Forgetting to flip when dividing.** You flip the second fraction (the divisor), not the first. 3/4 ÷ 2/5 = 3/4 × 5/2.
- **Misplacing the decimal point.** Always estimate first. If 2.5 × 3.4 gives you 85 instead of 8.5, the estimate (≈7.5) catches the error.
- **Not simplifying the final answer.** 8/12 should be written as 2/3. Check for common factors.

## Try drawing this
An area model for 2/3 × 3/4: draw a rectangle, divide it into 3 equal rows and shade 2 of them. Then divide the same rectangle into 4 equal columns and shade 3 of them. The overlap (double-shaded region) shows the answer — count the overlapping parts out of the total small rectangles.
