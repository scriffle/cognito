## Core content

**1. Rational numbers in context.** A **rational number** is any number that can be written as a fraction *a/b* (where *b* ≠ 0). This includes decimals that terminate or repeat and all percentages. Financial contexts use all three forms: 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%. *Quick check:* Convert 3/8 to a decimal and a percentage.

**2. Percentage of an amount.** To find 15% of $240: multiply 240 × 0.15 = $36. Mental shortcut: 10% = $24, 5% = $12, so 15% = $36. *Quick check:* Find 12.5% of $480 using a mental strategy.

**3. Percentage increase (profit, markup, GST).** **Profit** = selling price − cost price. **Percentage profit** = (profit ÷ cost price) × 100. To increase by 20%: multiply by 1.20. GST in Australia is 10%: multiply by 1.10. *Quick check:* A shirt costs $40 wholesale. The retailer marks it up 60%. What is the selling price?

**4. Percentage decrease (loss, discount).** **Loss** = cost price − selling price (when sold below cost). **Discount**: to decrease by 25%, multiply by 0.75 (or 1 − 0.25). *Quick check:* A $120 pair of shoes is discounted by 30%. What is the sale price?

**5. Successive percentages.** A 10% increase followed by a 10% decrease does **not** return to the original. $100 → +10% → $110 → −10% → $99. The multipliers compound: 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99. *Quick check:* Why does a 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease not give the original price?

**6. Worked example — Queen Victoria Market stall.** A stallholder buys avocados in bulk at $2.40 each. She sells them for $4.00 each. On Friday afternoon she discounts unsold stock by 25%. Profit per avocado at full price: $4.00 − $2.40 = $1.60 (66.7% markup). Discounted price: $4.00 × 0.75 = $3.00. Profit at discount: $3.00 − $2.40 = $0.60 (25% markup). If she sells 80 at full price and 20 at discount: total profit = 80 × $1.60 + 20 × $0.60 = $128 + $12 = $140. *Follow the thinking:* identify cost and selling prices → calculate profit per unit → apply discount correctly → aggregate and interpret.

**7. Reviewing the model.** Ask: Does this model account for wastage (unsold avocados that spoil)? Does it include stall fees? Are percentages applied to the right base? A common error is calculating percentage profit on the selling price instead of the cost price. Always state assumptions and limitations. *Quick check:* Why does applying a percentage to the wrong base give a misleading answer?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A pair of headphones is marked $89.99 in a 20%-off sale. Calculate the discount and the sale price. Then add 10% GST to the sale price. What do you actually pay?
2. **Civic.** Yarra City Council increases rates by 3.5% this year. A household currently pays $1 800. Calculate the new rate and the dollar increase. Discuss whether this is affordable for pensioners.
3. **Vocational.** A baker buys flour at $1.20 per kg and uses 0.5 kg per loaf. Other ingredients cost $0.80 per loaf. She sells each loaf for $6.50. Calculate her percentage profit per loaf.
4. **Ethical.** A store advertises "up to 70% off" but only applies 70% to one item while most items are 10–20% off. Discuss whether the advertisement is ethical and how consumers can use percentage skills to verify.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Gunaikurnai tourism enterprise charges $35 per person for a cultural walk. Costs are $400 per session. If 15 people attend, calculate the profit and the percentage profit on costs. Then model what happens if only 8 attend.

## Memorable takeaways
- Percentage increase: multiply by (1 + rate). Decrease: multiply by (1 − rate).
- Profit = selling price − cost price. Percentage profit is on the cost price.
- Successive percentage changes compound — they do not simply add or cancel.
- Choose mental, written or digital strategies based on complexity.
- Always review: is the percentage applied to the correct base?

## Watch out for these traps
- "Percentage profit is on the selling price." It is calculated on the cost price unless explicitly stated otherwise.
- "A 20% increase then 20% decrease returns to the original." It does not — successive percentages compound (1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96, a 4% loss).
- "GST is added to the original price." GST is 10% of the pre-GST price; the total is 110% of the pre-GST price.
- "50% off then 20% off = 70% off." No: 0.50 × 0.80 = 0.40, meaning 60% off the original, not 70%.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *3/8 as decimal and percentage.* 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 = 37.5%.
2. *12.5% of $480.* 12.5% = 1/8. $480 ÷ 8 = $60.
3. *$40 marked up 60%.* $40 × 1.60 = $64.
4. *$120 shoes, 30% off.* $120 × 0.70 = $84.
5. *20% up then 20% down.* 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96. Result is 96% of original — a 4% loss. The decrease is applied to the larger amount.
6. *Wrong base.* Percentage profit on selling price gives a smaller figure than on cost price, making the profit look lower than it actually is (or vice versa). It distorts comparisons.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Headphones.* Discount: $89.99 × 0.20 = $18.00. Sale price: $72.00 (rounded). Plus GST: $72.00 × 1.10 = $79.20.
2. *Council rates.* New rate: $1 800 × 1.035 = $1 863. Increase: $63. Discussion: even small percentages add up on fixed incomes.
3. *Baker's profit.* Cost per loaf: $0.60 + $0.80 = $1.40. Profit: $6.50 − $1.40 = $5.10. Percentage profit: (5.10 ÷ 1.40) × 100 ≈ 364%.
4. *"Up to 70% off."* The phrase is technically true if at least one item is 70% off, but misleads consumers expecting widespread discounts. Percentage literacy lets consumers verify each item individually.
5. *Cultural walk.* Revenue: 15 × $35 = $525. Profit: $525 − $400 = $125. Percentage profit: (125 ÷ 400) × 100 = 31.25%. At 8 people: Revenue = $280. Loss = $120. Percentage loss: 30%.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Full-price revenue: 70 × $7 = $490. Discounted price: $7 × 0.70 = $4.90. Discounted revenue: 20 × $4.90 = $98. Total revenue: $588. Outlay: $350. Profit: $238. Percentage profit: (238 ÷ 350) × 100 = 68%. Assumption: unsold stock is a total loss ($35 worth). If donated at cost and tax-deductible (say 30% tax rate): saves $35 × 0.30 = $10.50, effective loss reduced to $24.50. Model limitation: does not account for labour or stall costs.
