# VC2M8P01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8P01. Recognise that complementary events have a combined probability of one; use this relationship to calculate probabilities in applied contexts.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - If an event has probability *P(A)*, its complement has probability *1 − P(A)*.
  - Complementary events cover all possible outcomes — one or the other must happen.
  - Using complements is often the most efficient way to calculate a probability.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that **complementary events** have a combined probability of **one**.
- You are learning to use this relationship to **calculate probabilities** efficiently in applied contexts.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define complementary events and give examples.
- State the complement rule: *P(A′) = 1 − P(A)*.
- Use the complement to find a probability when the direct calculation is harder.
- Apply the concept to real-world scenarios.

## Core content

**1. What are complementary events?** Two events are **complementary** if exactly one of them must occur — they cover all outcomes with no overlap. "Raining" and "not raining" are complementary. "Rolling a 6" and "not rolling a 6" are complementary. *Quick check:* Are "rolling an even number" and "rolling an odd number" complementary on a standard die? Why?

**2. The complement rule.** If *P(A)* is the probability of event *A*, then *P(not A) = 1 − P(A)*. This works because all probabilities in a sample space sum to 1. *Quick check:* If the probability of rain tomorrow is 0.35, what is the probability of no rain?

**3. Why complements are useful.** Sometimes it is easier to calculate the probability of what you do **not** want and subtract from 1. Example: probability of getting at least one head in three coin tosses. Direct: count all outcomes with at least one head. Complement: *P(no heads) = (1/2)³ = 1/8*. So *P(at least one head) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8*. *Quick check:* Why is the complement method easier here?

**4. Identifying complements.** Key language: "at least one" → complement is "none." "Not all" → complement is "all." "Fewer than 3" → complement is "3 or more." Recognising these pairs helps you set up calculations efficiently. *Quick check:* What is the complement of "all students pass the test"?

**5. Probabilities as fractions, decimals and percentages.** A probability can be expressed any way: *P(A) = 3/5 = 0.6 = 60%*. The complement: *P(A′) = 2/5 = 0.4 = 40%*. They always sum to 1 (or 100%). *Quick check:* If *P(A) = 0.72*, express *P(A′)* as a fraction.

**6. Worked example — Melbourne weather forecast.** The Bureau of Meteorology says the probability of rain in Melbourne tomorrow is 0.4. A school plans an outdoor excursion. What is the probability of no rain? *P(no rain) = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6*. The school uses this to decide: 60% chance of dry weather is acceptable for an excursion with an indoor backup plan. *Follow the thinking:* identify the event and its complement → apply *P(A′) = 1 − P(A)* → interpret in context.

**7. Common applied contexts.** Quality control: if 2% of products are defective, *P(not defective) = 0.98*. Medical screening: if a test is 95% accurate, *P(incorrect result) = 0.05*. Sport: if a team wins 70% of home games, *P(not winning) = 0.30*. *Quick check:* A factory produces 500 items per day with a 3% defect rate. How many non-defective items are expected?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You draw a card from a standard 52-card deck. *P(heart) = 13/52 = 1/4*. Use the complement to find *P(not a heart)*.
2. **Civic.** Wyndham City Council reports that 85% of residents sort recycling correctly. What is the probability that a randomly chosen household does not sort correctly? If there are 60 000 households, how many are expected to sort incorrectly?
3. **Vocational.** A factory's quality-control data shows 4% of circuit boards fail testing. What is the probability that a randomly chosen board passes? In a batch of 250, how many are expected to pass?
4. **Ethical.** A medical test is 97% accurate (detects a condition when present). What is the probability of a false negative? Discuss why even a small complementary probability matters in health screening.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Yorta Yorta ranger observes that when river red gums flower early, there is a 0.8 probability of above-average spring rainfall. What is the probability of below-average rainfall given early flowering? How might this inform water management?

## Memorable takeaways
- Complementary events are mutually exclusive and exhaustive — one must occur.
- *P(A) + P(A′) = 1*, always.
- Use complements when "at least one" or "not all" appears — calculate what you do not want and subtract from 1.
- Probabilities can be fractions, decimals or percentages — they always sum to 1.
- Small complementary probabilities (defects, false negatives) still matter at scale.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Complementary means opposite." It means the event and its complement together cover all outcomes with no overlap — not just "opposite" in a vague sense.
- "P(A) + P(B) = 1 for any two events." Only for complementary events. Two arbitrary events may overlap or leave gaps.
- "0.97 accuracy means 0.97 chance of being correct for me." Accuracy rates apply to populations; individual results depend on additional factors (prevalence, specificity).
- "Small probabilities don't matter." At scale (thousands of products, millions of passengers), even 1% matters enormously.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Even and odd on a die — complementary?* Yes — every roll is either even or odd (covers all outcomes, no overlap).
2. *P(no rain).* 1 − 0.35 = 0.65.
3. *Why complement is easier for "at least one head."* Counting "at least one" requires listing many combinations (HHH, HHT, HTH, …). The complement "no heads" is just one outcome (TTT), making the calculation simpler.
4. *Complement of "all pass."* "At least one student does not pass."
5. *P(A′) when P(A) = 0.72.* P(A′) = 0.28 = 28/100 = 7/25.
6. *Non-defective items.* 500 × 0.97 = 485 items expected to be non-defective.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Not a heart.* P(not heart) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4.
2. *Recycling.* P(incorrect) = 0.15. Expected: 60 000 × 0.15 = 9 000 households.
3. *Circuit boards.* P(pass) = 0.96. Expected: 250 × 0.96 = 240 boards pass.
4. *Medical test.* P(false negative) = 0.03. Discussion: 3% sounds small, but if 10 000 people are tested, 300 cases are missed — significant for public health.
5. *Ranger observation.* P(below-average rainfall) = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2. Water managers use this: 80% confidence in good rain might mean less need for restrictions; the 20% complement means contingency planning is still required.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** P(on time one day) = 0.78. P(on time all 5 days) = 0.78⁵ ≈ 0.289. P(late at least once) = 1 − 0.289 ≈ 0.711 (71.1%). Interpretation: even with 78% daily reliability, there is a 71% chance of at least one late bus in a week. The "78% on-time" figure is misleading for weekly commuters. Assumption: days are independent — in reality, systemic issues (roadworks, weather) may cause correlated delays, making the actual probability of disruption even higher.
