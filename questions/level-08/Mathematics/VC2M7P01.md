# VC2M7P01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7P01. Identify the sample space for single-stage experiments; assign probabilities to the possible outcomes and predict relative frequencies for related experiments.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Probability).
- **Big ideas:**

  - Probability measures how likely an outcome is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **identify the sample space** for single-stage experiments, **assign probabilities** to outcomes, and **predict relative frequencies** for related experiments.
- You are learning to move between theoretical probability and what you expect to see in practice.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- List the sample space for a single-stage experiment.
- Assign a probability to each outcome using symmetry or equally likely reasoning.
- Express probability as a fraction, decimal and percentage.
- Predict the expected number of times an outcome will occur in a given number of trials.

## Core content

**1. What is a single-stage experiment?** A **single-stage experiment** (or trial) is an activity with a well-defined set of possible results performed once. Rolling a die, flipping a coin, spinning a spinner — each is a single stage. *Quick check:* Is drawing one card from a deck a single-stage experiment?

**2. Sample space.** The **sample space** (S) is the complete set of all possible outcomes. For a standard die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. For a coin: S = {Head, Tail}. Listing the sample space is always your first step. *Quick check:* What is the sample space for a spinner with four equal sectors coloured red, blue, green and yellow?

**3. Assigning probabilities.** If outcomes are **equally likely**, each has probability 1 ÷ (number of outcomes). For a fair die: P(any single number) = 1/6. Probabilities must satisfy two rules: each is between 0 and 1, and all probabilities in the sample space add to 1. *Quick check:* A bag has 3 red, 2 blue and 5 green marbles. What is P(blue)?

**4. Expressing probability in different forms.** P(blue) = 2/10 = 1/5 = 0.2 = 20%. All four forms say the same thing. Fractions are exact; decimals and percentages are often easier to compare. *Quick check:* Express P = 3/8 as a decimal and a percentage.

**5. Events (favourable outcomes).** An **event** is a subset of the sample space. "Rolling an even number" = {2, 4, 6}. P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2. Count the favourable outcomes and divide by total outcomes. *Quick check:* What is P(rolling a number greater than 4) on a fair die?

**6. Worked example: predicting relative frequency.**

A fair six-sided die is rolled 300 times. Predict how many times you expect to roll a 3.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Sample space: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. All equally likely.
- P(3) = 1/6.
- Predicted (expected) frequency = P(3) × number of trials = 1/6 × 300 = 50.
- Interpretation: we expect about 50 threes, though the actual count will vary due to randomness.

**7. Probability scale and language.** 0 = impossible. Between 0 and 0.5 = unlikely. 0.5 = even chance. Between 0.5 and 1 = likely. 1 = certain. Placing events on this scale builds intuition. *Quick check:* Where on the probability scale would you place "it will rain somewhere in Victoria tomorrow in winter"?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Melbourne Cup (Flemington) introduces millions of Australians to probability language — odds, chances, favourites. The State Library of Victoria holds historical lottery records showing early probability thinking. Melbourne weather forecasts from the Bureau of Meteorology state "70% chance of rain" — a direct probability communication.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Traditional games involving chance — such as the Warlpiri seed game or Koolchee (throwing games) — demonstrate that First Nations cultures engaged with randomness and fairness concepts long before European contact. These contexts can enrich classroom discussion about what "fair" means. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Dice and probability games exist across cultures — Korean yut, Indian pachisi, Chinese sic bo. Melbourne's diverse classrooms can explore how different cultures formalised chance.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You spin a spinner with 5 equal sectors numbered 1–5. List the sample space. What is P(landing on an odd number)? If you spin 100 times, how many odd results do you predict?
2. **Civic.** A local council in the City of Whittlesea runs a raffle with 500 tickets. You buy 4 tickets. What is P(you win)? Express as a fraction, decimal and percentage.
3. **Vocational.** A quality inspector at a Melbourne factory tests light bulbs. In a batch of 200, experience shows about 3% are defective. How many defective bulbs does she expect to find? What is the probability that a randomly chosen bulb is not defective?
4. **Ethical.** A classmate argues that because a coin has two sides, "getting two heads in a row" also has probability 1/2. Explain why this reasoning is flawed using sample-space thinking.
5. **Cross-cultural.** You are designing a fair game for a multicultural school fete using a six-sided die. The game must give each of three teams an equal chance of winning. Describe how you assign outcomes to teams and verify fairness.

## Memorable takeaways
- Always list the sample space first — it is the foundation of probability.
- P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes (for equally likely outcomes).
- Probabilities range from 0 to 1 and must sum to 1.
- Predicted frequency = probability × number of trials.
- Real results will vary from predictions — that is randomness, not error.

## Stretch yourself
A school uses a random-number generator (digits 0–9, equally likely) to assign students to one of four houses. House A gets digits 0 and 1. House B gets 2 and 3. House C gets 4, 5 and 6. House D gets 7, 8 and 9. Is this system fair? Calculate P(House A), P(House B), P(House C) and P(House D). If 500 new students are assigned, predict the number in each house. Propose a modification that makes the system fair and justify your answer using sample-space reasoning. Show all working. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "There are two outcomes — it happens or it doesn't — so the probability is always 1/2." Only valid when outcomes are equally likely. A meteor strike tomorrow has two outcomes but is not 50/50.
- "If I flip 5 heads in a row, tails is due next." Each flip is independent. P(Tail) is still 1/2 regardless of history — this error is the **gambler's fallacy**.
- "Probability 0 means it will never happen in practice." For continuous scenarios, individual points can have probability 0 but events still occur. At this level, think of 0 as impossible and values near 0 as very unlikely.
- "Predicted frequency is exact." It is an expectation — actual results will spread around it.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Is drawing one card a single-stage experiment?* Yes — it has one action with a well-defined set of outcomes (52 cards).
2. *Spinner sample space.* S = {Red, Blue, Green, Yellow}.
3. *P(blue) from 3 red, 2 blue, 5 green.* Total = 10. P(blue) = 2/10 = 1/5.
4. *3/8 as decimal and percentage.* 0.375 = 37.5%.
5. *P(greater than 4) on a die.* Favourable: {5, 6}. P = 2/6 = 1/3.
6. *Probability scale for Victorian winter rain.* Close to 1 (very likely) — say 0.85–0.95.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Spinner.* S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}. Odd = {1, 3, 5}. P(odd) = 3/5. Predicted in 100 spins: 60.
2. *Raffle.* P(win) = 4/500 = 1/125 = 0.008 = 0.8%.
3. *Light bulbs.* Expected defective: 0.03 × 200 = 6. P(not defective) = 0.97 = 97%.
4. *Two heads in a row.* The sample space for two flips is {HH, HT, TH, TT} — four equally likely outcomes. P(HH) = 1/4, not 1/2. The classmate confused the number of stages with the number of outcomes.
5. *Fair game.* Assign outcomes: Team 1 gets {1, 2}, Team 2 gets {3, 4}, Team 3 gets {5, 6}. Each team has P = 2/6 = 1/3. Fair because all teams have equal probability.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** P(A) = 2/10 = 0.2. P(B) = 2/10 = 0.2. P(C) = 3/10 = 0.3. P(D) = 3/10 = 0.3. Not fair — C and D each have 50% more chance than A and B. Predicted for 500 students: A = 100, B = 100, C = 150, D = 150. Fair modification: assign 0–1 to A, 2–4 to B... doesn't work with 10 digits and 4 houses. Instead: use digits 0–7 (discard 8 and 9). Assign 0–1 to A, 2–3 to B, 4–5 to C, 6–7 to D. Each house gets P = 2/8 = 1/4. Alternatively: use all 10 digits but assign 0, 1, 2 to A (discard one digit) — simplest is to re-draw if 8 or 9 appears.
