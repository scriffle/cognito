# VC2M8P02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8P02. Determine all possible outcome combinations for 2 events, using two-way tables, tree diagrams and Venn diagrams, and use these to determine probabilities of specific events in practical situations.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - When two events combine, you need a systematic way to list all possible outcomes.
  - Two-way tables, tree diagrams and Venn diagrams each display outcomes differently.
  - Choosing the right display depends on what probability question you are answering.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; ICT Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **determine all possible outcome combinations** for two events using **two-way tables**, **tree diagrams** and **Venn diagrams**.
- You are learning to use these tools to **calculate probabilities** of specific events in practical situations.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Construct a two-way table listing all outcome pairs for two events.
- Draw a tree diagram showing branches for each event and read combined outcomes from the paths.
- Use a Venn diagram to display overlapping and non-overlapping outcomes.
- Calculate probabilities by counting favourable outcomes from any of these displays.

## Core content

**1. Two events combined.** When two things happen (roll a die AND flip a coin), the total number of outcome combinations is the product of the individual outcomes: 6 × 2 = 12 combinations. Listing them systematically avoids missing any. *Quick check:* A café offers 3 sandwich fillings and 4 bread types. How many combinations are there?

**2. Two-way tables.** A **two-way table** (or grid) lists the outcomes of one event across the top and the other down the side. Each cell shows one combined outcome. You can count cells to find probabilities. *Quick check:* In a table for a coin (H, T) and a die (1–6), how many cells are there?

**3. Tree diagrams.** A **tree diagram** starts with branches for the first event, then each branch splits into branches for the second event. Each complete path (root to tip) is one combined outcome. Multiply along branches to find the probability of that path. *Quick check:* If a coin is tossed and then a die is rolled, how many complete paths does the tree have?

**4. Venn diagrams.** A **Venn diagram** shows overlapping circles inside a rectangle (the sample space). Use Venn diagrams when outcomes can belong to one event, both or neither. They are best for "and," "or" and "not" questions. *Quick check:* In a Venn diagram of "likes maths" and "likes sport," where do students who like both go?

**5. Calculating probabilities from displays.** *P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.* From a two-way table: count the cells that match your event. From a tree: add the probabilities of matching paths. From a Venn: read the number in the relevant region. *Quick check:* A two-way table has 12 equally likely cells. Three cells match your event. What is the probability?

**6. Worked example — school canteen choices.** A canteen offers 2 main meals (pasta, wrap) and 3 drinks (water, juice, milk). Two-way table:

| | Water | Juice | Milk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pasta | PW | PJ | PM |
| Wrap | WW | WJ | WM |

Six combinations. Tree diagram: first branch (pasta/wrap), each splits into three drink branches — six paths. P(pasta and juice) = 1/6. P(wrap and not water) = 2/6 = 1/3. *Follow the thinking:* list all outcomes systematically → count favourable → divide by total.

**7. Choosing the right display.** Two-way tables work best when both events have few outcomes and you want to see everything at once. Tree diagrams suit sequential events and unequal probabilities (multiply along branches). Venn diagrams suit overlapping categories and "or"/"and" questions. *Quick check:* Which display would you choose for "students who play netball, basketball, or both"?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You pick a shirt (red, blue, white) and shorts (black, grey). Draw a two-way table showing all outfit combinations. What is the probability of randomly choosing a blue shirt with grey shorts?
2. **Civic.** Moreland City Council surveys residents on two questions: "Do you use the library?" and "Do you use the pool?" Draw a Venn diagram. If 40% use the library, 55% use the pool, and 20% use both, what percentage use neither?
3. **Vocational.** A barista offers 4 coffee types and 3 milk options. Draw a tree diagram. How many combinations exist? What is the probability a random order is a flat white with oat milk?
4. **Ethical.** A school survey asks students about gender and favourite subject. Discuss whether displaying results in a two-way table risks reinforcing stereotypes, and how responsible reporting can avoid this.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Wurundjeri-led bush-food tasting offers 3 native flavours paired with 2 base foods. List all combinations using a systematic method. If one combination is randomly selected for the group to try, what is the probability it includes lemon myrtle?

## Memorable takeaways
- Total combinations = outcomes of event 1 × outcomes of event 2.
- Two-way tables show all pairs in a grid — good for equal probabilities.
- Tree diagrams show sequential branches — good for unequal probabilities (multiply along paths).
- Venn diagrams show overlap — good for "and," "or" and "neither" questions.
- P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.

## Stretch yourself
A Year 8 class of 30 students is surveyed on two questions: "Do you walk to school?" and "Do you have a pet?" Results: 12 walk and have a pet, 5 walk but no pet, 8 do not walk but have a pet, 5 neither walk nor have a pet. Construct a two-way table and a Venn diagram from this data. Calculate: P(walks to school), P(has a pet), P(walks AND has a pet), P(walks OR has a pet), P(neither). Verify that your Venn regions sum to 30. Then discuss: are "walking" and "having a pet" independent events? (Hint: check if P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).) 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Multiply outcomes to get probability." Multiply outcomes to get total combinations; probability is favourable ÷ total.
- "Venn diagram circles must overlap." If no outcomes are shared, the circles do not overlap — that is valid information.
- "Tree diagrams only work for coins and dice." They work for any sequential or two-stage event, including real-world choices.
- "'Or' means one but not both." In probability, "A or B" usually means "A or B or both" (inclusive or) unless stated otherwise.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Sandwich combinations.* 3 × 4 = 12.
2. *Coin and die table cells.* 2 × 6 = 12.
3. *Coin then die tree paths.* 12 paths (2 branches × 6 branches).
4. *Likes both in Venn.* In the overlapping (intersection) region of both circles.
5. *3 favourable out of 12.* P = 3/12 = 1/4.
6. *Netball/basketball display.* Venn diagram — it shows who plays one, both or neither.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Outfit combinations.* 3 × 2 = 6 combinations. P(blue + grey) = 1/6.
2. *Library and pool.* P(neither) = 1 − P(A or B). P(A or B) = 0.40 + 0.55 − 0.20 = 0.75. P(neither) = 0.25 (25%).
3. *Coffee tree.* 4 × 3 = 12 combinations. P(flat white + oat) = 1/12.
4. *Stereotype discussion.* Two-way tables present data neutrally, but interpretation can reinforce stereotypes if differences are overstated. Responsible reporting: present proportions fairly, avoid causal claims, acknowledge variation within groups.
5. *Bush-food combinations.* 3 × 2 = 6 combinations. Lemon myrtle appears in 2 of 6: P = 2/6 = 1/3.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Two-way table: Walk/Pet = 12, Walk/No pet = 5, No walk/Pet = 8, No walk/No pet = 5. Total = 30. P(walk) = 17/30. P(pet) = 20/30. P(walk AND pet) = 12/30. P(walk OR pet) = (17 + 20 − 12)/30 = 25/30. P(neither) = 5/30. Regions sum: 12 + 5 + 8 + 5 = 30. Independence check: P(walk) × P(pet) = (17/30)(20/30) = 340/900 ≈ 0.378. P(walk AND pet) = 12/30 = 0.400. These differ, so the events are not independent — walking and having a pet are slightly associated in this class.
