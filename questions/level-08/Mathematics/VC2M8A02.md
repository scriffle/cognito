# VC2M8A02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8A02. Graph linear relations on the Cartesian plane using digital tools where appropriate; solve linear equations and one-variable inequalities using graphical and algebraic techniques; verify solutions by substitution.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A linear relation produces a straight-line graph on the Cartesian plane.
  - You can solve equations algebraically or by reading where the line crosses a target value.
  - Inequalities describe a range of solutions, not just a single point.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; ICT Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **graph linear relations** on the Cartesian plane and to **solve linear equations and one-variable inequalities** using both graphical and algebraic methods.
- You are learning to **verify solutions by substitution**.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Create a table of values for a linear rule and plot the straight line.
- Identify the gradient and y-intercept from a graph and from the equation *y = mx + c*.
- Solve linear equations algebraically and check the solution on the graph.
- Solve one-variable inequalities and represent the solution on a number line.

## Core content

**1. Linear relations.** A **linear relation** has the form *y = mx + c*, where *m* is the **gradient** (steepness) and *c* is the **y-intercept** (where the line crosses the y-axis). Every input *x* gives exactly one output *y*. *Quick check:* In the relation *y = 3x − 2*, what is the gradient and what is the y-intercept?

**2. Table of values.** Choose at least three *x*-values, substitute into the rule and record the *y*-values. For *y = 2x + 1*: when *x = 0*, *y = 1*; *x = 1*, *y = 3*; *x = 2*, *y = 5*. Plot these points and draw the straight line through them. *Quick check:* Complete the table for *y = −x + 4* when *x* = 0, 2, 4.

**3. Graphing with digital tools.** Spreadsheets, GeoGebra or Desmos let you graph a relation instantly. Enter the rule, adjust the viewing window and read features directly. Digital tools are especially useful for comparing multiple lines or checking your hand-drawn graph. *Quick check:* Why might you adjust the viewing window on a graphing tool?

**4. Solving equations graphically.** To solve *2x + 1 = 7*, graph *y = 2x + 1* and draw the horizontal line *y = 7*. The x-coordinate where they meet is the solution (*x = 3*). *Quick check:* How would you solve *−x + 4 = 1* graphically?

**5. Solving equations algebraically.** Use inverse operations: *2x + 1 = 7* → *2x = 6* → *x = 3*. Always verify by substitution: *2(3) + 1 = 7*. Confirmed. *Quick check:* Solve *5x − 3 = 22* and verify.

**6. One-variable inequalities.** An **inequality** uses <, >, ≤ or ≥ instead of =. Solve like an equation, but if you multiply or divide by a negative number, **reverse the sign**. Example: *2x + 1 > 7* → *2x > 6* → *x > 3*. The solution is a range, shown on a number line with an open circle at 3 and an arrow to the right. *Quick check:* Solve *3x − 4 ≤ 8* and represent on a number line.

**7. Worked example — Melbourne tram fare comparison.** A myki Money fare costs $2 per trip. A myki Pass costs $10 per day for unlimited trips. When is myki Money cheaper? Let *t* = number of trips. Myki Money cost: *C₁ = 2t*. Myki Pass cost: *C₂ = 10*. Graph both lines. They cross at *t = 5*. Inequality: *2t < 10* → *t < 5*. So myki Money is cheaper for fewer than 5 trips. Verify: at *t = 4*, *2(4) = 8 < 10*. Confirmed. *Follow the thinking:* model each option → graph → find the crossover → write the inequality → verify.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** PTV myki fare structures, Melbourne Water usage tiers, Victorian electricity tariff comparisons and Melbourne rental-market cost models all generate linear relations that students can graph and compare. GeoGebra and Desmos are freely available to all Victorian government-school students.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung seasonal knowledge tracks how resources change over time — fish runs in the Birrarung, yam daisy availability across months — relationships between time and abundance that can be modelled as changing quantities. While not historically expressed in Cartesian notation, these are quantitative relationships. AIATSIS publishes research on Indigenous systems of environmental observation. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students from farming backgrounds understand seasonal cost patterns; students from small-business families track profit and loss as linear models. Financial literacy across cultures connects directly to graphing cost and revenue lines.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You earn $12 per hour at a part-time job. Write the rule for earnings *E* in terms of hours *h*, graph it, and use the graph to find how many hours you need to earn $84.
2. **Civic.** Hobsons Bay City Council offers two pool-entry plans: casual at $7 per visit, or membership at $50 plus $2 per visit. Graph both and find the crossover point. Write an inequality for when membership is cheaper.
3. **Vocational.** A plumber charges a $60 call-out fee plus $45 per hour. Write the linear rule, graph it, and solve algebraically for the time if the bill is $240.
4. **Ethical.** A "buy now, pay later" service advertises "no interest" but charges a $5 monthly fee. Model the total cost over *m* months for a $100 purchase and compare with paying upfront. Discuss whether the advertising is misleading.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Gunaikurnai ranger records water temperature in the Gippsland Lakes each month. If the relationship is approximately *T = 2m + 10* (where *m* = months since June), graph it and use the graph to estimate when the water reaches 20°C.

## Memorable takeaways
- A linear relation graphs as a straight line: *y = mx + c*.
- The gradient *m* is the steepness; the y-intercept *c* is where the line crosses the y-axis.
- Solve equations algebraically (inverse operations) or graphically (read the intersection).
- Inequalities have a range of solutions — show them on a number line.
- Always verify by substitution.

## Stretch yourself
A community market stall in Footscray sells handmade candles. Fixed costs (materials, stall hire) are $80 per market day. Each candle sells for $12. Revenue: *R = 12n*. Costs: *C = 80*. Profit: *P = 12n − 80*. Graph all three relations on the same Cartesian plane. Use the graph to identify the break-even point (where *P = 0*). Then solve the inequality *12n − 80 > 0* algebraically to confirm. Finally, if the stallholder wants at least $100 profit, write and solve the inequality *12n − 80 ≥ 100*. Verify both answers by substitution. Show all working and annotate your graph. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "The gradient is the y-intercept." The gradient is *m* (rise over run); the y-intercept is *c* (where the line crosses the y-axis). They are different values.
- "Multiply by a negative and keep the sign." When you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number, you must reverse the inequality sign.
- "A graph with only two points is reliable." Always plot at least three points — the third confirms the line is correct.
- "The graphical and algebraic answers can differ." They should agree. If they do not, check your working.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *y = 3x − 2.* Gradient: 3. Y-intercept: −2.
2. *Table for y = −x + 4.* x = 0 → y = 4; x = 2 → y = 2; x = 4 → y = 0.
3. *Adjusting viewing window.* To see the relevant part of the graph — the line might not appear if the window is too small or too large.
4. *Solve −x + 4 = 1 graphically.* Graph *y = −x + 4* and *y = 1*. They meet at *x = 3*.
5. *Solve 5x − 3 = 22.* Add 3: *5x = 25*. Divide by 5: *x = 5*. Check: 5(5) − 3 = 22.
6. *Solve 3x − 4 ≤ 8.* Add 4: *3x ≤ 12*. Divide by 3: *x ≤ 4*. Number line: closed circle at 4, arrow to the left.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Part-time job.* *E = 12h*. Graph passes through origin with gradient 12. At *E = 84*: *12h = 84 → h = 7* hours.
2. *Pool plans.* Casual: *C₁ = 7v*. Membership: *C₂ = 2v + 50*. Crossover: *7v = 2v + 50 → 5v = 50 → v = 10* visits. Inequality: *2v + 50 < 7v → 50 < 5v → v > 10*. Membership is cheaper for more than 10 visits.
3. *Plumber.* *C = 45h + 60 = 240 → 45h = 180 → h = 4* hours.
4. *Buy now, pay later.* Total after *m* months: *100 + 5m*. At 12 months: $160 vs $100 upfront. The "no interest" claim obscures the $60 in fees. Potentially misleading.
5. *Water temperature.* *T = 2m + 10 = 20 → 2m = 10 → m = 5*. Five months after June = November. Water reaches 20°C around November.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Break-even: *12n − 80 = 0 → n = 6.67*, so 7 candles to break even (first whole number giving profit). Inequality confirmed: *12(7) − 80 = 4 > 0*. For $100 profit: *12n − 80 ≥ 100 → 12n ≥ 180 → n ≥ 15*. Check: *12(15) − 80 = 100*. Graph shows revenue crossing cost line at approximately *n = 6.7* and profit reaching $100 at *n = 15*.
