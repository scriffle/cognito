## Snapshot

- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Measurement).

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **solve problems involving the volume and capacity of right prisms** using appropriate units.
- You are learning that one formula — V = A × h — works for every right prism, no matter the cross-sectional shape.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify the cross-section and height of a right prism.
- Calculate the volume of rectangular, triangular and other right prisms.
- Convert between volume units (cm³, m³) and capacity units (mL, L).
- Apply volume calculations to real-world contexts.

## Core content

**1. What is a right prism?** A **right prism** has a uniform cross-section and straight sides perpendicular to the base. A cereal box (rectangular prism), a Toblerone packet (triangular prism) and a hexagonal pencil are all right prisms. The cross-section is the same shape all the way through. *Quick check:* Is a cylinder a right prism? Why or why not?

**2. Volume of a right prism.** **V = area of cross-section × height.** For a rectangular prism: V = length × width × height. For a triangular prism: V = (½ × base × height of triangle) × length of prism. The formula works because you are "stacking" the cross-section along the height. *Quick check:* A rectangular prism is 5 cm × 4 cm × 10 cm. What is its volume?

**3. Triangular prisms.** Cross-section is a triangle. Area of triangle = ½ × b × h. Then multiply by the prism's length. Example: triangle base 6 cm, triangle height 4 cm, prism length 15 cm. V = ½ × 6 × 4 × 15 = 180 cm³. *Quick check:* A tent is a triangular prism with triangle base 3 m, triangle height 2 m, and length 4 m. What is the volume of air inside?

**4. Other right prisms.** Trapezoidal guttering, hexagonal nuts, L-shaped beams — find the area of the cross-section using appropriate formulas, then multiply by length. *Quick check:* A trapezoidal gutter has parallel sides 8 cm and 12 cm, depth 5 cm, and runs for 3 m (300 cm). What is its volume?

**5. Volume and capacity.** **1 cm³ = 1 mL. 1 000 cm³ = 1 L. 1 m³ = 1 000 L.** Capacity describes how much liquid a container holds. A fish tank 60 cm × 30 cm × 40 cm has volume 72 000 cm³ = 72 L. *Quick check:* A rectangular water tank is 2 m × 1 m × 0.5 m. What is its capacity in litres?

**6. Worked example: concrete for a path.**

A garden path is a rectangular prism: 8 m long, 1.2 m wide, 0.1 m deep. How much concrete is needed?

*Follow the thinking:*
- Cross-section: rectangle 1.2 m × 0.1 m. Area = 0.12 m².
- Height (length of path) = 8 m.
- V = 0.12 × 8 = 0.96 m³.
- Concrete is often sold by the cubic metre, so order 1 m³ (allowing for waste).
- Capacity check: 0.96 m³ = 960 L. This confirms the scale is reasonable for a domestic path.

**7. Choosing appropriate units.** A matchbox: cm³. A room: m³. A swimming pool: m³ or megalitres. A medicine dose: mL. Always check that your units make sense for the context. *Quick check:* What unit would you use for the volume of a shipping container?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Water manages reservoir capacity in megalitres (1 ML = 1 000 000 L = 1 000 m³). The Yarra Valley Water supply area holds reservoirs whose volumes are calculated from cross-sectional surveys. VicRoads specifies concrete volumes for road bases in cubic metres. The Royal Melbourne Hospital uses mL for medicine dosages — volume at the smallest scale.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Gunditjmara eel channels at Budj Bim involve water-volume management — weirs and channels control flow rates and holding-pond volumes. Understanding volume helps appreciate the engineering scale of these systems (6,600+ years old). Coolamon (carrying vessels) used across many First Nations communities have capacity determined by their curved cross-sections. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Cooking across cultures uses volume and capacity constantly — Vietnamese phở pots, Indian pressure cookers, Middle Eastern baking trays — all contexts where understanding litres, millilitres and container dimensions matters.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A fish tank is 80 cm long, 35 cm wide and 45 cm tall. Calculate the volume in cm³ and the capacity in litres. If you fill it to 5 cm below the top, how many litres of water do you need?
2. **Civic.** The City of Greater Geelong installs a new triangular-prism drainage channel: triangle base 1.2 m, triangle depth 0.8 m, channel length 50 m. What volume of water can it hold when full?
3. **Vocational.** A concreter in Werribee pours a rectangular driveway slab: 12 m × 3.5 m × 0.12 m. Calculate the volume of concrete in m³. At $260 per m³ (delivered), what is the concrete cost?
4. **Ethical.** A juice company labels its box as "1 litre." The box measures 7 cm × 7 cm × 20.5 cm. Calculate the actual volume. Is the label accurate?
5. **Cross-cultural.** You are designing a raised garden bed (rectangular prism) for a school multicultural garden. It must hold at least 600 L of soil. Propose dimensions and verify your design by calculating the volume.

## Memorable takeaways
- Right prism: V = area of cross-section × height.
- Rectangular prism: V = l × w × h.
- Triangular prism: V = ½ × b × h × length.
- 1 cm³ = 1 mL. 1 000 cm³ = 1 L. 1 m³ = 1 000 L.
- Identify the cross-section first — the rest is multiplication.

## Stretch yourself
A community swimming pool has a cross-section that is a trapezium: the shallow end is 1.2 m deep, the deep end is 2.4 m deep, and the pool is 25 m long. The pool is 10 m wide. Calculate the volume of water when the pool is completely full. Convert your answer to litres and megalitres. If the pool loses 2% of its volume per week to evaporation, how many litres must be added each week? The council charges $2.80 per kilolitre (1 000 L) for water. What is the weekly evaporation cost? Show all working. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I multiplied all three numbers for a triangular prism." You need the area of the triangle (½ × b × h) first, then multiply by the prism length. Forgetting the ½ doubles your answer.
- "Volume and capacity are different things." They measure the same space — volume in cm³ or m³, capacity in mL or L. 1 cm³ = 1 mL exactly.
- "I used cm for one dimension and m for another." Convert all measurements to the same unit before multiplying.
- "A bigger base always means more volume." A tall, narrow prism can have more volume than a short, wide one. Both cross-sectional area and height matter.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Is a cylinder a right prism?* Technically yes — its cross-section (a circle) is uniform and its sides are perpendicular to the base. Some textbooks treat it separately because the cross-section is curved, but the formula V = A × h still applies.
