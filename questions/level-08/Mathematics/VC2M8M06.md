# VC2M8M06 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8M06. Use Pythagoras' theorem to solve problems involving the side lengths of right-angled triangles.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Measurement and Geometry).
- **Big ideas:**
  - In any right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.
  - Pythagoras' theorem lets you find an unknown side when you know the other two.
  - The theorem connects geometry to algebra and appears across construction, navigation and design.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Design and Technologies.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to use **Pythagoras' theorem** to find unknown side lengths in **right-angled triangles**.
- You are learning to identify when a problem involves a right angle and to apply the theorem in practical contexts.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- State Pythagoras' theorem: a² + b² = c² (where c is the hypotenuse).
- Identify the hypotenuse in a right-angled triangle.
- Calculate the hypotenuse given two shorter sides.
- Calculate a shorter side given the hypotenuse and one shorter side.

## Core content

**1. Right-angled triangles and the hypotenuse.** A **right-angled triangle** has one angle of exactly 90°. The longest side, opposite the right angle, is called the **hypotenuse**. The other two sides are called the **legs** (or shorter sides). *Quick check:* In a right-angled triangle, which side is always the longest?

**2. Pythagoras' theorem.** For any right-angled triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c: **a² + b² = c²**. This means: the area of the square drawn on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the areas of the squares drawn on the other two sides. *Quick check:* If a = 3 and b = 4, what is c²?

**3. Finding the hypotenuse.** When you know both legs, find the hypotenuse by adding their squares and taking the square root: c = √(a² + b²). Example: a = 5, b = 12. c² = 25 + 144 = 169. c = √169 = 13. *Quick check:* Find the hypotenuse when a = 6 and b = 8.

**4. Finding a shorter side.** When you know the hypotenuse and one leg, rearrange: a² = c² − b². Example: c = 10, b = 6. a² = 100 − 36 = 64. a = √64 = 8. *Quick check:* The hypotenuse is 13 and one leg is 5. Find the other leg.

**5. Pythagorean triples.** Some right-angled triangles have all-integer side lengths: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25). Multiples of triples also work: (6, 8, 10) is 2 × (3, 4, 5). Recognising these saves calculation time. *Quick check:* Is (9, 12, 15) a Pythagorean triple? How do you know?

**6. Worked example — calculating a ramp length at Federation Square.**
A wheelchair ramp must rise 1.2 m over a horizontal distance of 5 m. What is the length of the ramp surface (the hypotenuse)?

*Follow the thinking:*
- Identify the right-angled triangle: height = 1.2 m (vertical leg), base = 5 m (horizontal leg), ramp = hypotenuse.
- Apply Pythagoras: c² = 1.2² + 5² = 1.44 + 25 = 26.44.
- c = √26.44 ≈ 5.14 m.
- The ramp surface is approximately 5.14 m long.
- Check: this is slightly longer than the horizontal distance, which makes sense since the ramp tilts upward.

**7. When to use Pythagoras' theorem.** Use it whenever you have a right-angled triangle and know two sides but need the third. Look for right angles in: buildings, ladders against walls, diagonal measurements, maps (distance as the crow flies versus along roads), and any situation where horizontal and vertical distances are known. *Quick check:* A ladder leans against a wall. The foot is 3 m from the wall and the ladder reaches 4 m up. How long is the ladder?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Builders and surveyors across Victoria use Pythagoras' theorem daily — setting right angles on building sites (the "3-4-5 rule"), calculating roof pitches, and measuring diagonal distances. The engineering of the West Gate Bridge, the MCG floodlight towers, and Federation Square's deconstructivist geometry all rely on right-angle calculations. Engineers at Engineers Australia use the theorem in structural analysis.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The practical geometry of right angles appears in First Nations construction — the precise stone-wall angles at Budj Bim on Gunditjmara Country, and the straight-line surveying evident in large stone arrangements. While these practices predate the Greek formalisation, they demonstrate the same spatial reasoning. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The relationship a² + b² = c² was known independently in Babylonian, Chinese, Indian and Greek mathematical traditions — well before Pythagoras. The Babylonian tablet Plimpton 322 (c. 1800 BCE) lists Pythagorean triples. Students can explore these diverse origins.

## Memorable takeaways
- a² + b² = c² — always with c as the hypotenuse (longest side, opposite the right angle).
- To find the hypotenuse: add the squares, then square root.
- To find a leg: subtract the squares, then square root.
- Check: the hypotenuse must be longer than either leg.
- Pythagorean triples (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17) are useful shortcuts.

## Stretch yourself
A surveyor in regional Victoria is measuring the distance across a river. She stands at point A on one bank, directly opposite a tree at point B on the other bank. She walks 40 m along her bank to point C, keeping the bank straight. She measures angle ACB and finds it is 90° (the right angle is at C, with B across the river and A along the bank). She then measures AC = 40 m and AB = 50 m. Calculate BC (the width of the river). Then the surveyor needs to check her work: she walks further to point D, 30 m past C. Calculate the distance BD. Finally, discuss why Pythagoras' theorem is useful for measuring distances you cannot walk directly. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Squaring the hypotenuse instead of the legs.** c² = a² + b², not a² = b² + c². The hypotenuse is always alone on one side of the equation.
- **Forgetting the square root.** After finding c² = 169, you must take √169 = 13. The answer is 13, not 169.
- **Using Pythagoras when there is no right angle.** The theorem only works for right-angled triangles. If no right angle is present, you need different methods.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep the exact value under the square root as long as possible; round only at the final step.

A note on certainty. Pythagoras' theorem is a proven result of Euclidean geometry — it holds exactly for any right-angled triangle on a flat surface. On curved surfaces (like the Earth at large scales), it is an approximation. For the distances you encounter in everyday problems, it is effectively exact.

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Longest side.* The hypotenuse — always opposite the right angle.
2. *c² when a = 3, b = 4.* 9 + 16 = 25.
3. *Hypotenuse when a = 6, b = 8.* c² = 36 + 64 = 100. c = 10.
4. *Other leg when c = 13, b = 5.* a² = 169 − 25 = 144. a = 12.
5. *(9, 12, 15) a triple?* Yes: 81 + 144 = 225 = 15². It is 3 × (3, 4, 5).
6. *Ramp at Federation Square.* c = √26.44 ≈ 5.14 m (worked through in example).
7. *Ladder length.* c² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25. c = 5 m.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Bedroom cable.* c² = 4² + 3² = 16 + 9 = 25. c = 5 m.
2. *Zip-line.* c² = 8² + 15² = 64 + 225 = 289. c = 17 m.
3. *Door frame diagonal.* c² = 2.1² + 0.9² = 4.41 + 0.81 = 5.22. c = √5.22 ≈ 2.28 m.
4. *Checking 5, 6, 8.* 5² + 6² = 25 + 36 = 61. 8² = 64. 61 ≠ 64, so not a right-angled triangle.
5. *Plimpton 322.* A Babylonian clay tablet (c. 1800 BCE) listing sets of numbers that satisfy a² + b² = c² — Pythagorean triples recorded over a thousand years before Pythagoras.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Triangle ACB with right angle at C: AC = 40 m, AB = 50 m. BC² = AB² − AC² = 2500 − 1600 = 900. BC = 30 m. The river is 30 m wide. For point D (30 m past C along the bank): AD = 40 + 30 = 70 m. Triangle BDC has legs BC = 30 m and CD = 30 m. BD² = 30² + 30² = 900 + 900 = 1800. BD = √1800 ≈ 42.4 m. Pythagoras' theorem is useful because it calculates distances you cannot physically cross (rivers, ravines, buildings) using only measurements you can take from accessible positions.
