# VC2M8ST04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8ST04. Plan and conduct statistical investigations involving samples of a population; use ethical and fair methods to make inferences about the population and report findings, acknowledging uncertainty.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Statistics and Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A well-designed investigation uses fair sampling to make inferences about a larger population.
  - Inference means going beyond your sample to draw conclusions about the whole group.
  - Acknowledging uncertainty is a mark of good statistical reasoning, not a weakness.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan and conduct statistical investigations** using samples, and to **make inferences about the population** while **acknowledging uncertainty**.
- You are learning to apply ethical and fair methods throughout.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Plan an investigation with a clear question, defined population, and fair sampling method.
- Conduct data collection using ethical practices.
- Make an inference about the population based on sample results.
- Report findings with an explicit statement of uncertainty.

## Core content

**1. From sample to population — inference.** **Statistical inference** means using what you learn from a sample to draw conclusions about the whole population. If 72% of a random sample of 100 students prefer the new timetable, you infer that approximately 72% of the whole student body does — but you acknowledge this might not be exact. *Quick check:* What is the difference between describing a sample and making an inference?

**2. Planning a fair investigation.** A fair investigation requires: a clear statistical question, a defined population, a sampling method that gives everyone a chance of selection (ideally random), an appropriate sample size, and ethical data-collection practices. Fair means unbiased — you are not designing the method to get a particular answer. *Quick check:* A researcher only surveys people who agree with their view. Is this fair? Why not?

**3. Ethical methods.** Ethical data collection means: **informed consent** (participants know what the study is about), **anonymity or confidentiality** (responses cannot be traced back), **no harm** (participation does not cause distress), **voluntary** (no one is forced to participate), and **honest reporting** (results are not manipulated). *Quick check:* A survey asks students about mental health but names are recorded and shared with teachers. What ethical principle is violated?

**4. Conducting the investigation.** Collect data according to your plan. Keep careful records. Check for errors or missing responses. Note the response rate (how many of those asked actually participated). A low response rate can introduce bias even in a random sample. *Quick check:* You randomly select 50 students but only 20 respond. Why might this be a problem?

**5. Making inferences.** After analysis (shape, centre, spread), state your inference clearly: "Based on our sample of 80 students, we estimate that approximately 65% of the school population prefers option A." Add a qualifier: "This estimate may vary due to sampling variability. A different sample of the same size could give a result between roughly 55% and 75%." *Quick check:* Why do we say "approximately" and "estimate" rather than "exactly"?

**6. Worked example — inferring study habits across a year level.**
A student investigates: "How many hours per week do Year 8 students at our school spend on homework?"

*Follow the thinking:*
- **Plan:** Population = all 180 Year 8 students. Method = simple random sample of 30 (using class rolls and a random-number generator). Anonymous online survey.
- **Ethical check:** Consent form sent home. Responses anonymous. Participation voluntary.
- **Collect:** 27 of 30 responded (90% response rate — good). Data recorded in a spreadsheet.
- **Analyse:** Median = 5 hours. Mean = 5.8 hours. Range = 0–15 hours. Shape: positively skewed (a few students report very high hours).
- **Infer:** "Based on our random sample, we estimate the typical Year 8 student does about 5–6 hours of homework per week. The median (5 h) is a better measure of 'typical' because the distribution is skewed."
- **Uncertainty:** "Our sample is 30 of 180. Different random samples could give slightly different results. The true population median is likely between 4 and 7 hours."

**7. Acknowledging uncertainty.** Uncertainty is not a flaw — it is an honest reflection of reality. Every sample-based finding carries some degree of uncertainty. Reporting it shows statistical maturity. Use phrases like: "we estimate," "approximately," "likely between ___ and ___," "based on our sample," "further investigation could refine this estimate." *Quick check:* Two students make claims. Student A: "68% of students like maths." Student B: "Based on our sample of 40, we estimate that around 65–70% of students like maths." Which is better statistical communication?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS reports all survey findings with margins of error. VicHealth's survey reports include confidence intervals. The Victorian Curriculum and Assessment Authority (VCAA) analyses NAPLAN results with explicit acknowledgement of sampling and measurement uncertainty. The Royal Statistical Society of Australia promotes transparent reporting.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Yoorrook Justice Commission's truth-telling process gathers both quantitative data and community testimonies — acknowledging that statistical data alone cannot capture the full picture. Ethical research with First Nations communities follows protocols set by organisations like AIATSIS (the AIATSIS Code of Ethics for Research). Fair inference means involving the community in interpreting what the data means. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students from diverse backgrounds can consider how survey language, cultural norms around privacy, and trust in institutions all affect whether people participate in data collection — and how this influences the inferences we can make.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You survey 25 randomly selected classmates about their preferred weekend activity. 60% say "sport." Can you claim that 60% of the whole school prefers sport? Write an inference statement that acknowledges uncertainty.
2. **Civic.** The City of Whittlesea surveys 300 residents about public transport satisfaction. Results show 45% are satisfied. Write a sentence reporting this finding with appropriate uncertainty language. Then explain why 300 out of 200 000 residents still gives a useful inference.
3. **Vocational.** A café owner surveys 40 customers about a new menu item. 70% say they would buy it. The owner plans to stock accordingly for all 200 daily customers. Write an inference and identify one source of uncertainty the owner should consider.
4. **Ethical.** A student wants to investigate whether classmates have ever cheated on a test. Discuss two ethical concerns with this investigation and suggest how the student could design the study to be fair and ethical.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A national survey about reading habits is conducted only in English. Explain how this affects who responds, what inferences can be made, and suggest one improvement to make the study fairer.

## Memorable takeaways
- Inference = going beyond the sample to draw conclusions about the population.
- Fair methods: random sampling, ethical practices, honest reporting.
- Always state: "Based on our sample of [n], we estimate..."
- Acknowledge uncertainty: "A different sample might give slightly different results."
- Ethical research respects consent, anonymity, voluntariness and truthful reporting.

## Stretch yourself
Design and conduct a complete statistical investigation to answer a question about your school community. Include: (1) a clear question; (2) defined population; (3) sampling method with justification; (4) ethical checklist (consent, anonymity, voluntary participation); (5) data collection from at least 20 people; (6) analysis with a display and summary statistics; (7) an inference statement about the population; (8) an explicit statement of uncertainty with at least two sources identified (sampling variability, non-response, measurement error). Discuss whether your inference would change if the sample were twice as large. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Stating results as certainties.** Never say "72% of students think..." Say "Based on our sample, we estimate about 72%..."
- **Ignoring non-response.** If many people refuse to participate, those who respond may differ from those who do not — this affects your inference.
- **Confusing sample statistics with population parameters.** The sample mean is an estimate of the population mean — they are not the same thing.
- **Claiming causation from a survey.** A survey shows association only. To claim one thing causes another, you need an experiment.

A note on certainty. Statistical inference is about making the best possible claim given incomplete information. It is honest about what it does not know. This is a strength, not a weakness — it means you are reasoning carefully rather than overstating your evidence.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Describing vs inferring.* Describing reports what the sample shows. Inferring draws a conclusion about the whole population based on the sample.
2. *Only surveying people who agree.* Not fair — it is selection bias. Fair sampling gives everyone a chance, not just those who confirm the researcher's view.
3. *Confidentiality violation.* Sharing named responses with teachers violates confidentiality and could cause harm — students might not answer honestly.
4. *Low response rate problem.* The 20 who responded may differ from the 30 who did not (e.g. more engaged or available students). This introduces non-response bias.
5. *Why "approximately"?* Because the sample is not the population. Sampling variability means a different sample might give a different result.
6. *Homework investigation.* Inference: typical Year 8 student does 5–6 hours/week. Uncertainty: true median likely 4–7 hours. Good response rate (90%) strengthens the inference.
7. *Better communication.* Student B — they name the sample size, use "estimate," and give a range acknowledging uncertainty.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Weekend activity.* "Based on our random sample of 25 classmates, we estimate that around 60% of students in our year level prefer sport on weekends. However, a different sample could give a result between approximately 45% and 75% due to sampling variability."
2. *Public transport.* "Based on a random sample of 300 Whittlesea residents, approximately 45% are satisfied with public transport." 300 is useful because, if randomly selected, it captures enough variability to give a reasonably precise estimate — precision depends more on sample size than on the fraction of the population sampled.
3. *Café inference.* "Based on 40 surveyed customers, approximately 70% would buy the new item. The owner should expect roughly 140 of 200 daily customers to be interested, but acknowledge that surveyed customers may not represent all customers (e.g. regular vs first-time visitors)."
4. *Cheating investigation ethics.* Concerns: (1) students may feel pressured or judged; (2) honest answers could have consequences if anonymity fails. Design: fully anonymous, no identifying information collected, voluntary participation with clear opt-out, results reported only in aggregate.
5. *English-only survey.* Excludes non-English speakers — inferences apply only to English-proficient readers. Improvement: translate into community languages and provide multilingual collectors.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Question, population and method clearly stated. Ethical practices followed. Data from 20+ people analysed with display and statistics. Inference: "Based on our sample, we estimate [finding] for the school population." Uncertainty: sampling variability (different sample could give different results), non-response (some selected students did not participate, possibly differing from responders), and measurement error (self-reported data may be inaccurate). If the sample were doubled, variability would decrease and the inference would be more precise — but bias sources (non-response, self-report) would remain.
