# VC2M8ST02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8ST02. Analyse and report on the distribution of data from primary and secondary sources using random and non-random sampling techniques.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Statistics and Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - How you select your sample shapes the distribution you observe.
  - Random sampling reduces bias; non-random sampling can introduce it.
  - Analysing and reporting means describing shape, centre and spread, then connecting findings to the sampling method.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse and report on data distributions** collected from primary and secondary sources using **random and non-random sampling**.
- You are learning to evaluate how the sampling technique affects what the data shows.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Distinguish random from non-random sampling techniques.
- Collect or access data from primary and secondary sources.
- Analyse the distribution (shape, centre, spread, outliers).
- Report findings while acknowledging how the sampling method may affect results.

## Core content

**1. Random sampling techniques.** In **random sampling**, every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected. Types: **simple random** (names from a hat, random-number generator), **systematic** (every 10th person on a roll), **stratified random** (divide into groups, then randomly sample within each). Random methods minimise **selection bias**. *Quick check:* You assign every student a number and use a random-number generator to pick 30. What type of sampling is this?

**2. Non-random sampling techniques.** In **non-random sampling**, selection depends on availability, convenience or judgement. Types: **convenience** (survey whoever is nearby), **voluntary** (people choose to respond), **purposive** (researcher deliberately selects certain groups). Non-random methods are quicker and cheaper but are more likely to produce **biased** results that do not represent the population. *Quick check:* A radio station asks listeners to call in and vote on a question. Is this random or non-random? Why?

**3. Primary and secondary sources.** **Primary data** — you collect it yourself (surveys, measurements, experiments). **Secondary data** — collected by others (ABS, Bureau of Meteorology, published studies). Both can be sampled randomly or non-randomly. When using secondary data, check: Who collected it? How? What was the population? What sampling method was used? *Quick check:* You download rainfall data from the Bureau of Meteorology. Is this primary or secondary data?

**4. Analysing the distribution.** Once you have data, create a display (dot plot, stem-and-leaf, histogram) and describe: **shape** (symmetric, skewed, uniform, bimodal), **centre** (mean, median, mode), **spread** (range, interquartile range), **outliers**. Consider: does the sampling method explain any features of the distribution? A convenience sample from one location might cluster differently than a random sample across the whole population. *Quick check:* A voluntary online survey about screen time might overrepresent heavy screen users. How could this affect the shape of the distribution?

**5. Comparing random and non-random results.** If you take a random sample and a convenience sample from the same population, their distributions may differ. The random sample is more likely to reflect the true population distribution. Differences highlight **sampling bias**. This is why researchers prefer random methods when practical. *Quick check:* Two surveys measure student sleep hours. Survey A is random; Survey B only asks students in the library before school. Which likely has more low-sleep respondents?

**6. Worked example — analysing commute data from two sampling methods.**
A student investigates how long it takes Year 8 students to travel to school.

Method 1 (random): 20 students selected randomly from the year-level roll.
Results: 8, 10, 12, 15, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 25, 28, 30, 30, 32, 35, 38, 40, 45, 50, 55 minutes.
Median = 26.5 min. Mean = 27.2 min. Range = 47. Shape: roughly symmetric, slight right skew.

Method 2 (convenience): 20 students who arrive in the first 10 minutes of the day.
Results: 5, 5, 8, 8, 10, 10, 10, 12, 12, 12, 14, 15, 15, 15, 18, 18, 20, 20, 22, 25 minutes.
Median = 13 min. Mean = 13.7 min. Range = 20. Shape: positively skewed, clustered low.

*Follow the thinking:*
- The convenience sample captured only students who arrive early — those with short commutes.
- The random sample represents the full range of travel times.
- Reporting the convenience sample as "typical" would underestimate how long most students travel.
- Conclusion: the sampling method directly shaped the distribution observed.

**7. Reporting findings.** A good report includes: the question, population, sampling technique (random or non-random and which type), sample size, a display, summary statistics, interpretation of shape, and a statement about how the sampling method may have influenced results. If the sample is non-random, state this honestly and discuss the likely direction of bias. *Quick check:* Why should a report always name the sampling method?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** VicHealth uses stratified random sampling for the Victorian Population Health Survey — results inform state health policy. The ABS uses random sampling for the Labour Force Survey (monthly employment data). By contrast, social media polls are voluntary (non-random) and often misrepresent public opinion. Students can compare ABS data quality to online poll quality.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Non-random sampling has historically underrepresented Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in Australian data. The Closing the Gap framework and the Yoorrook Justice Commission use community-controlled data methods to ensure First Nations voices are accurately included. Understanding sampling bias helps explain why official statistics sometimes conflict with community experience. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Surveys conducted only in English may underrepresent non-English-speaking communities. Students can investigate how the ABS translates Census forms into multiple languages and employs community-language collectors to improve representation.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to know the most popular music genre among Year 8 students. You survey your friendship group (15 people). Identify the sampling type and explain one way the results might not represent the whole year level.
2. **Civic.** A council conducts a voluntary online survey about park usage. Only 120 of 5 000 residents respond. Discuss why the results may be biased and suggest how a random sampling method could improve the data.
3. **Vocational.** A market research firm compares two methods: interviewing shoppers at a mall (convenience) versus phoning randomly selected households. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of each for a product survey.
4. **Ethical.** A school principal uses a voluntary survey to claim "95% of students are happy." Only 80 of 400 students responded. Discuss why this claim may be misleading and what sampling method would give a fairer picture.
5. **Cross-cultural.** The ABS Labour Force Survey uses stratified random sampling by region. Explain why stratification helps ensure that rural and remote communities (including remote Aboriginal communities) are represented proportionally.

## Memorable takeaways
- Random sampling: every member has a known chance — minimises bias.
- Non-random sampling: quicker but higher bias risk.
- The sampling method shapes the distribution you observe.
- Always name your method and discuss how it may affect results.
- Compare random and non-random samples to see bias in action.

## Stretch yourself
Design an investigation to compare random and non-random sampling from the same population. Choose a measurable variable (e.g. time spent on homework, number of books read this term). Collect a random sample of 15 students (using a random-number generator on the class roll) and a convenience sample of 15 students (e.g. students sitting near you). Create a display for each sample. Calculate mean, median and range for both. Write a report comparing the two distributions: describe shape, centre and spread for each, explain how the sampling method likely influenced the results, and state which sample you would trust more to represent the whole class and why. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Assuming voluntary responses represent everyone.** People who choose to respond often have stronger opinions — this is **voluntary response bias**.
- **Thinking convenience sampling is worthless.** It can provide useful initial insights, but you must acknowledge its limitations honestly.
- **Ignoring who is missing.** The most important question about any sample is: "Who was left out, and how might they differ from those included?"
- **Treating secondary data as automatically trustworthy.** Always check who collected it, how, and whether the sampling method was appropriate for your question.

A note on certainty. No sample perfectly represents a population. Random sampling gives the best chance of representativeness, but even random samples vary due to chance (sampling variability). Statistical inference is about acknowledging this uncertainty, not eliminating it.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Friendship group.* Convenience sampling. Friends may share similar tastes — results may not reflect the year level's diversity.
2. *Voluntary park survey.* Only people with strong opinions (love or hate the park) respond. Random sampling of addresses with follow-up would improve representation.
3. *Mall vs phone.* Mall (convenience): advantage — quick, face-to-face detail. Disadvantage — only captures shoppers at that time/location. Phone (random): advantage — better population coverage. Disadvantage — people may not answer or may rush.
4. *"95% happy" claim.* 80 of 400 is 20% response rate. Those who responded may be happier (or more vocal). A random sample with higher response rate would be fairer.
5. *Stratification.* Without stratification, a random sample could miss small or remote communities. Stratification guarantees proportional representation from every region.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Random sample: wider spread, centre near the class average, shape roughly reflecting the true distribution. Convenience sample: likely narrower spread, centre shifted toward students near you (who may share habits). Report compares: random has larger range and median closer to the class mean; convenience clusters around a particular value. The random sample is more trustworthy because every student had an equal chance of selection, while the convenience sample reflects only a localised subset.
