# VC2M8ST03 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Compare variations in distributions and proportions obtained from random samples of the same size drawn from a population and recognise the effect of sample size on this variation.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Different random samples give different results — sampling variability. Larger samples produce less variability and match the population more closely. No single sample is perfect.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **compare variation between random samples** and to **recognise how sample size affects that variation**.
- You should understand why no single sample perfectly represents the population.

## Core content

**Sampling variability** is the natural fluctuation between different random samples from the same population. Take a sample and calculate the mean, take another — the means will differ. This is not error; it is a feature of randomness.

Example: a jar has 60% blue marbles. Samples of 10 might give proportions of 50%, 70%, 60% — varying widely around the true value. Samples of 100 cluster much tighter: perhaps 57%, 62%, 59%.

**The key rule: larger samples have less variability.** With small samples, extreme results are common. With large samples, values balance out and estimates stabilise near the population value.

**Worked example.** A school's true mean screen time is 3.2 h. Five samples of 20 gave means ranging from 2.8 to 3.5 (spread 0.7 h). Samples of 100 would cluster from about 3.0 to 3.4 (spread ~0.4 h). Individual samples vary, but larger samples vary less.

**Why it matters.** A poll of 50 saying "52% support" could easily be 46% or 58% in reality. A poll of 1 000 saying 52% is much more stable. Sample size determines how much trust to place in a result.

**Victorian context.** The ABS uses large samples (tens of thousands) for employment surveys. Newspoll reports ±3% margins for n ≈ 1 000. Small samples have historically produced unreliable data about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities.

## Memorable takeaways
- Different samples give different results — this is sampling variability.
- Larger samples → less variability → more reliable estimates.
- No single sample perfectly matches the population.
- Small samples can mislead by chance.

## Watch out for these traps
- Thinking one sample tells the whole story — it is one snapshot of many possible ones.
- Ignoring sample size when interpreting results — 10 people vs 1 000 people carry very different weight.
- Confusing variability (random, fixable with size) with bias (systematic, not fixable with size alone).

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Will two random samples give the same mean?* Almost certainly not — different students, different results.
- *Sample of 15 vs 150 — which more reliable?* 150 — less variability.
- *70% heads from 10 flips — surprising?* Not very — high variability from a small sample.
- *60% from 30 people — confident the whole population agrees?* No — small sample means high variability; true value could easily be 50%.
- *Why does variability shrink with sample size?* Extreme values balance out in larger groups.
