# VC2M8M01 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Solve problems involving the area and perimeter of irregular and composite shapes using appropriate units.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Composite shapes split into simpler shapes whose areas you know. Perimeter means every outer edge counts. Choosing the right decomposition makes hard problems straightforward.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **calculate area and perimeter of irregular and composite shapes** using appropriate units.
- You should be able to decompose complex figures into rectangles, triangles and semicircles.

## Core content

A **composite shape** is formed by joining or cutting simple shapes. An L-shaped room, a house cross-section with a triangular roof, or a pool with a semicircular end.

**Area by addition.** Split into non-overlapping parts. L-shape = two rectangles: 8 × 3 + 5 × 4 = 24 + 20 = 44 m².

**Area by subtraction.** Start with a larger shape, remove the cut-out. Rectangle (20 × 15) with a square hole (5 × 5): 300 − 25 = 275 cm².

**Finding missing lengths.** Deduce from given dimensions: overall width 10 m, one section 6 m → other section 4 m. Label everything first.

**Perimeter.** Trace the entire outside boundary. Include indented edges. A common error is forgetting internal-facing edges in L-shapes.

**Worked example.** Rectangle (20 × 12 m) with a triangular alcove (base 6 m, height 4 m). Area = 240 + 12 = 252 m². Perimeter involves three rectangle sides + remaining edge + two triangle slant sides (each √(3² + 4²) = 5 m) = 68 m.

**Units.** Match to context: mm² for stamps, m² for rooms, hectares for farms.

**Victorian and cultural context.** Federation Square's pinwheel-triangle tiling creates complex composite perimeters. Gunditjmara stone-house foundations at Budj Bim feature semicircular and rectangular sections requiring composite-area calculation. Islamic architectural tiles in Melbourne mosques demonstrate composite geometry.

## Memorable takeaways
- Split into rectangles, triangles, semicircles.
- Area: add parts (or subtract cut-outs).
- Perimeter: trace every outer edge — miss nothing.
- Find missing lengths before calculating.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Forgot internal edges on the perimeter." In an L-shape, edges facing the cut-out are still part of the boundary.
- "Mixed cm and m." Convert to the same unit first.
- "Perimeter and area use the same units." Perimeter is in metres; area is in m².

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *T-shaped garden (10×2 + 4×6).* Area = 20 + 24 = 44 m².
- *12×8 rectangle, 3×3 corner cut.* Area = 96 − 9 = 87 m².
- *Missing length: total 12 m, one part 5 m.* Other part = 7 m.
- *Courtyard 8×6 minus circle r = 1.5.* Paving area = 48 − π(1.5²) ≈ 40.93 m².
- *Basketball court area unit?* Square metres (m²).
