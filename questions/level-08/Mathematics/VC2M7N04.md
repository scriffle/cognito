# VC2M7N04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7N04. Round decimals to a given accuracy appropriate to the context and use appropriate rounding and estimation to check the reasonableness of computations.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Number).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Rounding replaces an exact value with a nearby simpler value at a chosen level of accuracy.
  - The right level of rounding depends on context — money, measurement and science demand different precision.
  - Estimation catches errors before they become problems.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **round decimals to a given accuracy** appropriate to the context and to **use rounding and estimation to check** whether computations are reasonable.
- You are learning that precision is a choice, not a default.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Round a decimal to a specified number of decimal places.
- Choose an appropriate level of rounding for a given context.
- Use estimation (rounding before computing) to predict and check answers.
- Identify when a computed answer is unreasonable.

## Core content

**1. What is rounding?** **Rounding** replaces a number with a nearby value that has fewer decimal places. The rule: look at the digit immediately after your rounding position. If it is 5 or more, round up; if it is less than 5, round down. *Quick check:* Round 3.847 to one decimal place.

**2. Rounding to different levels of accuracy.** 6.3729 rounded to three decimal places: 6.373 (the fourth digit is 9, so round up). To two decimal places: 6.37 (the third digit is 2, round down). To one decimal place: 6.4 (the second digit is 7, round up). To the nearest whole number: 6 (the first decimal digit is 3, round down). *Quick check:* Round 12.0462 to two decimal places.

**3. Choosing accuracy to fit the context.** Money: round to two decimal places (cents). Measurement in a school science lab: often one decimal place. A builder's plan: to the nearest millimetre (three decimal places of a metre). Population estimates: to the nearest thousand or million. The context tells you how precise to be. *Quick check:* You are calculating the cost of 3 items at $4.833... each. What accuracy is appropriate?

**4. Estimation by rounding first.** To estimate 48.7 × 21.3, round to 50 × 20 = 1 000. The exact answer is 1 037.31 — the estimate is close, confirming the calculation is reasonable. Estimation catches order-of-magnitude mistakes (e.g. accidentally typing 487 × 213 = 103 731 — your estimate of 1 000 flags this as too large). *Quick check:* Estimate 9.8 × 31.2 by rounding each to the nearest whole number.

**5. Front-end estimation and compatible numbers.** **Front-end estimation** uses the leading digits: 5 892 + 3 145 ≈ 5 000 + 3 000 = 8 000. **Compatible numbers** are adjusted to make mental maths easier: 398 ÷ 19 ≈ 400 ÷ 20 = 20. *Quick check:* Estimate 7 215 + 2 843 using front-end estimation.

**6. Worked example: checking a shopping receipt.**

You buy 2.5 kg of apples at $4.80 per kg and 1.75 kg of bananas at $3.20 per kg. The register says $18.60.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Estimate apples: 2.5 × $5 = $12.50 (rounded $4.80 up).
- Estimate bananas: 2 × $3 = $6.00 (rounded both down).
- Estimated total: $12.50 + $6.00 = $18.50.
- Exact: 2.5 × $4.80 = $12.00; 1.75 × $3.20 = $5.60; total = $17.60.
- The register says $18.60 — that is $1.00 more than the exact answer. Something is wrong. Check the receipt.

**7. When rounding misleads.** Rounding each item in a list and then adding can introduce **accumulated rounding error**. For example, five items each rounded down by 0.4 lose $2.00 total. In financial and scientific contexts, consider rounding only the final answer, not intermediate steps. *Quick check:* Why might a scientist keep extra decimal places during calculation and only round at the end?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Bureau of Meteorology (Melbourne) reports daily rainfall to one decimal place (e.g. 12.4 mm) and temperature to one decimal place. VicRoads speed detectors round speed readings according to legislated tolerances. The Reserve Bank of Australia rounds cash transactions to the nearest five cents since 1-cent and 2-cent coins were withdrawn.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations resource management involves practical estimation — judging water levels, fire intensity and seasonal food availability based on accumulated observation rather than precise instruments. Gunditjmara eel harvesting at Budj Bim requires estimating water flow and seasonal timing, where precision comes from intergenerational knowledge rather than decimal accuracy. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Market traders at Melbourne's Queen Victoria Market round prices mentally every transaction. Currency systems worldwide use different decimal conventions — understanding rounding supports financial literacy across cultures.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You run 5.00 km in 27 minutes and 43 seconds (27.717 minutes). Calculate your pace in minutes per km and round appropriately for a running app display.
2. **Civic.** The City of Brimbank reports its recycling rate as 43.678%. Round this to one decimal place for a council newsletter. Discuss why they might choose a different level of rounding for an internal data report.
3. **Vocational.** A carpenter in Geelong measures a length as 2.347 m. The cutting machine accepts measurements to the nearest millimetre. Round appropriately. If the carpenter rounds to the nearest centimetre instead, what is the maximum error introduced?
4. **Ethical.** A charity reports it spent "approximately 90% on programs." The exact figure is 87.3%. Is this rounding reasonable or misleading? Discuss.
5. **Cross-cultural.** You are comparing rainfall data from two Melbourne stations. Station A recorded 12.45 mm and Station B recorded 12.449 mm. Both are rounded to one decimal place. Are they the same? Explain.

## Memorable takeaways
- Round at the digit after your target: 5 or more rounds up, less than 5 rounds down.
- Context dictates precision — money to cents, rain to one decimal place.
- Estimate before you calculate to catch errors.
- Round the final answer, not every intermediate step.
- Estimation is a skill, not laziness.

## Stretch yourself
A school canteen sells four items. The prices before GST are: sandwich $4.273, juice $2.818, muffin $3.636 and fruit cup $2.455. Calculate the exact total before rounding. Then round each price to two decimal places (cents) and add the rounded prices. Compare the two totals. Explain the difference as accumulated rounding error. Finally, discuss which method — rounding each price first or rounding only the total — is fairer to the customer and why. Show all working. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Always round 5 up." In standard rounding (used in schools) the digit 5 rounds up. But in "round half to even" (banker's rounding), 2.5 rounds to 2 and 3.5 rounds to 4. Know which rule your context expects.
- "More decimal places is always better." Reporting a room width as 4.56732 m when you measured with a tape to the nearest centimetre is false precision.
- "My calculator answer must be right." Calculators do not catch input errors. Always estimate first and compare.
- "Rounding and truncating are the same." Truncating 3.87 gives 3.8; rounding 3.87 to one decimal place gives 3.9. They are different operations.

## Model responses

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Round 3.847 to one decimal place.* 3.8 (the digit after the first decimal place is 4, which is less than 5).
2. *Round 12.0462 to two decimal places.* 12.05 (the third decimal digit is 6, which rounds the second-place digit up).
3. *Cost of 3 items at $4.833... each.* Round to two decimal places: $4.83 each, total $14.49 (or compute exact total $14.50 and round once).
4. *Estimate 9.8 × 31.2.* 10 × 31 = 310. Exact answer: 305.76. Estimate confirms reasonableness.
5. *Front-end estimate of 7 215 + 2 843.* 7 000 + 2 000 = 9 000. (Refined: 7 200 + 2 800 = 10 000.)
6. *Why keep extra decimal places during calculation?* To avoid accumulated rounding error that could distort the final result.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Running pace.* 27.717 ÷ 5 = 5.5434 min/km. Round to two decimal places for the app: 5.54 min/km. One decimal place (5.5 min/km) is also reasonable.
2. *City of Brimbank.* 43.678% → 43.7% for the newsletter. An internal report might keep two decimal places (43.68%) for finer comparisons.
3. *Carpenter.* To the nearest mm: 2.347 m stays 2.347 m (already in mm). To the nearest cm: 2.35 m. Maximum error from rounding to cm: up to 0.005 m = 5 mm.
4. *Charity rounding.* Rounding 87.3% to "approximately 90%" overstates by 2.7 percentage points. This could mislead donors. A fairer rounding would be "approximately 87%" or "nearly 90% with the exact figure being 87.3%."
5. *Rainfall comparison.* Both round to 12.4 mm at one decimal place. However, their exact values differ (12.45 vs 12.449), so they are not exactly the same — rounding has hidden the difference.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Exact total: $4.273 + $2.818 + $3.636 + $2.455 = $13.182, which rounds to $13.18. Rounded prices: $4.27 + $2.82 + $3.64 + $2.46 = $13.19. Difference: $0.01 (one cent of accumulated rounding error). Rounding only the final total is fairer because it introduces less distortion. In practice, GST-inclusive pricing rounds each item to the cent, so small accumulated errors are accepted as a trade-off for displayable prices.
