# VC2M8A03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8A03. - You are learning to **use mathematical modelling** to solve applied problems involving **linear relations**, especially in **financial contexts** (profit and loss).
- You are learning to **formulate**, **choose a representation**, **interpret solutions in context** and **review the model's appropriateness**.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify a real-world problem that can be modelled with a linear function.
- Write the linear rule, choosing variables and units.
- Represent the model as a table, equation or graph.
- Interpret the solution (break-even, profit, loss) in real-world terms.
- Evaluate whether the linear model is appropriate or has limitations.

## Core content

**1. What is mathematical modelling?** Modelling means translating a real situation into mathematics, solving the maths, then interpreting the answer back in context. The cycle: **Problem → Formulate → Solve → Interpret → Review**. *Quick check:* What does "interpret" mean in the modelling cycle?

**2. Formulating a linear model.** Identify the variables (what changes), the constant rate of change (gradient) and any fixed starting value (y-intercept). Example: a food truck's daily cost is $150 (fixed: rent, fuel) plus $4 per meal prepared. Model: *C = 4n + 150*. *Quick check:* What are the variables, gradient and y-intercept in this model?

**3. Revenue, cost and profit.** **Revenue** = income from sales = price × quantity. **Cost** = fixed costs + variable costs. **Profit** = Revenue − Cost. If profit is negative, the business makes a **loss**. *Quick check:* If revenue is $800 and costs are $650, is this a profit or loss, and how much?

**4. Break-even point.** The **break-even point** is where revenue equals cost (profit = 0). Solve *Revenue = Cost*. Graphically, it is where the two lines cross. Below break-even the business makes a loss; above it, a profit. *Quick check:* Why is the break-even point important for a new business?

**5. Choosing a representation.** A table shows specific values. A graph shows the overall trend and the break-even visually. An equation lets you solve for any value precisely. Choose based on your audience and purpose. *Quick check:* When would a graph be more useful than an equation?

**6. Worked example — sausage sizzle fundraiser at a Victorian school.** A Year 8 class runs a sausage sizzle. Costs: $120 for supplies (bread, sausages, sauce, onions). Revenue: $3.50 per sausage sold. Model: *P = 3.5n − 120*. Break-even: *3.5n = 120 → n ≈ 34.3*, so they need to sell 35 sausages to make a profit. If they sell 80: *P = 3.5(80) − 120 = 280 − 120 = $160* profit. *Follow the thinking:* define variables → write cost and revenue rules → find break-even → calculate profit for a target → communicate the answer in context.

**7. Reviewing the model.** Ask: Is the rate of change truly constant? (Bulk discounts might reduce per-unit cost.) Are there hidden costs? (Permit fees, volunteer time.) Does the model apply for all values of *n*? (You cannot sell a negative number of sausages, and demand has a ceiling.) A model is useful, not perfect. *Quick check:* Name one limitation of the sausage-sizzle model.

## Stretch yourself
A Year 8 class plans a car-wash fundraiser. They estimate fixed costs of $60 (buckets, sponges, hose hire) and variable costs of $1.50 per car (water, soap). They charge $10 per car. Write the cost model, the revenue model and the profit model. Graph all three on the same Cartesian plane. Find the break-even point algebraically and verify on the graph. If they wash 25 cars, calculate profit. Then review the model: identify two assumptions that might not hold (e.g., water costs might increase with volume, demand might drop after a peak). Suggest how the model could be improved. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Profit is always positive." Profit can be negative (a loss) if costs exceed revenue.
- "Break-even means success." Break-even means zero profit — you have covered costs but earned nothing extra.
- "The model works for all values." Real models have constraints: you cannot sell negative items, and demand has a maximum.
- "Fixed costs change with quantity." Fixed costs stay the same regardless of how many items you sell — that is what makes them fixed.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Bracelets.* Cost per bracelet: $30/20 = $1.50. Revenue: $5 per sale. Profit: *P = 5n − 30*. Break-even: *5n = 30 → n = 6* bracelets.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Cost: *C = 1.5n + 60*. Revenue: *R = 10n*. Profit: *P = 10n − 1.5n − 60 = 8.5n − 60*. Break-even: *8.5n = 60 → n ≈ 7.1*, so 8 cars. At 25 cars: *P = 8.5(25) − 60 = 212.5 − 60 = $152.50*. Assumptions: constant water cost per car (might rise with volume); constant demand (might drop as queue grows). Improvement: add a stepped cost function for water usage above a threshold.
