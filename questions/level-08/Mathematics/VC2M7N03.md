## Core content

**1. What is a rational number?** A **rational number** is any number that can be written as a fraction a/b where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0. This includes whole numbers (e.g. 5 = 5/1), fractions (3/4), terminating decimals (0.75) and repeating decimals (0.333... = 1/3). *Quick check:* Is −2.5 a rational number? Why?

**2. Equivalent fractions.** Two fractions are **equivalent** when they represent the same value: 2/4 = 1/2 because both equal 0.5. Multiply or divide numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number. *Quick check:* Write three fractions equivalent to 3/5.

**3. Converting between fractions, decimals and percentages.** Fraction → decimal: divide numerator by denominator (3/8 = 0.375). Decimal → percentage: multiply by 100 (0.375 = 37.5%). Percentage → fraction: write over 100 and simplify (37.5% = 375/1000 = 3/8). *Quick check:* Convert 7/20 to a decimal and a percentage.

**4. Mixed numbers and improper fractions.** A **mixed number** combines a whole number and a fraction: 2 3/4. An **improper fraction** has a numerator greater than or equal to the denominator: 11/4. They are equivalent: 2 3/4 = 11/4 because 2 × 4 + 3 = 11. *Quick check:* Convert 3 2/5 to an improper fraction.

**5. Negative rational numbers.** The number line extends left of zero. Negative fractions and decimals follow the same rules: −3/4 sits three-quarters of a unit left of zero. The further left, the smaller the value: −2.5 < −1.3 < 0 < 0.7. *Quick check:* Which is larger, −3/4 or −1/2?

**6. Worked example: placing numbers on a number line.**

A student needs to place −1.5, −3/4, 0, 2/3, 1.25 and 2 1/4 on a number line from −2 to 3.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Convert all to decimals: −1.5, −0.75, 0, 0.667, 1.25, 2.25.
- Mark equal intervals on the line (e.g. every 0.5 unit).
- Place each number at its position: −1.5 is halfway between −2 and −1; −0.75 is three-quarters left of 0; 2.25 is one-quarter past 2.
- Check order: −1.5 < −0.75 < 0 < 0.667 < 1.25 < 2.25. ✓

**7. Why equivalent representations matter.** Different forms suit different situations. Fractions are exact (1/3 stays exact, while 0.333... is rounded). Decimals are easy to compare and compute with. Percentages communicate proportions clearly. Choosing the right form is part of mathematical reasoning. *Quick check:* When would you prefer to use a fraction rather than a decimal?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Bureau of Meteorology (Melbourne headquarters) reports rainfall as decimals and temperature changes as positive and negative values — a daily encounter with rational numbers on a number line. VicHealth reports health statistics as percentages, fractions and rates. The MCG scoreboard uses decimals for batting averages and strike rates.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations seasonal calendars — such as the Wurundjeri seven-season calendar — divide the year into unequal parts, an intuitive application of fractional thinking. Sharing practices in many Aboriginal communities involve proportional division of food and resources, grounded in kinship obligations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Market traders in Melbourne's Queen Victoria Market use fractions (half a kilo), decimals ($3.50) and percentages (20% off) interchangeably — a living classroom of equivalent representations.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A recipe calls for 3/4 cup of flour. Your measuring cup shows millilitres. If 1 cup = 250 mL, how many millilitres is 3/4 cup? Express your answer as a decimal.
2. **Civic.** The City of Yarra reports that 37.5% of parkland has been revegetated. Write this as a fraction in simplest form and as a decimal. Place all three representations at the same point on a 0-to-1 number line.
3. **Vocational.** A baker in Footscray needs 2 1/3 kg of flour. The digital scale reads in decimals. What number should appear on the display? Convert 2 1/3 to an improper fraction as well.
4. **Ethical.** A classmate says −0.5 is smaller than −0.75 because 5 is smaller than 75. Explain why they are wrong using a number line.
5. **Cross-cultural.** You are comparing temperature records. Melbourne's coldest recorded temperature is −2.8 °C and Ballarat's is −4.6 °C. Place both on a number line and explain which city recorded the colder temperature.

## Memorable takeaways
- A rational number = integer ÷ non-zero integer.
- Fractions, decimals and percentages are three faces of the same value.
- Negative numbers sit left of zero — further left means smaller.
- Converting to a common form (usually decimals) makes ordering easy.
- Choose the representation that fits the context.

## Watch out for these traps
- "−0.5 is smaller than −0.75 because 5 < 75." On a number line, −0.5 is to the right of −0.75, so −0.5 > −0.75.
- "1/3 = 0.33." This is a rounded approximation. The exact decimal is 0.333... (repeating). Use the fraction when exactness matters.
- "3/4 and 6/12 are not equivalent." Simplify 6/12: divide both by 6 to get 1/2. Compare: 3/4 ≠ 1/2, so they are indeed not equivalent. But check your simplification — a common error is cancelling incorrectly.
- "Mixed numbers and improper fractions are different amounts." They are the same value in different forms: 2 3/4 = 11/4.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Is −2.5 rational?* Yes — it can be written as −5/2.
2. *Three fractions equivalent to 3/5.* 6/10, 9/15, 12/20.
3. *Convert 7/20.* 7 ÷ 20 = 0.35 = 35%.
4. *Convert 3 2/5 to an improper fraction.* 3 × 5 + 2 = 17, so 17/5.
5. *Which is larger, −3/4 or −1/2?* −1/2 is larger (closer to zero). On a number line, −0.5 is to the right of −0.75.
6. *When prefer a fraction?* When the decimal repeats (e.g. 1/3 rather than 0.333...) or when exactness is needed.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Recipe.* 3/4 × 250 = 187.5 mL.
2. *City of Yarra.* 37.5% = 3/8 = 0.375. All sit at the same point, three-eighths of the way from 0 to 1.
