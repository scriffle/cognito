# VC2M7SP03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7SP03. Describe the effect of transformations of a set of points using coordinates in the Cartesian plane, including translations, reflections in an axis, and rotations about the origin.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Space).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A transformation moves or changes a shape according to a precise rule.
  - Coordinates let you describe exactly where each point goes.
  - Translations slide, reflections flip, and rotations turn — each follows a predictable coordinate pattern.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Technologies (Digital).

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **describe the effect of transformations** on a set of points using **coordinates** in the Cartesian plane.
- You are learning the coordinate rules for **translations**, **reflections in an axis** and **rotations about the origin**.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Plot a shape on the Cartesian plane using coordinates.
- Apply a translation, reflection or rotation to each point.
- State the new coordinates after the transformation.
- Describe what changed and what stayed the same.

## Core content

**1. The Cartesian plane.** A grid with a horizontal **x-axis** and vertical **y-axis** meeting at the **origin** (0, 0). Every point is described by an ordered pair *(x, y)*. The plane has four **quadrants**. *Quick check:* In which quadrant is the point (−3, 5)?

**2. Translation (slide).** Every point moves the same distance in the same direction. If you translate by *(a, b)*, each point *(x, y)* becomes *(x + a, y + b)*. The shape does not rotate or flip — it just slides. Example: translate triangle with vertices (1, 2), (3, 2), (2, 5) by (4, −1) → (5, 1), (7, 1), (6, 4). *Quick check:* Translate the point (−2, 3) by (5, 2). What are the new coordinates?

**3. Reflection in the x-axis.** Each point flips across the horizontal axis: *(x, y)* becomes *(x, −y)*. The x-coordinate stays the same; the y-coordinate changes sign. *Quick check:* Reflect (4, 7) in the x-axis.

**4. Reflection in the y-axis.** Each point flips across the vertical axis: *(x, y)* becomes *(−x, y)*. The y-coordinate stays the same; the x-coordinate changes sign. *Quick check:* Reflect (−3, 2) in the y-axis.

**5. Rotation about the origin.** Common rotations: **90° anticlockwise** → *(x, y)* becomes *(−y, x)*. **180°** → *(x, y)* becomes *(−x, −y)*. **90° clockwise** (or 270° anticlockwise) → *(x, y)* becomes *(y, −x)*. The shape turns around the origin while keeping its size and shape. *Quick check:* Rotate (3, 1) by 90° anticlockwise about the origin.

**6. Worked example — street-art design on Hosier Lane.** A street artist plans a symmetrical mural using a coordinate grid. The original motif has corners at A(1, 1), B(4, 1), C(4, 3), D(1, 3) — a rectangle. Step 1: reflect in the y-axis → A′(−1, 1), B′(−4, 1), C′(−4, 3), D′(−1, 3). Step 2: translate the reflected copy down by (0, −5) → A″(−1, −4), B″(−4, −4), C″(−4, −2), D″(−1, −2). The mural now has four copies arranged symmetrically. *Follow the thinking:* plot the original → apply the rule to each vertex → plot the image → check that shape and size are preserved.

**7. What stays the same?** Translations, reflections and rotations are all **isometries** — they preserve side lengths and angles. The shape and size do not change; only position and/or orientation change. Reflection reverses the orientation (a clockwise path becomes anticlockwise). *Quick check:* After a translation, does the shape change size?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Hosier Lane street art, the tessellated pavements at the Royal Exhibition Building, the symmetrical design of Flinders Street Station's facade and the geometric patterns on Melbourne's tram livery all use transformations in their design. Game designers at Melbourne studios use coordinate transformations for character movement and animation.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal art across south-eastern Australia uses reflection and rotational symmetry in shield carvings, possum-skin cloak designs and meeting-ground layouts. Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung and Gunaikurnai designs show bilateral symmetry — the same transformation principles expressed in cultural form. AIATSIS publishes research on Indigenous spatial design. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Islamic geometric art relies heavily on reflections and rotations to generate complex tessellations from a single motif. Students from South-East Asian, Middle Eastern and South Asian backgrounds bring rich traditions of symmetrical pattern-making.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You design a logo on graph paper. The letter "V" has vertices at (0, 4), (2, 0) and (4, 4). Reflect it in the x-axis and state the new coordinates. Does the reflected shape still look like a "V"?
2. **Civic.** Darebin City Council commissions a mural with rotational symmetry. The original shape has a vertex at (2, 3). Find its image after 90° anticlockwise, 180° and 270° anticlockwise rotations about the origin.
3. **Vocational.** A CNC machinist programs a drill path starting at (5, 2). The path must be translated by (−8, 0) to mirror the part. What is the new starting coordinate?
4. **Ethical.** A game designer reflects a character sprite in the y-axis to create a "facing left" version. Discuss whether this changes the character's identity or just its orientation.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A possum-skin cloak design uses a motif at (1, 2), (3, 2), (2, 4). Reflect it in the y-axis and then translate by (0, −6). List the final coordinates and describe the overall pattern.

## Memorable takeaways
- Translation slides: *(x, y) → (x + a, y + b)*.
- Reflection in x-axis flips y: *(x, y) → (x, −y)*.
- Reflection in y-axis flips x: *(x, y) → (−x, y)*.
- Rotation 90° anticlockwise: *(x, y) → (−y, x)*.
- All three preserve shape and size (isometries).

## Stretch yourself
Design a four-part tile pattern on a Cartesian plane. Start with a simple shape (triangle or quadrilateral) in Quadrant I. Apply a reflection in the y-axis to create a copy in Quadrant II. Apply a reflection of the original in the x-axis to create a copy in Quadrant IV. Apply a 180° rotation of the original to create a copy in Quadrant III. List all vertex coordinates for all four copies. Describe the symmetry of the overall pattern. Is there a single transformation that maps the Quadrant I shape directly to the Quadrant III shape? Justify. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Reflection in the x-axis changes the x-coordinate." No — it changes the y-coordinate (flips up/down).
- "Rotation changes the size." Rotation about the origin preserves size and shape.
- "Translation changes orientation." Translation preserves both size and orientation — it only changes position.
- "90° clockwise is the same as 90° anticlockwise." They produce different images: clockwise *(x, y) → (y, −x)*; anticlockwise *(x, y) → (−y, x)*.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Quadrant for (−3, 5).* Quadrant II (x is negative, y is positive).
2. *Translate (−2, 3) by (5, 2).* (−2 + 5, 3 + 2) = (3, 5).
3. *Reflect (4, 7) in the x-axis.* (4, −7).
4. *Reflect (−3, 2) in the y-axis.* (3, 2).
5. *Rotate (3, 1) by 90° anticlockwise.* (−1, 3).
6. *Does translation change size?* No — translation is an isometry; shape and size are preserved.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"V" reflection.* (0, −4), (2, 0), (4, −4). The reflected shape is an upside-down V (like a caret ∧ flipped).
2. *Mural rotations.* 90° anticlockwise: (−3, 2). 180°: (−2, −3). 270° anticlockwise: (3, −2).
3. *CNC translation.* (5 + (−8), 2 + 0) = (−3, 2).
4. *Game sprite reflection.* The reflection changes orientation (left ↔ right) but preserves shape, proportions and features. It is a visual flip, not an identity change.
5. *Cloak design.* Reflect in y-axis: (−1, 2), (−3, 2), (−2, 4). Translate by (0, −6): (−1, −4), (−3, −4), (−2, −2). The pattern creates a vertically separated mirror pair.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Quadrant I: (1, 1), (3, 1), (2, 3). Quadrant II (y-axis reflection): (−1, 1), (−3, 1), (−2, 3). Quadrant IV (x-axis reflection): (1, −1), (3, −1), (2, −3). Quadrant III (180° rotation): (−1, −1), (−3, −1), (−2, −3). The pattern has both horizontal and vertical line symmetry and 180° rotational symmetry. The Quadrant III image can be obtained from Quadrant I by a single 180° rotation, because reflecting in both axes is equivalent to a 180° rotation.
