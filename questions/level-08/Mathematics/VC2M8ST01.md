## Core content

**1. Population and sample.** The **population** is the entire group you want to draw conclusions about — every student in your school, every household in Victoria, every fish in a lake. A **sample** is a smaller group selected from the population to represent it. We use samples because studying every member of a population is often impractical or impossible. *Quick check:* You want to know the average height of all Year 8 students in Victoria. What is the population? Why might you use a sample?

**2. Census.** A **census** collects data from every member of the population. Australia's national Census (run by the ABS every five years) attempts to survey every household. Advantage: complete data with no sampling error. Disadvantage: expensive, time-consuming, and sometimes impossible (you cannot catch every fish). *Quick check:* Why does Australia only run a national Census every five years, not every year?

**3. Sampling.** **Sampling** collects data from a subset of the population. If the sample is well chosen, it can represent the population accurately. Key types: **random sampling** (every member has an equal chance of selection), **systematic sampling** (every nth item), **stratified sampling** (divide into groups, sample proportionally from each). Poor sampling leads to **bias** — the sample does not reflect the population. *Quick check:* A school surveys only students who are in the library at lunchtime. Why might this sample be biased?

**4. Experiment.** An **experiment** deliberately changes one variable (the **independent variable**) and measures the effect on another (the **dependent variable**), while controlling other factors. Example: testing whether a new study method improves test scores. Experiments can show **cause and effect** — something surveys and observations alone cannot do. *Quick check:* What is the advantage of an experiment over a survey?

**5. Observation.** **Observation** involves watching and recording behaviour or events without interference. A traffic count at an intersection, bird-watching surveys, or timing how long customers wait in a queue. Advantage: captures natural behaviour. Disadvantage: observer may miss events, or their presence may change behaviour (the **Hawthorne effect**). *Quick check:* You observe how many students use the recycling bin at lunch. Why might your presence change the result?

**6. Worked example — choosing a technique for a school investigation.**
A Year 8 class wants to know whether students prefer the new canteen menu.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Population: all students at the school (say, 900).
- Census: survey all 900. Gives complete data but takes a long time to distribute, collect and enter.
- Sampling: randomly select 100 students (using student numbers and a random-number generator). Faster and cheaper; if truly random, results generalise well.
- Experiment: not applicable here — you are measuring preference, not testing a cause-and-effect intervention.
- Observation: you could count how many students buy canteen food before and after the change, but this does not directly measure preference.
- Decision: random sampling of 100 students via an online survey is practical, cost-effective and likely representative.

**7. Practicalities and implications.** Every technique involves trade-offs. Census gives certainty but costs more. Sampling is cheaper but introduces **sampling error** (the sample may not perfectly match the population). Experiments require careful design and ethical approval. Observation is unobtrusive but may miss context. Always state which technique you used and acknowledge its limitations. *Quick check:* Name one limitation of sampling that a census does not have.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS runs Australia's Census from offices in Melbourne and Geelong, employing thousands of collectors. VicHealth conducts population health surveys using stratified random sampling. Melbourne Water uses observational data (flow meters, weather stations) to manage water supply. CSIRO runs controlled experiments on agriculture, climate and health.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations communities have raised significant concerns about how government data collection (including the Census) represents Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. The concept of **Indigenous data sovereignty** — the right of communities to control how data about them is collected, stored and used — is central to organisations like the Maiam nayri Wingara Data Sovereignty Collective. Ethical data collection means genuine community involvement. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Census data reveals Victoria's diversity — languages spoken, countries of birth, religions. Students from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds can explore how sampling techniques affect whether their communities are accurately represented in data.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to know which streaming service is most popular among your friends (30 people). Would you use a census or a sample? Justify your choice.
2. **Civic.** The City of Maribyrnong wants to know how residents feel about a new bike path. They survey 200 randomly selected households. Identify the population, the sample, and one potential source of bias.
3. **Vocational.** A quality-control officer at a biscuit factory needs to check that biscuits weigh at least 25 g. Explain why sampling (not a census) is used and what type of sampling would be appropriate.
4. **Ethical.** A researcher wants to study the eating habits of teenagers. They propose observing students in the canteen without telling them. Discuss one ethical concern and suggest how to address it.
5. **Cross-cultural.** The ABS Census has been criticised for undercounting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in remote communities. Suggest two reasons why a census might miss people in remote areas and one way to improve accuracy.

## Memorable takeaways
- Population = the whole group. Sample = a subset chosen to represent it.
- Census: everyone, but expensive. Sampling: subset, but risk of bias.
- Experiment: change one variable, measure the effect — shows causation.
- Observation: watch without interfering — captures natural behaviour.
- Always state your technique, justify your choice, and acknowledge limitations.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Assuming a sample always represents the population.** Only well-designed samples generalise well. Biased samples can give misleading results.
- **Confusing experiment with observation.** Experiments deliberately change something; observations just watch what happens naturally.
- **Thinking bigger samples are always better.** A large biased sample is worse than a small random sample. Quality of selection matters more than size alone.
- **Ignoring ethics.** Data collection involving people requires informed consent, privacy protections, and consideration of potential harm.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Population and sample.* Population: all Year 8 students in Victoria (tens of thousands). A sample is used because surveying every student would be impractical and costly.
2. *Census every five years.* Cost (billions of dollars), logistics (millions of forms), and the time needed to process results make annual censuses impractical.
3. *Library-only sample.* Students in the library may be more studious or have different habits than the general student population — this is selection bias.
4. *Experiment advantage.* An experiment can establish cause and effect; a survey can only show association.
5. *Presence changing behaviour.* Students may use the bin more (or less) if they know they are being watched — the Hawthorne effect.
6. *Canteen investigation.* Random sample of 100 via online survey — practical, representative, acknowledged limitation: non-response bias if some students do not complete the survey.
7. *Sampling limitation.* Sampling error — the sample may not perfectly represent the population by chance.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Streaming service.* Census — 30 friends is small enough to ask everyone. No sampling needed.
2. *Bike path.* Population: all Maribyrnong residents. Sample: 200 households. Bias: only households with a landline or fixed address may be contacted; renters or non-English speakers may be under-represented.
3. *Biscuit factory.* A census would mean weighing every biscuit (destroying some in testing). Systematic sampling (every 50th biscuit off the line) is practical and non-destructive.
4. *Ethical concern.* Observing without consent raises privacy issues — students have not agreed to be studied. Address by: gaining informed consent (even if general), or using aggregated anonymous data.
5. *Census undercounting.* Reasons: remote locations are hard to access; cultural or language barriers may prevent completion. Improvement: employ local Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community members as Census collectors.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Population: all Year 8 students in Victoria (~80 000). Technique: stratified random sampling (strata by region: metro, regional, rural) to ensure geographic representation. Sample size: 400 (gives reasonable precision; larger is better but costly). Collection: anonymous online survey distributed through randomly selected schools. Ethical consideration: parental consent required for students under 18; anonymity protected. Limitations: (1) non-response bias if some schools or students do not participate; (2) self-reported breakfast habits may be inaccurate. A census would require surveying all ~80 000 students across hundreds of schools — logistically impractical and far more expensive for a single health question.
