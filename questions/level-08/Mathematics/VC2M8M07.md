# VC2M8M07 — Mathematics

## Snapshot

## Core content

**1. The modelling cycle (recap).** **Formulate** (set up the maths) → **Solve** (do the calculations) → **Interpret** (what does the answer mean?) → **Communicate** (explain clearly) → **Review** (is the model reasonable?). The review step is what separates good modelling from just doing sums. *Quick check:* What question should you ask at the "review" step?

**2. Distance-time problems.** For constant speed: **D = S × T** (distance = speed × time). Rearrange: S = D ÷ T, T = D ÷ S. "Constant speed" means the speed does not change during the journey. In real life, speed varies — the model assumes it is constant. *Quick check:* A train travels at 90 km/h for 2.5 hours. How far does it go?

**3. Multi-stage journeys.** When speed changes partway, calculate each stage separately, then add. A cyclist rides 12 km at 16 km/h, then 8 km at 12 km/h. Stage 1 time = 12 ÷ 16 = 0.75 h. Stage 2 time = 8 ÷ 12 = 0.667 h. Total time = 1.417 h ≈ 1 h 25 min. Total distance = 20 km. Average speed = 20 ÷ 1.417 ≈ 14.1 km/h (not the average of 16 and 12). *Quick check:* Why is the average speed not simply (16 + 12) ÷ 2 = 14 km/h?

**4. Financial rates — percentages.** A percentage is a rate "per hundred." 15% means 15 per 100. Discount: 20% off $85 → 0.20 × 85 = $17 discount → pay $68. Mark-up: 30% on $50 cost → 0.30 × 50 = $15 → sell at $65. GST: 10% added → multiply by 1.10. *Quick check:* A jacket costs $120. It is discounted by 25%. What do you pay?

**5. Simple interest.** **Simple interest** = Principal × Rate × Time: **I = PRT**. A student deposits $500 at 3% per year for 2 years: I = 500 × 0.03 × 2 = $30. Total after 2 years = $530. The rate must be expressed as a decimal (3% = 0.03) and time in the same unit as the rate (per year). *Quick check:* $1 000 at 4.5% p.a. for 3 years — how much interest?

**6. Worked example — modelling a road trip budget.**
A family drives from Melbourne to Ballarat (115 km) at an average speed of 80 km/h. Their car uses 9 L/100 km. Petrol is $1.92/L.

*Follow the thinking:*
- **Formulate:** Find travel time and fuel cost. Assume constant speed and constant fuel rate.
- **Solve (time):** T = D ÷ S = 115 ÷ 80 = 1.4375 h ≈ 1 h 26 min.
- **Solve (fuel):** Fuel = 9 × 115 ÷ 100 = 10.35 L. Cost = 10.35 × $1.92 = $19.87.
- **Interpret:** The trip takes about 1.5 hours and costs roughly $20 in fuel.
- **Review:** Constant 80 km/h is optimistic (traffic, speed zones). Actual time is likely longer. Fuel rate assumes highway driving — city sections use more. The model gives a lower-bound estimate.

**7. Reviewing the model.** Always ask: What assumptions did I make? Are they reasonable? What would change if I relaxed an assumption? For distance-time: constant speed is rarely exact. For finance: simple interest ignores compounding. Stating limitations honestly makes your modelling stronger, not weaker. *Quick check:* A model assumes constant speed for a city commute. Why might this be a poor assumption?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** V/Line and Metro Trains Melbourne publish timetables based on distance-time models. VicRoads speed data informs travel-time estimates on signs. The Victorian Government's First Home Owner Grant and stamp duty calculations are financial-rate models that affect thousands of Victorians. Melbourne Water uses rate models to forecast demand.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal corporations across Victoria manage budgets, apply for grants and track expenditure using financial-rate reasoning. First Nations businesses in tourism, land management and the arts apply percentage calculations to pricing, grant acquittals and wages. Understanding these models supports economic self-determination. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students from migrant backgrounds encounter financial rates in family contexts: comparing remittance services (fees as a percentage), understanding exchange rates, evaluating loan terms, and budgeting for small businesses. These are direct applications of the modelling cycle.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You cycle to school at 18 km/h. The distance is 4.5 km. On the way home, you average only 12 km/h because of headwind. Calculate the total time for both trips and your average speed for the round trip.
2. **Civic.** The City of Greater Geelong is comparing two road-repair quotes. Company A charges $45/m² and estimates 3 days. Company B charges $38/m² but estimates 5 days (with traffic disruption costing $2 000/day in lost trade). For a 200 m² job, which option costs the community less overall?
3. **Vocational.** A hairdressing apprentice earns $14.50/h for 38 hours/week. After completing their apprenticeship, the rate rises to $24.80/h. Calculate the weekly pay increase as a dollar amount and as a percentage of the apprentice rate.
4. **Ethical.** A store advertises "50% off, then a further 20% off the sale price." A customer assumes this means 70% off the original price. Show why the customer is wrong and calculate the actual total discount.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A family sends $500 AUD to relatives overseas. Service A charges a 3% fee with an exchange rate of 1 AUD = 32.5 local units. Service B charges no fee but offers 1 AUD = 31.0 local units. Which service delivers more local currency? Show your modelling.

## Memorable takeaways
- D = S × T — rearrange for any unknown.
- Average speed = total distance ÷ total time (not the average of speeds).
- Percentage calculations: multiply by the decimal (25% = 0.25).
- Simple interest: I = PRT.
- Always review: "Does this answer make sense? What did I assume?"

## Stretch yourself
A Year 8 student is saving for a $1 200 laptop. They earn $52 per week from a part-time job. They save 60% of their earnings each week and deposit it in a savings account earning 3.5% p.a. simple interest. Model: how many weeks until they have $1 200 (including interest)? Then the student finds the laptop discounted by 15%. Recalculate how many weeks are now needed. Finally, discuss: what assumptions does this model make, and what real-world factors could change the timeline? 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Averaging speeds directly.** Average speed is total distance ÷ total time, not (speed 1 + speed 2) ÷ 2. The time spent at each speed matters.
- **Applying successive discounts as a sum.** 50% off then 20% off is not 70% off — it is 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4 of the original, so 60% off.
- **Forgetting to convert percentage to decimal.** 4.5% is 0.045, not 4.5. This is the most common error in interest calculations.
- **Ignoring the review step.** A number that seems too large or too small probably is. Check your assumptions.

A note on certainty. The formulas (D = ST, I = PRT, percentage calculations) are exact for constant rates. In reality, speed varies, interest compounds, and prices change. A model is a simplification — its power lies in giving a useful estimate, and its honesty lies in acknowledging what it simplifies.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Review question.* "Does this answer make sense given the real situation? What did I assume?"
2. *Train distance.* D = 90 × 2.5 = 225 km.
3. *Average speed not (16+12)÷2.* Because more time is spent at the slower speed (longer stage takes longer), pulling the average below the midpoint.
4. *Discounted jacket.* 25% of $120 = $30. Pay $120 − $30 = $90.
5. *Simple interest.* I = 1 000 × 0.045 × 3 = $135.
6. *Road trip.* Time ≈ 1 h 26 min, fuel cost ≈ $19.87 (worked through in example).
7. *Constant speed in city.* Traffic lights, congestion and speed zones mean speed varies constantly — the model would underestimate travel time.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Cycling round trip.* To school: 4.5 ÷ 18 = 0.25 h. Home: 4.5 ÷ 12 = 0.375 h. Total = 0.625 h. Total distance = 9 km. Average speed = 9 ÷ 0.625 = 14.4 km/h.
2. *Road-repair quotes.* A: 200 × $45 = $9 000. B: 200 × $38 = $7 600 + 5 × $2 000 = $17 600. Company A is cheaper overall ($9 000 vs $17 600).
3. *Hairdressing pay.* Apprentice: 14.50 × 38 = $551. Qualified: 24.80 × 38 = $942.40. Increase = $391.40. Percentage = 391.40 ÷ 551 × 100 ≈ 71%.
4. *Successive discounts.* 50% off $100 = $50. Then 20% off $50 = $10. Final price = $40. Actual discount = 60%, not 70%.
5. *Remittance.* A: (500 − 3% fee) × 32.5 = 485 × 32.5 = 15 762.50 local units. B: 500 × 31.0 = 15 500 local units. Service A delivers more.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Weekly savings = 60% × $52 = $31.20. Weeks without interest: 1 200 ÷ 31.20 ≈ 38.5 weeks. With interest (small, since deposits are weekly): approximate total interest over ~38 weeks on average balance of ~$600 for ~0.73 years: I ≈ 600 × 0.035 × 0.73 ≈ $15.33. Reduces weeks to about 38. With 15% discount: target = $1 020. Weeks ≈ 1 020 ÷ 31.20 ≈ 32.7, so about 33 weeks. Assumptions: constant earnings, no other expenses from savings, stable interest rate, laptop price unchanged. Real factors: irregular shifts, unexpected costs, interest compounding (not simple), possible further price changes.
