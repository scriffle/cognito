## What you are learning
- You are learning to **solve problems involving the area and perimeter of irregular and composite shapes** using appropriate units.
- You are learning to decompose complex shapes into rectangles, triangles and other familiar figures.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify component shapes within a composite figure.
- Calculate area by adding (or subtracting) the areas of component shapes.
- Calculate perimeter by identifying and summing all outer edges.
- Choose appropriate units (mm², cm², m², km²) for context.

## Core content

**1. What is a composite shape?** A **composite shape** is made by joining two or more simple shapes (rectangles, triangles, semicircles, etc.) or by cutting one shape from another. An L-shaped room, a house cross-section with a triangular roof, or a swimming pool with a semicircular end are all composite shapes. *Quick check:* Name two simple shapes that form an L-shape.

**2. Area by addition.** Split the composite shape into non-overlapping simple shapes. Calculate each area separately, then add. Example: an L-shape can be split into two rectangles. If Rectangle 1 is 8 m × 3 m = 24 m² and Rectangle 2 is 5 m × 4 m = 20 m², total area = 44 m². *Quick check:* A T-shaped garden is made of a 10 m × 2 m rectangle and a 4 m × 6 m rectangle. What is the total area?

**3. Area by subtraction.** Sometimes it is easier to start with a larger shape and subtract a cut-out. A rectangular card (20 cm × 15 cm) with a square hole (5 cm × 5 cm) cut from the centre: area = 300 − 25 = 275 cm². *Quick check:* A 12 m × 8 m rectangle has a 3 m × 3 m square cut from one corner. What is the remaining area?

**4. Finding missing lengths.** Before calculating, you often need to deduce missing side lengths from the given dimensions. If the overall width is 10 m and one section is 6 m, the other must be 4 m. Label everything before calculating. *Quick check:* An L-shape is 12 m wide overall. The left section is 5 m wide. How wide is the right section?

**5. Perimeter of composite shapes.** Walk around the outside of the shape and add every edge. Include indented edges that face inward. A common error is forgetting internal edges that form part of the perimeter of an indented shape. *Quick check:* An L-shaped room has outer dimensions 10 m × 8 m with a 4 m × 3 m rectangle removed from one corner. What is the perimeter?

**6. Worked example: area and perimeter of a school courtyard.**

A school courtyard is shaped like a rectangle (20 m × 12 m) with a triangular alcove (base 6 m, height 4 m) attached to one end.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Decompose: one rectangle + one triangle.
- Area of rectangle = 20 × 12 = 240 m².
- Area of triangle = ½ × 6 × 4 = 12 m².
- Total area = 240 + 12 = 252 m².
- Perimeter: trace the outside. The rectangle contributes three full sides (12 + 20 + 12 = 44 m). The fourth side of the rectangle is partially replaced by two slant sides of the triangle. Remaining rectangle edge = 20 − 6 = 14 m. Triangle slant sides: use Pythagoras — each slant = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5 m. Two slants = 10 m.
- Perimeter = 44 + 14 + 10 = 68 m.

**7. Choosing appropriate units.** A postage stamp: mm². A room: m². A farm paddock: hectares (10 000 m²) or km². Match the unit to the context and convert when needed (1 m² = 10 000 cm²). *Quick check:* What unit would you use for the area of a basketball court?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your bedroom is L-shaped: one section is 4 m × 3 m and the other is 5 m × 3 m. Calculate the total floor area and the perimeter (for skirting boards). If carpet costs $35 per m², find the total carpet cost.
2. **Civic.** The City of Darebin is building a new playground on an irregular block shaped like a rectangle (30 m × 18 m) with a triangular extension (base 10 m, height 8 m). Calculate the total area and the fencing needed (perimeter).
3. **Vocational.** A landscaper in the Yarra Valley quotes for paving a courtyard shaped like a large rectangle (8 m × 6 m) minus a circular garden bed (radius 1.5 m). Calculate the paving area. If pavers cost $45 per m², estimate the materials cost.
4. **Ethical.** A property listing says a block is "approximately 650 m²." You calculate the composite area as 612 m². Is "approximately 650" reasonable or misleading? Discuss the role of rounding and transparency.
5. **Cross-cultural.** You are helping design a community garden at a multicultural centre. The space is a trapezium (parallel sides 12 m and 8 m, height 6 m) with a 2 m × 2 m shed in one corner. Calculate the usable garden area.

## Memorable takeaways
- Split composite shapes into rectangles, triangles and semicircles.
- Area: add component areas (or subtract cut-outs).
- Perimeter: trace every outer edge — do not miss indented sections.
- Find missing lengths before calculating.
- Match units to context: mm², cm², m², km².

## Watch out for these traps
- "I forgot the internal edges on the perimeter." In an L-shape, the two edges facing the cut-out are part of the perimeter. Trace the whole boundary.
- "I used the wrong decomposition and double-counted." Check that your component shapes do not overlap. If they do, subtract the overlap.
- "Area in cm and metres mixed." Convert all measurements to the same unit before calculating. 150 cm = 1.5 m.
- "Perimeter and area use the same units." Perimeter is measured in length units (m); area is in squared units (m²).
