# VC2M8ST01 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Distinguish between a population and a sample, and investigate techniques for data collection including census, sampling, experiment and observation, and explain the practicalities and implications of obtaining data through these techniques.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** A population is the whole group; a sample is a subset you study. Census, sampling, experiment and observation each have strengths and limitations. Your technique affects data quality, cost and ethics.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **distinguish population from sample** and to understand four **data-collection techniques**: census, sampling, experiment and observation.
- You should be able to explain practical trade-offs and ethical considerations.

## Core content

The **population** is the entire group you want conclusions about. A **sample** is a smaller subset chosen to represent it. Studying everyone is often too costly or impossible, so we sample.

**Census** — data from every member. Complete but expensive (Australia's Census costs billions and runs only every five years). **Sampling** — data from a subset. Types: random (equal chance), systematic (every nth), stratified (proportional from subgroups). Risk: **bias** if the sample does not reflect the population. **Experiment** — deliberately change one variable and measure the effect. Can show **cause and effect**. **Observation** — watch and record without interfering. Captures natural behaviour but may miss context.

**Worked example.** A school wants to know canteen preferences (900 students). Census: survey all 900 — thorough but slow. Random sample of 100 via online survey — faster, cheaper, and generalisable if truly random. Experiment: not applicable (no intervention). Observation: count purchases, but this misses preference. Best choice: random sampling.

**Victorian context.** The ABS runs Australia's Census from Melbourne. VicHealth uses stratified sampling for health surveys. The concept of **Indigenous data sovereignty** — communities controlling how data about them is collected — is central to ethical practice.

## Memorable takeaways
- Population = whole group. Sample = subset.
- Census: everyone, expensive. Sampling: subset, risk of bias.
- Experiment: shows cause and effect. Observation: natural behaviour.
- Always state your technique and acknowledge limitations.

## Watch out for these traps
- Assuming any sample represents the population — only well-designed samples do.
- Confusing experiment with observation — experiments change something; observations just watch.
- Thinking bigger samples are always better — a large biased sample is worse than a small random one.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why use a sample instead of a census?* The population is too large, costly or impractical to study completely.
- *Library-only survey — biased?* Yes — library users may differ from the general student body.
- *Experiment vs survey advantage.* Experiments establish causation; surveys show association only.
- *Biscuit factory — why sample?* Testing every biscuit is impractical (and destructive); systematic sampling is efficient.
- *One limitation of sampling a census avoids.* Sampling error — the sample may not perfectly match the population.
