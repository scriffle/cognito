# VC2M7M05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7M05. Demonstrate that the interior angle sum of a triangle in the plane is 180° and apply this to determine the interior angle sum of other shapes and the size of unknown angles.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Measurement and Geometry).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The interior angles of any triangle on a flat surface add to exactly 180°.
  - Any polygon can be split into triangles, giving a formula for its angle sum.
  - This lets you find unknown angles by reasoning rather than measuring.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Design and Technologies.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that the **interior angle sum of a triangle is 180°** and how to **extend this to other polygons**.
- You are learning to calculate unknown angles using the angle-sum property.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Demonstrate that the angles in a triangle sum to 180°.
- State and use the formula for the interior angle sum of any polygon: (n − 2) × 180°.
- Calculate unknown angles in triangles and other shapes.
- Explain your reasoning step by step.

## Core content

**1. The triangle angle sum.** Draw any triangle on a flat surface. Tear off the three corners and arrange them on a straight line — they fit exactly, forming 180°. This works for every triangle: equilateral, isosceles, scalene, right-angled. The **interior angle sum of a triangle is 180°**. *Quick check:* A triangle has angles of 50° and 70°. What is the third angle?

**2. Why 180°?** Draw a triangle and extend one side to create an exterior angle. Using the parallel-line rules from VC2M7M04, you can prove that the two non-adjacent interior angles equal the exterior angle. Since the exterior angle and its adjacent interior angle sit on a straight line (180°), the three interior angles must also sum to 180°. *Quick check:* An exterior angle of a triangle is 120°. The two non-adjacent interior angles are 45° and ___°.

**3. Types of triangles by angle.** **Acute** — all angles less than 90°. **Right-angled** — one angle exactly 90°, so the other two sum to 90°. **Obtuse** — one angle greater than 90°, so the other two sum to less than 90°. *Quick check:* Can a triangle have two obtuse angles? Explain.

**4. Angle sum of quadrilaterals.** A quadrilateral can be split into **two triangles** by drawing one diagonal. Two triangles × 180° = **360°**. *Quick check:* A quadrilateral has angles 90°, 90° and 110°. What is the fourth angle?

**5. Angle sum of any polygon.** A polygon with **n** sides can be split into **(n − 2) triangles**. So its interior angle sum = **(n − 2) × 180°**. Pentagon (5 sides): (5 − 2) × 180° = 540°. Hexagon (6 sides): (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°. *Quick check:* What is the interior angle sum of an octagon?

**6. Worked example — finding an unknown angle in a Victorian park shelter.**
A park shelter in the Royal Botanic Gardens has a pentagonal cross-section. Four of its interior angles are 108°, 108°, 120° and 95°. What is the fifth angle?

*Follow the thinking:*
- Angle sum of a pentagon = (5 − 2) × 180° = 540°.
- Sum of the four known angles: 108 + 108 + 120 + 95 = 431°.
- Unknown angle = 540° − 431° = 109°.
- Check: 431 + 109 = 540°. ✓

**7. Regular polygons.** In a **regular polygon**, all sides and all angles are equal. Each interior angle = (n − 2) × 180° ÷ n. For a regular hexagon: 720° ÷ 6 = 120°. For a regular octagon: 1080° ÷ 8 = 135°. *Quick check:* What is each interior angle of a regular pentagon?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The hexagonal bluestone pavers on Melbourne's laneways, the octagonal bandstand in the Fitzroy Gardens, and the triangular trusses of the MCG roof all demonstrate polygon angle sums in built form. Engineers at AMSI teach structural stability through triangulation — the triangle is the only rigid polygon.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Triangular and polygonal forms appear in First Nations shield designs, woven fish traps and stone arrangements. The stone houses at Budj Bim on Gunditjmara Country use angular stone placement to create stable enclosures. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Tessellation patterns in Islamic art — common in Melbourne mosque architecture — rely on polygon angle sums to tile a surface without gaps. Students can explore how regular polygons with interior angles that divide evenly into 360° create tessellations.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You are building a picture frame. You cut four pieces of wood. Three corners measure 90° each. What must the fourth corner be for the frame to close properly?
2. **Civic.** The City of Melbourne is designing a hexagonal garden bed for Federation Square. If five of the six angles are each 115°, what must the sixth angle be?
3. **Vocational.** A tiler is laying regular octagonal tiles with small square tiles filling the gaps. Explain why this combination works by calculating the angles at the meeting point.
4. **Ethical.** A student measures a triangle's angles with a protractor and gets 58°, 63° and 61°, totalling 182°. They claim the angle-sum rule is wrong. What is a more likely explanation?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Investigate which regular polygons tessellate (tile a flat surface with no gaps). Explain your answer using the interior angle sum formula.

## Memorable takeaways
- Triangle interior angles sum to 180° — always.
- Polygon angle sum = (n − 2) × 180°.
- Quadrilateral: 360°. Pentagon: 540°. Hexagon: 720°.
- Regular polygon angle = (n − 2) × 180° ÷ n.
- The triangle is the building block — every polygon breaks into triangles.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Assuming the formula works for non-planar shapes.** (n − 2) × 180° is for flat (Euclidean) surfaces only. On a sphere, a triangle's angles can sum to more than 180°.
- **Confusing interior and exterior angles.** The interior angle is inside the polygon; the exterior angle is the supplement. They are not the same thing.
- **Miscounting sides.** When splitting into triangles, draw diagonals from one vertex only — the number of triangles is always n − 2.
- **Rounding errors compounding.** If each measured angle is slightly off, the total error grows. This does not disprove the rule — it shows the limits of measurement.

## Try drawing this
Draw a triangle, a quadrilateral, a pentagon and a hexagon in a row. In each shape, draw all diagonals from one vertex. Write the number of triangles formed and the total angle sum beneath each shape. Connect them with arrows to show the pattern.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Third angle of 50°, 70° triangle.* 180° − 50° − 70° = 60°.
2. *Non-adjacent interior angle.* 120° − 45° = 75°.
3. *Two obtuse angles?* No. Two angles each greater than 90° would already exceed 180°, leaving no room for a third positive angle.
4. *Fourth angle of quadrilateral.* 360° − 90° − 90° − 110° = 70°.
5. *Octagon angle sum.* (8 − 2) × 180° = 1080°.
6. *Pentagonal shelter.* Fifth angle = 540° − 431° = 109° (worked through in the example).
7. *Regular pentagon angle.* 540° ÷ 5 = 108°.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Picture frame.* 360° − 3 × 90° = 90°. The fourth corner must be 90° — a rectangle.
2. *Hexagonal garden bed.* 720° − 5 × 115° = 720° − 575° = 145°.
3. *Octagonal and square tiles.* Regular octagon angle = 135°. At the meeting point: 135° + 135° + 90° = 360°. The square fills the gap exactly.
4. *Protractor measurement.* Measurement error — each angle may be off by a degree or so. The rule holds; the protractor has limited precision.
5. *Tessellating regular polygons.* Only equilateral triangles (60° × 6 = 360°), squares (90° × 4 = 360°) and regular hexagons (120° × 3 = 360°). No other regular polygon has an interior angle that divides evenly into 360°.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Regular hexagon interior angle = 120°. Equilateral triangle interior angle = 60°. At a vertex: 120° + 120° + 60° + 60° = 360° — no gap. Regular pentagon interior angle = 108°. 108° does not combine with itself or with 60° to reach exactly 360° (e.g. 108° × 3 = 324°, leaving 36°, which is not 60° or 108°). So pentagons cannot replace hexagons in this tessellation. Diagram shows hexagons with triangles filling the spaces at each vertex.
