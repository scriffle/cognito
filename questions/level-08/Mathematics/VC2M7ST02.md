# VC2M7ST02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7ST02. Create different types of displays of numerical data, including dot plots and stem-and-leaf plots, using software where appropriate; describe and compare the distribution of data, commenting on the shape, centre and spread including outliers and determining the range, median, mean and mode.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Statistics and Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A good display makes the shape, centre and spread of data visible at a glance.
  - Dot plots and stem-and-leaf plots show every data value while revealing the distribution.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **create dot plots and stem-and-leaf plots** and to **describe and compare distributions** using shape, centre and spread.
- You are learning to choose a display that makes the story in the data clear.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Create a dot plot and a stem-and-leaf plot from a data set.

## Core content

**1. Why display data?** A list of numbers is hard to read. A display organises data so patterns — clusters, gaps, peaks, outliers — jump out. Different displays suit different data sets and different questions. *Quick check:* Name one advantage a graph has over a raw list of numbers.

**2. Dot plots.** A **dot plot** places a dot above a number line for each data value. Repeated values stack vertically. Dot plots work well for small to medium data sets with discrete values or values rounded to convenient intervals. They show every individual value and make clusters and outliers visible. *Quick check:* In a dot plot, how can you tell which value is the mode?

**3. Stem-and-leaf plots.** A **stem-and-leaf plot** splits each value into a **stem** (the leading digit or digits) and a **leaf** (the last digit). Leaves are written in order from smallest to largest on each row. This creates a sideways histogram that retains every original value. For example, the value 47 has stem 4 and leaf 7. A **key** must be included (e.g. 4 | 7 means 47). *Quick check:* In a stem-and-leaf plot with key 3 | 5 = 35, how would you record the value 38?

**4. Shape of a distribution.** **Symmetric** — roughly mirror-image either side of the centre. **Positively (right) skewed** — tail stretches to the right (a few high values). **Negatively (left) skewed** — tail stretches to the left (a few low values). **Uniform** — values are roughly evenly spread. Look for **clusters** (groups of data), **gaps** (empty spaces) and **peaks** (modes). *Quick check:* House prices in a suburb are clustered around $600 000 with a few sales above $2 000 000. Is this distribution symmetric, positively skewed or negatively skewed?

**5. Centre and spread from a display.** Read the median from a dot plot by counting to the middle dot. Read it from a stem-and-leaf plot by counting leaves. The mean can be estimated from the display or calculated from the values. The range is max − min. **Outliers** are values that sit far from the main cluster — they affect the mean more than the median. *Quick check:* A dot plot has 15 dots. Which dot (counting from the left) is the median?

**6. Worked example — comparing two Melbourne suburbs' daily maximum temperatures.**
January daily maxima (°C) for two suburbs:

Suburb A (inner): 25, 26, 27, 27, 28, 28, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 34, 38, 40, 42.
Suburb B (coastal): 22, 23, 24, 24, 25, 25, 25, 26, 26, 27, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Create a back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot (stems shared in the middle):

```
     Suburb A | Stem | Suburb B
           5  |  2   | 2 3 4 4 5 5 5 6 6 7 7 8 9
     8 8 8 7 7 6 |  2   |
       2 1 0 9  |  3   | 0 1
           2 0  |  4   |
```

- Suburb A: median = 29, mean ≈ 31.0, range = 42 − 25 = 17. Positively skewed (tail to the right with 38, 40, 42).
- Suburb B: median = 26, mean ≈ 26.1, range = 31 − 22 = 9. Roughly symmetric.
- Suburb A is hotter on average, more spread out, and skewed by extreme heat days. Suburb B is cooler and more consistent.

**7. Choosing a display.** Dot plots are best for small discrete data sets where individual values matter. Stem-and-leaf plots handle larger numerical data and show shape clearly. Bar charts suit categorical data. Histograms group continuous data into intervals. Use software (spreadsheets, online tools) for large data sets, but always check the display makes sense. *Quick check:* Would you use a dot plot or a stem-and-leaf plot for 50 test scores out of 100? Why?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You record the number of text messages you send each day for two weeks. Create a dot plot. Describe the shape, centre and spread of your data.
2. **Civic.** The City of Brimbank publishes the number of library visitors per day over a month. Create a stem-and-leaf plot from the data and identify whether the distribution is symmetric or skewed. Suggest what might cause any outliers.
3. **Vocational.** A bakery records the number of loaves sold each day for 20 days. Create a dot plot and calculate the mean and median. The owner wants to know how many loaves to bake on a typical day — which measure do you recommend?
4. **Ethical.** A news report says "average screen time for teens is 7 hours." The data set includes a few students at 14+ hours. Discuss how the choice of display and summary statistic could make this claim misleading.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Collect data from your class on the number of languages spoken at home. Create a dot plot. Compare the shape of the distribution to what you would expect in a less multilingual community.

## Memorable takeaways
- Dot plots show every value — look for clusters, gaps and outliers.
- Stem-and-leaf plots retain all values and reveal shape like a sideways histogram.
- Describe distributions with three words: shape, centre, spread.
- Outliers affect the mean more than the median.
- Always include a key on a stem-and-leaf plot.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting to order the leaves.** Stem-and-leaf plots require leaves in ascending order on each row — unordered leaves make the shape misleading.
- **Leaving out the key.** Without a key (e.g. 4 | 7 = 47), no one can read your stem-and-leaf plot.
- **Confusing shape description.** A distribution with a tail to the right is positively skewed (not "skewed right to left"). The tail names the skew direction.
- **Ignoring outliers in comparison.** When comparing two data sets, note whether outliers are pulling one mean away from its median.
