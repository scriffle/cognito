## Core content

**1. Simple vs compound events.** A **simple event** has one stage: rolling a single die, flipping one coin. A **compound event** combines two or more stages: rolling a die *and* flipping a coin, drawing two cards in succession. The total number of outcomes multiplies: 6 outcomes × 2 outcomes = 12 compound outcomes. *Quick check:* How many outcomes are there when you flip two coins?

**2. Listing outcomes systematically.** Use a **tree diagram** or a **two-way table** to list every possible outcome without missing any. For two coins: HH, HT, TH, TT — four outcomes. A tree diagram branches at each stage; a two-way table uses rows for one event and columns for the other. *Quick check:* Draw a two-way table for rolling a die and flipping a coin. How many cells does it have?

**3. Theoretical probability of a compound event.** P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. For two coins, P(exactly one head) = 2/4 = 0.5 (the outcomes HT and TH). For a die and a coin, P(even number and heads) = 3/12 = 0.25 (the outcomes 2H, 4H, 6H out of 12). *Quick check:* Two dice are rolled. What is P(double six)?

**4. Why we simulate.** Some compound events are hard to calculate theoretically — many stages, unequal probabilities, dependent events. A simulation runs the experiment digitally many times and counts how often the event occurs. The experimental probability = (number of times event occurred) ÷ (number of trials). *Quick check:* Before running a simulation of 100 coin flips, predict how many heads you expect. Why might the actual count differ?

**5. Running a simulation — spreadsheet or code.** In a spreadsheet: use =RANDBETWEEN(1,6) for a die roll, =RANDBETWEEN(0,1) for a coin flip. Copy down for hundreds of trials. Count successes using =COUNTIF(). In Python:

```python
import random
successes = 0
trials = 10000
for i in range(trials):
    die = random.randint(1, 6)
    coin = random.choice(["H", "T"])
    if die >= 5 and coin == "H":
        successes += 1
print(f"P(die ≥ 5 and heads) ≈ {successes / trials:.4f}")
```

Theoretical P(die ≥ 5 and heads) = 2/12 ≈ 0.1667. With 10,000 trials, the simulation result will be close to this. *Quick check:* Why does the simulation get closer to the theoretical value as you increase the number of trials?

**6. Worked example — compound-event simulation with convergence.**

You want to find P(rolling a sum of 7 with two dice).

*Theoretical calculation:* The outcomes that give a sum of 7 are: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) — six outcomes out of 36 total. P(sum of 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.1667.

*Simulation in Python:*

```python
import random
successes = 0
trials_list = [10, 100, 1000, 10000]
for n in trials_list:
    successes = sum(
        1 for _ in range(n)
        if random.randint(1,6) + random.randint(1,6) == 7
    )
    print(f"{n:>6} trials: P(sum=7) ≈ {successes/n:.4f}")
```

*Follow the thinking:*
- At 10 trials, the result might be far from 0.1667 — perhaps 0.3000 or 0.1000.
- At 100 trials, it typically falls between 0.12 and 0.22.
- At 1,000 trials, it narrows to roughly 0.15–0.19.
- At 10,000 trials, it is usually within 0.01 of the theoretical value.
- This pattern — experimental probability converging on theoretical probability — is the **long-run frequency** effect.

**7. Describing results.** When you report simulation results, state: the event, the number of trials, the experimental probability, the theoretical probability (if known), and how close they are. Use language like "the experimental probability of ___ was approximately ___, based on ___ trials, which is close to the theoretical probability of ___." *Quick check:* You simulate 500 trials of flipping three coins and get 60 occurrences of three heads. What is the experimental probability? What is the theoretical probability?

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting that order matters.** HT and TH are different outcomes. A tree diagram helps you see this.
- **Adding probabilities when you should multiply.** For independent compound events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B), not P(A) + P(B).
- **Expecting experimental results to match theory exactly.** Small samples produce noisy results. Convergence takes many trials.
- **Assuming the gambler's fallacy.** Past results do not change future probabilities for independent events. After five heads, the next flip is still 50/50.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Two coins.* 2 × 2 = 4 outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT.
2. *Die and coin two-way table.* 6 rows × 2 columns = 12 cells.
3. *P(double six).* 1 outcome (6,6) out of 36 total = 1/36 ≈ 0.0278.
4. *Prediction for 100 coin flips.* Expect about 50 heads. The actual count differs due to random variation — each flip is independent.
5. *Why simulation improves with more trials.* Random variation averages out over large numbers (law of large numbers). Extreme results become proportionally less influential.
6. *Three coins, 500 trials, 60 three-heads.* Experimental: 60/500 = 0.12. Theoretical: 1/8 = 0.125. Close — within 0.005.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Sum of 7.* In a 6×6 two-way table, sum = 7 occurs in 6 cells: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1). Sum = 12 occurs in only 1 cell: (6,6). More combinations produce 7, so it is more likely.
2. *Raffle.* P(win major) = 5/200. P(not win major) = 195/200. Given you did not win major, P(win minor) = 5/199. P(at least one) = 1 − P(win neither) = 1 − (195/200 × 194/199) ≈ 1 − 0.9511 ≈ 0.049 or about 4.9 %.
3. *Factory defects.* P(both defective) = 0.03 × 0.05 = 0.0015. Simulation with 10,000 trials should produce roughly 15 successes, giving an experimental probability near 0.0015.
4. *Phone app.* P(lose 30 times) = (2/3)^30 ≈ 0.0000052 — about 5 in a million. It is possible but extremely unlikely. "1 in 3 chance" means each independent play has a 1/3 probability — it does not guarantee a win within any fixed number of plays.
5. *Traditional game.* Example: a stick-guessing game where one player hides a marked stick among several and the other guesses which hand or bundle it is in. If there are 4 bundles and two rounds, the compound probability of guessing correctly both times is (1/4) × (1/4) = 1/16 = 0.0625. The game involves compound-event thinking because the overall outcome depends on the result of multiple independent guesses.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Event: drawing two red cards without replacement from a 52-card deck. Theoretical: P(first red) = 26/52 = 1/2. P(second red | first red) = 25/51. P(both) = (26/52) × (25/51) = 650/2652 ≈ 0.2451. Simulation (Python, 5,000 trials) returned 0.2438, 0.2470, 0.2462 across three runs — all within 0.002 of theory. At 100,000 trials, variation would shrink further because the law of large numbers ensures convergence. The key is that without replacement makes the events dependent, so P(B|A) differs from P(B).
