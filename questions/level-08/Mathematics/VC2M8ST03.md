# VC2M8ST03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8ST03. Compare variations in distributions and proportions obtained from random samples of the same size drawn from a population and recognise the effect of sample size on this variation.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Statistics and Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Different random samples from the same population will give different results — this is sampling variability.
  - Larger samples produce less variability between samples and more closely match the population.
  - Understanding sampling variability is the foundation of statistical inference.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; ICT Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **compare variations between random samples** of the same size and to **recognise how sample size affects this variation**.
- You are learning why no single sample perfectly represents the population.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Demonstrate that repeated random samples give different results.
- Compare distributions and proportions across multiple samples.
- Explain that larger samples produce less variation between samples.
- Use this understanding to judge how much trust to place in a single sample.

## Core content

**1. Sampling variability.** If you take a random sample and calculate the mean, then take another random sample of the same size and calculate the mean again, the two means will almost certainly differ. This natural fluctuation is called **sampling variability**. It is not a mistake — it is a fundamental feature of working with samples. *Quick check:* You take two random samples of 10 students and ask their heights. Will the two sample means be identical? Why or why not?

**2. Repeating the sampling process.** Imagine a jar of 200 coloured marbles: 60% blue, 40% red. You draw samples of 10 and record the proportion of blue. Sample 1: 7/10 = 70%. Sample 2: 5/10 = 50%. Sample 3: 6/10 = 60%. The proportions vary around the true population value (60%) but do not match it exactly every time. *Quick check:* In the marble example, which sample proportion is closest to the true population proportion?

**3. Comparing distributions across samples.** Take multiple samples of size 20 from a population and create a dot plot or histogram for each. The shapes will be similar but not identical. Centres (means, medians) will cluster near the population mean but spread out. The spread of the sample means is the **sampling distribution** — a distribution of statistics, not of individual values. *Quick check:* If 10 teams each take a random sample of 20 and calculate the mean, will all 10 means be the same?

**4. The effect of sample size.** **Larger samples have less variability.** With n = 10, sample proportions might range from 40% to 80%. With n = 50, they might range from 50% to 70%. With n = 200, they cluster tightly around 60%. This happens because larger samples better represent the population — extreme results "average out." *Quick check:* Which gives a more reliable estimate of the population mean: a sample of 15 or a sample of 150?

**5. Why this matters.** If a poll of 50 people says 52% support a policy, that could easily be 48% or 56% in another sample of the same size. But a poll of 1 000 people saying 52% is much more stable. Understanding variability helps you judge whether a difference is meaningful or just due to chance. *Quick check:* A poll of 30 people finds 60% prefer brand A. How confident should you be that the whole population prefers brand A?

**6. Worked example — simulating samples from a school population.**
A school has 400 students. The true mean daily screen time is 3.2 hours. A student takes five random samples of size 20 and calculates the mean of each:

Sample 1: mean = 3.0 h. Sample 2: mean = 3.5 h. Sample 3: mean = 2.8 h. Sample 4: mean = 3.3 h. Sample 5: mean = 3.1 h.

*Follow the thinking:*
- The five means range from 2.8 to 3.5 — a spread of 0.7 hours.
- The average of the five means = (3.0 + 3.5 + 2.8 + 3.3 + 3.1) ÷ 5 = 3.14 h — close to the true mean of 3.2.
- If the student repeated with samples of size 100, the means would cluster more tightly around 3.2 (perhaps ranging from 3.0 to 3.4).
- Conclusion: individual samples vary, but larger samples vary less. Taking multiple samples and averaging brings you closer to the truth.

**7. Proportions and sample size.** The same principle applies to proportions (percentages). A sample of 20 students might find 40% walk to school. Another sample of 20 might find 55%. With samples of 100, results might cluster between 45% and 52%. The true proportion "settles down" as sample size increases. *Quick check:* A sample of 10 finds 30% left-handed. A sample of 200 finds 12% left-handed. Which is more likely to reflect the true population proportion?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS uses large samples (tens of thousands) for the Labour Force Survey to minimise sampling variability and produce reliable monthly employment estimates. VicHealth's population surveys are designed with sample sizes calculated to give specific levels of precision. Polling companies like Newspoll report margins of error based on sample size — typically ±3% for n ≈ 1 000. AMSI education resources include sampling simulations.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Small sample sizes in health and education surveys have historically led to unreliable data about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities — results vary wildly between surveys due to sampling variability, yet are sometimes reported as definitive. Understanding the effect of sample size helps students critically evaluate claims made about small populations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Nationally representative surveys must have large enough subsamples to capture minority communities accurately. Students can explore how the ABS ensures that smaller community groups are not lost in aggregated statistics through stratification and oversampling.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads (70%). Your friend flips 100 times and gets 53 heads (53%). Which result is more surprising? Which is a more reliable estimate of the true probability of heads?
2. **Civic.** A local council polls 50 residents and finds 54% support a new library. The opposition says this is "basically a coin flip." Explain, using sampling variability, why a sample of 50 gives less certainty than a sample of 500.
3. **Vocational.** A quality-control team tests 5 bolts from a batch and finds one defective (20%). They test 100 bolts and find 3 defective (3%). Explain why the smaller sample gave a misleading result.
4. **Ethical.** A newspaper reports "Youth crime up 15%" based on one month's data from a single suburb. Discuss how small sample size (one month, one location) might make this statistic unreliable due to natural variability.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Design a simulation: place 30 red and 20 blue counters in a bag (60% red). Draw samples of 5, 10 and 20 (with replacement) and record the proportion of red each time. Repeat five times for each size. Describe how variability changes as sample size increases.

## Memorable takeaways
- Different random samples give different results — this is sampling variability.
- Larger samples produce less variability and more reliable estimates.
- No single sample perfectly matches the population — expect variation.
- The spread of sample means shrinks as sample size grows.
- Small samples can give misleading results by chance.

Use a random-number generator (or draw from a bag) to simulate taking random samples from a known population. Create a "population" of 100 numbers with a known mean (e.g. the numbers 1–100, mean = 50.5). Take 10 random samples of size 5 and record the mean of each. Then take 10 random samples of size 25 and record each mean. Create a dot plot of the 10 means for each sample size on the same number line. Calculate the range of the means for each group. Explain why this happens and discuss the implications for real-world surveys. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Thinking one sample tells the whole story.** A single sample is one snapshot. Different samples give different snapshots.
- **Expecting exact results from samples.** Even a good random sample will not match the population perfectly — expect some discrepancy.
- **Ignoring sample size when interpreting results.** A finding from 10 people carries much less weight than the same finding from 1 000 people.
- **Confusing sampling variability with bias.** Variability is natural random fluctuation (reduced by larger samples). Bias is systematic error (not fixed by larger samples).

## Try drawing this
Draw a target with the population mean at the bull's-eye. Around it, place dots representing sample means from small samples (n = 10) — scattered widely. Then place dots for large samples (n = 100) — clustered tightly around the centre. Label both groups. This shows that larger samples are more "accurate" (less spread) even though both are unbiased (centred on the true value).

A note on certainty. Sampling variability is a mathematical consequence of randomness, not a flaw in technique. It cannot be eliminated, only reduced (by increasing sample size). Professional statisticians quantify this uncertainty using confidence intervals and margins of error — tools you will encounter in later studies.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Will two sample means be identical?* Almost certainly not. Different random selections include different students, producing different means.
2. *Closest to true 60%.* Sample 3 (6/10 = 60%) matches exactly by chance, but all three samples are reasonable results.
3. *Will all 10 means be the same?* No — each team's random sample includes different students, so each mean will differ slightly.
4. *Sample of 15 vs 150.* 150 — larger samples produce less variability and a more reliable estimate.
5. *60% from 30 people.* Not very confident. A sample of 30 has high variability — the true population proportion could easily be 50% or less.
6. *Screen time simulation.* Means range from 2.8 to 3.5 (spread 0.7). Average of means ≈ 3.14, close to true mean of 3.2. Larger samples would cluster more tightly.
7. *Which reflects true proportion?* The sample of 200 — larger samples give more reliable estimates. The 30% from 10 people could easily be due to chance.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Coin flips.* 70% from 10 flips is not very surprising (small sample, high variability). 53% from 100 is a better estimate of the true 50% — and still within normal variability.
2. *Library poll.* With n = 50, the true proportion could easily be anywhere from 40% to 68%. With n = 500, the estimate would be much more precise (perhaps 51%–57%). Larger samples narrow the range of plausible values.
3. *Bolts.* With only 5 tested, one defective bolt looks like 20% — but this is sampling variability. The 100-bolt sample (3%) is far more reliable because variability shrinks with sample size.
4. *Youth crime.* One month in one suburb is a tiny sample of all possible months and suburbs. Natural variability (random fluctuation) can easily produce a 15% swing. A year of data across many suburbs would be more reliable.
5. *Counter simulation.* Results will show: samples of 5 vary widely (perhaps 20%–100% red); samples of 20 cluster closer to 60% (perhaps 45%–75%). Variability clearly decreases with size.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Samples of size 5: means ranged from (say) 35 to 68, range = 33. Samples of size 25: means ranged from 44 to 57, range = 13. The range decreased by more than half. Larger samples better represent the population because extreme values balance out. Dot plots show tight clustering for n = 25 and wide scatter for n = 5, both centred near 50.5. Implication: real-world surveys need sufficiently large samples to give stable, trustworthy results — small polls can be wildly misleading.
