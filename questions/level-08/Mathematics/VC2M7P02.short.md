# VC2M7P02 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Conduct repeated chance experiments and run simulations with a large number of trials using digital tools; compare predicted with observed results, explaining the differences and the effect of sample size on the outcomes.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Observed results vary from predictions due to randomness. More trials bring relative frequency closer to theoretical probability. Simulations let you test thousands of trials quickly.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **conduct repeated experiments and simulations** and to **compare predicted with observed results**, explaining the effect of sample size.
- You should understand why larger samples give more reliable estimates.

## Core content

**Relative frequency** = (times an outcome occurred) ÷ (total trials). After 50 die rolls with twelve 4s: relative frequency = 12/50 = 0.24. The theoretical probability is 1/6 ≈ 0.167. The gap is expected in a small sample.

**The law of large numbers.** As trials increase, relative frequency settles closer to theoretical probability. 10 flips might give 70% heads; 10 000 flips will be very close to 50%.

**Simulations.** Use a spreadsheet (=RANDBETWEEN(1,6) for a die) to run thousands of trials. Count results with COUNTIF, then compare observed and predicted frequencies.

**Comparing predicted vs observed.** Predicted = P × trials. If differences are small relative to the total, the model fits. If differences are large, investigate: small sample? Biased equipment? Wrong model?

**Worked example.** Spinner: Red 50%, Blue 30%, Green 20%. After 1 000 simulated spins: Red = 487, Blue = 312, Green = 201. All close to predictions (500, 300, 200). Small differences are expected — the model is consistent with the data.

**Sample size matters.** Small samples produce high variation; large samples are stable. This is why scientific studies need large samples and one cricket match does not prove who is better.

**Victorian and cultural context.** The Bureau of Meteorology validates forecasts by comparing predictions with observed weather over thousands of days. First Nations intergenerational ecological knowledge — fire-return intervals, seasonal patterns — represents long-term observation that mirrors the statistical principle of accumulated data. International sport generates large datasets for Victorian students to analyse.

## Memorable takeaways
- Relative frequency = observed count ÷ total trials.
- More trials → relative frequency approaches theoretical probability.
- Small samples are noisy; large samples are stable.
- Simulations are fast but assume the model is correct.

## Watch out for these traps
- "My results differ from theory, so the theory is wrong." Check sample size first — small samples always vary.
- "More trials guarantee the exact probability." They get closer but never guarantee exact equality.
- "One unusual result proves bias." 10 heads in a row has P = 1/1 024 — rare but possible with a fair coin.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *13 heads in 20 flips — relative frequency?* 13/20 = 0.65 = 65%.
- *Why 10 flips look unfair.* One extra head shifts the proportion by 10%; in 10 000 flips the same shift is 0.01%.
- *130 sixes in 600 rolls — fair die?* 30% above the expected 100. Warrants investigation — run more trials.
- *P(zero rares in 20 loot boxes at 10% rate)?* 0.9²⁰ ≈ 0.12. Unlucky but not improbable — not strong evidence of false advertising.
- *20 flips vs 2 000 flips?* 2 000 gives a more reliable estimate because variation shrinks with sample size.
