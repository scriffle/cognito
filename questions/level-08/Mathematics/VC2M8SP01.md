# VC2M8SP01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8SP01. Identify the conditions for congruence and similarity of triangles and explain the conditions for other sets of common shapes to be congruent or similar, including those formed by transformations.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Space).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Congruent shapes are identical in size and shape — one maps exactly onto the other.
  - Similar shapes have the same shape but different sizes — all angles equal, all sides in proportion.
  - Specific minimum conditions (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS) guarantee triangle congruence without checking every measurement.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **identify conditions for congruence and similarity** of triangles and other common shapes, including shapes formed by transformations.
- You are learning that a few measurements can prove shapes are identical or proportionally scaled.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define congruence and similarity.
- State and apply the five triangle congruence conditions (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS).
- State the conditions for triangle similarity (AAA, SAS-similarity, SSS-similarity).
- Identify congruent and similar shapes created by reflection, rotation, translation and enlargement.

## Core content

**1. Congruence.** Two shapes are **congruent** (≅) if one can be placed exactly on top of the other — same shape, same size. Every matching side is equal in length; every matching angle is equal. Reflections, rotations and translations produce congruent images. *Quick check:* Are two circles with the same radius congruent?

**2. Triangle congruence conditions.** You do not need to check all six measurements (3 sides + 3 angles). Any one of these is sufficient:
- **SSS** — three sides equal.
- **SAS** — two sides and the included angle equal.
- **ASA** — two angles and the included side equal.
- **AAS** — two angles and a non-included side equal.
- **RHS** — right angle, hypotenuse and one other side equal.

*Quick check:* Two triangles both have sides 5 cm, 7 cm and 9 cm. Are they congruent? Which condition applies?

**3. Why SSA is not a congruence condition.** Two sides and a non-included angle (SSA) can produce two different triangles (the ambiguous case). This is why SSA is excluded. *Quick check:* Draw two different triangles with the same two sides and a non-included angle.

**4. Similarity.** Two shapes are **similar** (∼) if they have the same shape but may differ in size. All corresponding angles are equal; all corresponding sides are in the same ratio (the **scale factor**). Enlargements (dilations) produce similar figures. *Quick check:* A triangle has sides 3, 4, 5 cm. Another has sides 6, 8, 10 cm. Are they similar? What is the scale factor?

**5. Triangle similarity conditions.**
- **AAA (or AA)** — two pairs of equal angles (the third follows automatically).
- **SSS-similarity** — all three pairs of sides in the same ratio.
- **SAS-similarity** — two pairs of sides in the same ratio with the included angle equal.

*Quick check:* Two triangles each have angles of 40°, 60° and 80°. Are they similar?

**6. Worked example: proving congruence with SAS.**

In a park, two triangular garden beds share a common boundary fence (AB). Triangle ABP has AB = 8 m, BP = 5 m, ∠ABP = 65°. Triangle ABQ (on the other side) has AB = 8 m, BQ = 5 m, ∠ABQ = 65°.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Matching sides: AB = AB (common), BP = BQ = 5 m.
- Included angle: ∠ABP = ∠ABQ = 65°.
- Condition: SAS. ∴ △ABP ≅ △ABQ.
- Consequence: AP = AQ and the two beds have equal area.

**7. Congruence and similarity through transformations.** Translations, reflections and rotations produce congruent images (same size and shape). Enlargements (dilations) produce similar images (same shape, different size). A combination of dilation + rotation/reflection gives a similar figure. *Quick check:* A shape is reflected then enlarged by scale factor 2. Is the image congruent or similar to the original?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The NGV's Great Hall ceiling features a stained-glass design with repeated congruent triangular panels — restoration requires identifying congruent pieces for replacement. Surveyors in Victoria use triangle congruence conditions to verify boundary measurements without re-measuring every side. The Eureka Tower's facade uses similar parallelogram panels at different scales.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations art traditions — such as possum-skin cloak designs, cross-hatching (rarrk) in Arnhem Land and sand-drawing traditions — use repeated congruent and similar geometric motifs. The symmetry operations (reflection, rotation) that produce these patterns are the same transformations that produce congruent shapes in mathematics. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Islamic geometric art (visible in Melbourne's mosques and galleries) relies on congruent tiles assembled through reflections and rotations. Japanese origami, studied in Victorian schools, produces congruent folds and similar subdivisions.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You have two photos of the same image: one is 10 cm × 15 cm and the other is 20 cm × 30 cm. Are they congruent, similar or neither? State the scale factor.
2. **Civic.** A surveyor at the City of Knox checks a triangular park boundary. She measures two sides (45 m and 60 m) and the included angle (72°). A second surveyor measures the same two sides and included angle independently. What congruence condition confirms they are mapping the same triangle?
3. **Vocational.** A glazier cutting triangular glass panels needs each panel to have sides 30 cm, 40 cm and 50 cm. How does she verify each cut piece is congruent to the template? Which condition does she use?
4. **Ethical.** A student claims two triangles are congruent because they share two sides and an angle (SSA). Explain why this might be insufficient and describe the ambiguous case.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A tiling artist at a Melbourne gallery creates a pattern using a single triangular tile reflected and rotated. Explain why every tile in the pattern is congruent to the original. Which transformations preserve congruence?

## Memorable takeaways
- Congruent = same shape AND size. Similar = same shape, proportional size.
- Triangle congruence: SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS — learn these five.
- SSA does NOT guarantee congruence (ambiguous case).
- Similarity: AAA (or AA), SSS-ratio, SAS-ratio.
- Transformations: reflect/rotate/translate → congruent. Enlarge → similar.

## Watch out for these traps
- "SSA proves congruence." No — two sides and a non-included angle can produce two different triangles (the ambiguous case).
- "Same area means congruent." A 3 × 6 rectangle and a 2 × 9 rectangle both have area 18, but they are neither congruent nor similar.
- "Similar means the same." Similar means same shape, not same size. Congruent shapes are a special case of similarity (scale factor 1).
- "All equilateral triangles are congruent." They are all similar (same angles: 60°, 60°, 60°), but only congruent if they have the same side length.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Two circles with same radius — congruent?* Yes. One maps exactly onto the other by translation.
2. *Triangles with sides 5, 7, 9 — congruent?* Yes, by SSS.
3. *SSA ambiguity.* With sides 5, 7 and an angle of 30° opposite the side of 5, you can construct two different triangles (the longer side can swing to two positions).
4. *Sides 3,4,5 and 6,8,10 — similar?* Yes. Scale factor = 2 (each side doubled). All ratios equal: 6/3 = 8/4 = 10/5 = 2.
5. *Triangles with angles 40°, 60°, 80° — similar?* Yes, by AAA (or AA, since the third angle follows).
6. *Reflected then enlarged by 2 — congruent or similar?* Similar (same shape, different size due to the enlargement).

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Photos 10×15 and 20×30.* Similar. Scale factor = 2. Not congruent (different size).
2. *Surveyor SAS.* Two sides and included angle match → SAS confirms it is the same triangle.
3. *Glazier.* She measures all three sides of each cut piece and checks they equal 30, 40, 50 → SSS congruence.
4. *SSA ambiguity.* Given sides a, b and angle A opposite side a, the circle of radius a centred at C may intersect line AB in two points, giving two possible triangles with the same SSA measurements.
5. *Tiling artist.* Reflections and rotations are rigid transformations — they preserve all side lengths and angles, so every image is congruent to the original.
