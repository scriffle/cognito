# VC2M7A06 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7A06. Manipulate formulas involving several variables using digital tools, and describe the effect of systematic variation in the values of the variables.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A formula is a compact instruction linking several variables.
  - Changing one variable while holding the others fixed reveals how the formula behaves.
  - Digital tools let you test many values quickly and see the effect.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Numeracy; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **rearrange and evaluate formulas that have more than one variable**, and to **use spreadsheets or calculators to see what happens when you change one variable systematically**.
- You are learning that formulas are not fixed answers — they are tools for exploring "what if?"

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify the variables in a formula and state which is the subject.
- Substitute values into a formula involving two or more variables and evaluate the result.
- Rearrange a simple formula to make a different variable the subject.
- Use a spreadsheet to vary one variable systematically and describe the effect on the output.

## Core content

**1. What is a formula?** A **formula** is an equation that shows the relationship between several **variables**. The area of a rectangle, A = l × w, links three variables: area, length and width. The variable on the left (A) is called the **subject**. *Quick check:* In the formula v = u + at, which variable is the subject?

**2. Substitution.** To evaluate a formula, replace each variable with a given value and calculate. For A = l × w, if l = 8 and w = 5, then A = 8 × 5 = 40. Always include **units** in your answer — here, 40 cm² if the lengths were in centimetres. *Quick check:* In v = u + at, find v when u = 10, a = 3 and t = 4.

**3. Rearranging (changing the subject).** Sometimes you know the output and need to find one of the inputs. To make w the subject of A = l × w, divide both sides by l: w = A ÷ l. The rule: what you do to one side, you do to the other. *Quick check:* Rearrange v = u + at to make t the subject.

**4. Systematic variation.** **Systematic variation** means changing one variable by regular steps while keeping the others constant. In A = l × w, hold w = 5 and let l go from 1 to 10 in steps of 1. Record A each time. You see A increasing by 5 at every step — a **linear** relationship between A and l when w is fixed. *Quick check:* If you doubled w instead, what would happen to every A value in the table?

**5. Using digital tools.** A **spreadsheet** is ideal for systematic variation. Enter your l-values in column A, type the formula =A2*5 in column B, and drag down. Instantly you have a table and can insert a chart. Graphing calculators and online tools like Desmos do the same job. *Quick check:* Why is a spreadsheet faster than pen-and-paper for testing 50 values?

**6. Worked example — speed, distance, time.**
The formula is d = s × t (distance = speed × time).

Hold s = 60 km/h constant and vary t from 1 to 6 hours:

| t (hours) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| d (km) | 60 | 120 | 180 | 240 | 300 | 360 |

*Follow the thinking:* Each extra hour adds 60 km — a constant increase. Plot (t, d) and you get a straight line through the origin with gradient 60. Now rearrange for time: t = d ÷ s. If d = 210 km and s = 60 km/h, then t = 3.5 hours.

**7. Describing the effect.** After systematic variation, describe your findings in words: "When l increases by 1 (with w fixed at 5), A increases by 5." State whether the relationship is linear or non-linear, whether the output increases or decreases, and by how much per step. This is the mathematical story behind the numbers. *Quick check:* In the formula A = πr², if you increase r by 1 each time, is the increase in A constant?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A mobile plan costs $30 per month plus $0.10 per text over 200. Write the formula for total monthly cost. Build a spreadsheet table for 200 to 300 texts (steps of 10) and describe the pattern.
2. **Civic.** The City of Yarra estimates parkland irrigation using W = A × d × r (water = area × depth × rate). If the council fixes depth and rate, use a spreadsheet to vary area from 100 to 1000 m² and advise which park sizes stay within a 5000-litre budget.
3. **Vocational.** An apprentice electrician uses P = V × I (power = voltage × current). Rearrange for I. If P = 2400 W and V = 240 V, find I. Then build a spreadsheet varying V from 200 to 250 in steps of 10 and describe how I changes.
4. **Ethical.** A student uses a spreadsheet model to claim that doubling study hours always doubles test scores. Identify two reasons this claim is flawed.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A school's multicultural cooking day needs to scale a recipe for 20, 40, 60 and 80 people. Write the formula for total flour (F = f × n, where f is flour per person and n is the number of people). Use a spreadsheet to generate the table and describe the relationship.

## Memorable takeaways
- A formula links variables — change one, watch what happens to the others.
- The subject is the variable on its own; rearranging swaps the subject.
- Systematic variation means changing one thing at a time and recording the effect.
- Spreadsheets make "what if?" testing fast and visual.
- Always describe the effect in words, not just numbers.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting order of operations when substituting.** In v = u + at, calculate a × t first, then add u. Use brackets if needed: v = u + (a × t).
- **Rearranging incorrectly.** Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. Check by substituting known values back in.
- **Changing two variables at once.** Systematic variation means one variable changes at a time. If you change both l and w simultaneously, you cannot tell which caused the change in A.
- **Confusing the formula with reality.** A formula models a relationship; it does not guarantee the real world behaves perfectly. Always consider whether the model still applies.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Subject of v = u + at.* v is the subject.
2. *Find v when u = 10, a = 3, t = 4.* v = 10 + 3 × 4 = 10 + 12 = 22.
3. *Rearrange v = u + at for t.* Subtract u: v − u = at. Divide by a: t = (v − u) ÷ a.
4. *Doubling w.* Every A value would also double, because A = l × w and doubling one factor doubles the product.
5. *Spreadsheet faster than pen-and-paper.* It calculates all 50 values instantly from one formula and can chart them automatically.
6. *Is the increase in A = πr² constant?* No. When r goes from 1 to 2, A goes from π to 4π (increase of 3π). When r goes from 2 to 3, A goes from 4π to 9π (increase of 5π). The increases grow — the relationship is non-linear.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Mobile plan.* C = 30 + 0.10(t − 200) for t > 200. At 250 texts: C = 30 + 0.10 × 50 = $35. Linear increase of $1 per 10 extra texts.
2. *City of Yarra irrigation.* With d and r fixed, W is proportional to A. Find the area where W = A × d × r ≤ 5000 and identify the cut-off.
3. *Electrician.* I = P ÷ V = 2400 ÷ 240 = 10 A. As V increases from 200 to 250, I decreases — the relationship is non-linear (inverse).
4. *Study-hours claim.* (a) The relationship between study and performance is unlikely to be perfectly linear — gains diminish. (b) Other variables (sleep, prior knowledge) are not held constant.
5. *Scaling recipe.* F = f × n is linear. Doubling the people doubles the flour. The graph is a straight line through the origin.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Weekday: C = 120 + 45h. Weekend: C = 120 + 65h. For weekend cost > $500: 120 + 65h > 500 → 65h > 380 → h > 5.846… → first whole hour is h = 6 (C = $510). Rearranged: h = (C − B) ÷ r. The weekend line is steeper (gradient 65 vs 45); both start at $120 when h = 0. A community group booking 6+ hours on a weekend pays significantly more — they might choose a weekday or negotiate a shorter booking.
