# VC2M7SP02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7SP02. Classify triangles, quadrilaterals and other polygons according to their side and angle properties; identify and reason about relationships.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Space).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Shapes are classified by measurable properties, not by how they look.
  - Side lengths and angle sizes determine a shape's category.
  - Classification reveals relationships — every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Technologies (Design).

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **classify triangles, quadrilaterals and other polygons** by their **side and angle properties**.
- You are learning to **identify and reason about relationships** between shape categories.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Name and define types of triangles by sides (scalene, isosceles, equilateral) and by angles (acute, right, obtuse).
- Name and define types of quadrilaterals and state their properties.
- Explain hierarchical relationships (e.g., a square is a special rectangle).
- Classify an unfamiliar polygon by measuring or reasoning about its properties.

## Core content

**1. What is a polygon?** A **polygon** is a closed flat shape with straight sides. Triangles (3 sides), quadrilaterals (4), pentagons (5), hexagons (6) and so on. A **regular** polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal. *Quick check:* Is a circle a polygon? Why not?

**2. Classifying triangles by sides.** **Equilateral** — all three sides equal (and all angles 60°). **Isosceles** — two sides equal (and two base angles equal). **Scalene** — no sides equal. *Quick check:* A triangle has sides 5 cm, 5 cm and 8 cm. What type is it?

**3. Classifying triangles by angles.** **Acute** — all angles less than 90°. **Right** — one angle exactly 90°. **Obtuse** — one angle greater than 90°. You can combine both systems: a triangle can be "right isosceles" (one 90° angle, two equal sides). *Quick check:* Can a triangle be both right and obtuse? Explain.

**4. Angle sum of a triangle.** The interior angles of every triangle add to **180°**. This is a foundational property — use it to find a missing angle. If two angles are 50° and 70°, the third is 180° − 50° − 70° = 60°. *Quick check:* A triangle has angles 90° and 35°. Find the third angle.

**5. Classifying quadrilaterals.** **Square** — four equal sides, four right angles. **Rectangle** — opposite sides equal, four right angles. **Rhombus** — four equal sides, opposite angles equal. **Parallelogram** — opposite sides equal and parallel, opposite angles equal. **Trapezium** — exactly one pair of parallel sides. **Kite** — two pairs of adjacent equal sides. *Quick check:* A shape has four equal sides and no right angles. What is it?

**6. Hierarchical relationships.** Classification is hierarchical: every square is a rectangle (it meets all rectangle requirements plus more). Every rectangle is a parallelogram. Every rhombus is a parallelogram. This is not about appearance — it follows from the definitions. *Quick check:* Is every parallelogram a rectangle? Why or why not?

**7. Worked example — tiling at Flinders Street Station.** The floor tiles at Flinders Street Station include squares, rectangles and hexagons. Classify each tile: the square tiles have four equal sides and four 90° angles. The rectangular tiles have opposite sides equal and four 90° angles. The hexagonal border tiles are regular hexagons — six equal sides and interior angles of 120° each. *Follow the thinking:* measure or identify side lengths → check angle sizes → apply the definition → classify.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Flinders Street Station's tiled floors, the geometric facade of Melbourne's Royal Exhibition Building, the hexagonal basalt columns at the Organ Pipes National Park and the angular design of ACMI's building at Federation Square all demonstrate polygon properties in Victorian architecture and geology.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal shield designs from south-eastern Australia use symmetrical geometric shapes — diamonds, triangles and nested polygons — with precise proportional relationships. Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung and Gunaikurnai shield carvings demonstrate sophisticated spatial reasoning. AIATSIS publishes research on Indigenous geometric design. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Islamic geometric art (visible in Melbourne's mosques and cultural centres) uses tessellations of polygons with strict angle and side rules. Students from diverse backgrounds bring rich traditions of geometric pattern-making.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Look at the front of your house or school building. Identify and classify three polygons you can see (windows, doors, decorative panels).
2. **Civic.** Moreland City Council is designing a new park with triangular garden beds. Explain why equilateral triangles tessellate and how this helps the landscaper.
3. **Vocational.** A carpentry apprentice cuts a piece of timber into a quadrilateral with one pair of parallel sides. What shape is it? What measurements should they check?
4. **Ethical.** A textbook says "a diamond is a type of quadrilateral." Discuss whether this is mathematically precise and what the correct term should be.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Compare the geometric patterns on a Wurundjeri shield with the patterns in Islamic tilework at a Melbourne mosque. What polygon types appear in each?

## Memorable takeaways
- Classify by properties (sides and angles), not by appearance.
- Triangle angle sum is always 180°.
- Quadrilateral classification is hierarchical — a square is a special rectangle is a special parallelogram.
- Regular polygons have all sides and all angles equal.
- Properties determine the name; the name does not determine the properties.

## Watch out for these traps
- "A square is not a rectangle." It is — it meets every rectangle requirement and has equal sides as well.
- "Diamonds are a shape category." The mathematical terms are rhombus or kite, depending on properties.
- "You can tell a shape's type by looking." Always check measurements — a shape that looks like a square might be a rhombus with angles slightly off 90°.
- "An obtuse triangle can have two obtuse angles." Impossible — two angles over 90° would exceed the 180° sum.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Is a circle a polygon?* No — a polygon must have straight sides; a circle has a curved boundary.
2. *Triangle with sides 5, 5, 8.* Isosceles — two sides are equal.
3. *Right and obtuse?* No — a right angle is exactly 90° and an obtuse angle is more than 90°. A triangle can have at most one non-acute angle (because 90° + 91° already exceeds 180°).
4. *Third angle.* 180° − 90° − 35° = 55°.
5. *Four equal sides, no right angles.* Rhombus.
6. *Is every parallelogram a rectangle?* No — a rectangle requires all four angles to be 90°, and a parallelogram only requires opposite angles to be equal.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *House polygons.* Windows (rectangles), door (rectangle), gable (triangle). Classify by measuring or reasoning about sides and angles.
2. *Equilateral triangles tessellate.* Six equilateral triangles meet at a point (6 × 60° = 360°), leaving no gaps. This helps the landscaper create uniform beds with no wasted space.
3. *Carpentry quadrilateral.* Trapezium. Check: one pair of parallel sides (use a set square) and measure all four side lengths.
4. *"Diamond" discussion.* Not a standard mathematical term. The shape is usually a rhombus (four equal sides) or a square rotated 45°. Precision matters in mathematics.
5. *Shield vs tilework.* Both use triangles, diamonds (rhombuses) and nested polygons. Shield designs emphasise bilateral symmetry; Islamic tilework emphasises rotational symmetry and tessellation.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Hierarchy: all squares are rhombuses (four equal sides) and rectangles (four right angles). A rhombus has four equal sides but angles are not necessarily 90°. Therefore every square meets rhombus requirements, but a rhombus with non-right angles is not a square. Properties determine the classification, not appearance.
