# VC2M8N05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8N05. Solve problems involving the use of percentages, including percentage increases and decreases and percentage error, with and without digital tools.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Number).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A percentage is a rate per hundred — it lets you compare parts of different-sized wholes on equal footing.
  - Percentage increase and decrease use a multiplier: multiply by (1 + r) to increase, (1 − r) to decrease.
  - Percentage error measures how far a measurement or estimate sits from the accepted value.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **solve problems involving percentages** — increases, decreases and error — using both pen-and-paper methods and digital tools.
- You are learning to choose the right method for the context and to interpret percentage results critically.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Calculate a percentage of a quantity.
- Apply percentage increase and decrease using a multiplier.
- Calculate percentage error given a measured and accepted value.
- Use a calculator or spreadsheet to solve multi-step percentage problems.

## Core content

**1. Percentage as a rate per hundred.** "Per cent" means "per hundred." 35 % means 35 out of every 100. To find a percentage of a quantity, convert the percentage to a decimal and multiply: 35 % of 240 = 0.35 × 240 = 84. *Quick check:* Find 12 % of 450.

**2. Expressing one quantity as a percentage of another.** Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. If 18 out of 30 students walk to school: (18 ÷ 30) × 100 = 60 %. *Quick check:* A class of 25 has 8 students who ride bikes. What percentage is that?

**3. Percentage increase.** A percentage increase adds a fraction of the original. Two methods: (a) find the increase and add it, or (b) use the multiplier 1 + r, where r is the rate as a decimal. A $60 item marked up by 15 %: multiplier = 1.15, new price = 60 × 1.15 = $69. *Quick check:* A $200 phone bill increases by 8 %. What is the new amount?

**4. Percentage decrease.** A percentage decrease subtracts a fraction of the original. Use the multiplier 1 − r. A $120 jacket discounted by 25 %: multiplier = 0.75, new price = 120 × 0.75 = $90. *Quick check:* A $480 bike is reduced by 30 %. What is the sale price?

**5. Successive percentage changes.** Multiply the multipliers. A 10 % increase followed by a 10 % decrease is not a return to the original: 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99 — a 1 % loss overall. *Quick check:* A price rises by 20 % then falls by 20 %. Is the final price the same as the original? Show the multiplier calculation.

**6. Worked example — percentage increase and percentage error side by side.**

*Part A — Percentage increase.* A Victorian council's annual rates notice shows a property valued at $520,000 last year. The valuation increases by 6.5 % this year.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Multiplier = 1 + 0.065 = 1.065.
- New valuation = 520,000 × 1.065 = $553,800.
- The increase amount = 553,800 − 520,000 = $33,800.

*Part B — Percentage error.* A student estimates the school oval's length at 95 m. The surveyed length is 102 m. Calculate the percentage error.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Error = |measured − accepted| = |95 − 102| = 7 m.
- Percentage error = (error ÷ accepted) × 100 = (7 ÷ 102) × 100 ≈ 6.86 %.
- The estimate was about 6.9 % below the accepted value.

**7. Percentage error in context.** Percentage error = (|measured value − accepted value| ÷ accepted value) × 100. It tells you the relative size of an error, not just the absolute size. A 2 cm error on a 10 cm length (20 % error) is more significant than a 2 cm error on a 200 cm length (1 % error). *Quick check:* You estimate the mass of a rock at 350 g. The actual mass is 400 g. What is the percentage error?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Government publishes annual rate notices based on property valuations — percentage increases in valuations directly affect household budgets. The City of Melbourne reports percentage changes in retail spending, foot traffic and tourism. VicHealth uses percentage statistics to communicate public health data, such as the percentage decrease in smoking rates over a decade.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Yoorrook Justice Commission has published data showing percentage disparities in outcomes for First Nations Victorians across health, education and justice. Understanding percentage comparison is essential for reading these reports critically and advocating for equity. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Victoria's diverse communities navigate percentage-based systems daily — GST (10 %), interest rates on savings, rental increases capped by Consumer Affairs Victoria, and currency exchange margins when sending money overseas. Percentage literacy is a practical life skill across all communities.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A streaming service costs $14.99 per month. It announces an 18 % price increase. Calculate the new monthly cost and the extra amount you pay per year.
2. **Civic.** The City of Greater Geelong publishes that residential waste sent to landfill decreased by 12 % from 2022 to 2023, dropping from 85,000 tonnes to 74,800 tonnes. Verify this figure using the percentage decrease formula, and explain what the council might report if the decrease were measured in tonnes alone versus as a percentage.
3. **Vocational.** A carpentry apprentice measures a beam at 2.43 m. The specification says 2.50 m. Calculate the percentage error. The tolerance is 2 %. Does the beam pass inspection?
4. **Ethical.** A shop advertises "50 % off, then an extra 20 % off!" A customer assumes this means 70 % off. Explain why successive discounts of 50 % and 20 % do not equal 70 %, and calculate the actual overall percentage discount.
5. **Cross-cultural.** First Nations health data published by the Yoorrook Justice Commission shows that hospitalisation rates for Aboriginal Victorians for certain preventable conditions are a stated percentage higher than the state average. Explain what "percentage higher" means mathematically and why percentage comparison (rather than raw numbers) is the appropriate measure when comparing groups of different sizes.

## Memorable takeaways
- Percentage increase multiplier: 1 + r. Percentage decrease multiplier: 1 − r.
- Successive percentage changes: multiply the multipliers — they do not simply add.
- Percentage error = (|measured − accepted| ÷ accepted) × 100.
- A small absolute error can be a large percentage error on a small quantity.
- Percentages let you compare fairly across different-sized groups or quantities.

## Stretch yourself
A small business owner in Footscray buys stock for $8,000. She marks it up by 40 % for retail. At the end of the season, she discounts unsold stock by 25 %. Calculate the retail price and the discounted price. What is her overall percentage profit or loss on the discounted stock compared to the original cost? Now suppose she offers a further 10 % loyalty discount on the already-discounted price. Calculate the final price and the overall percentage change from the original cost. Discuss whether she makes a profit or a loss on these items and by how much. Show all working with multipliers. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Adding successive percentages.** A 30 % increase then a 20 % decrease is not a 10 % increase. Multiply the multipliers: 1.30 × 0.80 = 1.04, so it is a 4 % increase.
- **Confusing percentage of with percentage change.** "20 % of 80" (= 16) is different from "80 increased by 20 %" (= 96).
- **Using the wrong base for percentage error.** Always divide by the accepted (true) value, not the measured value.
- **Forgetting absolute value in percentage error.** The formula uses |measured − accepted|, so the error is always positive.

A note on certainty. The mathematics of percentages is exact and well-established. Where uncertainty enters is in the values being measured — a percentage error is only meaningful if the "accepted value" is itself reliable. In real-world contexts like property valuations or health statistics, the underlying data carries its own margins.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *12 % of 450.* 0.12 × 450 = 54.
2. *8 out of 25 as a percentage.* (8 ÷ 25) × 100 = 32 %.
3. *$200 increased by 8 %.* 200 × 1.08 = $216.
4. *$480 reduced by 30 %.* 480 × 0.70 = $336.
5. *20 % up then 20 % down.* 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96. Final price is 96 % of original — a 4 % decrease. Not the same.
6. *Percentage error on rock mass.* |350 − 400| ÷ 400 × 100 = 50 ÷ 400 × 100 = 12.5 %.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Streaming service.* New price = 14.99 × 1.18 = $17.69 (rounded to nearest cent). Extra per year = (17.69 − 14.99) × 12 = $2.70 × 12 = $32.40.
2. *Geelong waste.* Decrease = 85,000 − 74,800 = 10,200 tonnes. Percentage decrease = (10,200 ÷ 85,000) × 100 = 12.0 %. Verified. In tonnes alone, 10,200 tonnes sounds large but does not convey the proportion. As a percentage, 12 % gives context relative to the starting amount.
3. *Beam measurement.* Error = |2.43 − 2.50| = 0.07 m. Percentage error = (0.07 ÷ 2.50) × 100 = 2.8 %. Tolerance is 2 %, so 2.8 % exceeds it — the beam does not pass.
4. *Successive discounts.* 50 % off then 20 % off: 0.50 × 0.80 = 0.40. You pay 40 % of the original, so the discount is 60 %, not 70 %.
5. *Percentage higher explained.* If the state average hospitalisation rate is, say, 50 per 1,000 and the Aboriginal rate is 75 per 1,000, the rate is (75 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = 50 % higher. Percentage comparison accounts for different population sizes — raw numbers would mislead because the Aboriginal population is smaller.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Cost = $8,000. Retail = 8,000 × 1.40 = $11,200. Discounted = 11,200 × 0.75 = $8,400. Overall on discounted stock: (8,400 − 8,000) ÷ 8,000 × 100 = 5 % profit. With loyalty discount: 8,400 × 0.90 = $7,560. Overall: (7,560 − 8,000) ÷ 8,000 × 100 = −5.5 %, a loss. Using multipliers throughout: 1.40 × 0.75 = 1.05 (5 % profit); 1.40 × 0.75 × 0.90 = 0.945 (5.5 % loss). The business loses money on loyalty-discounted clearance items.
