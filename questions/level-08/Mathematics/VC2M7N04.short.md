# VC2M7N04 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Round decimals to a given accuracy appropriate to the context and use appropriate rounding and estimation to check the reasonableness of computations.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Rounding replaces exact values with simpler nearby values. Context determines precision. Estimation catches errors before they matter.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **round decimals to a given accuracy** and to **use estimation to check computations**.
- You should be able to choose the right level of rounding for the situation.

## Core content

**Rounding** replaces a number with a simpler nearby value. Look at the digit after your rounding position: 5 or more rounds up, less than 5 rounds down. Example: 3.847 rounded to one decimal place is 3.8.

**Context decides precision.** Money rounds to two decimal places. Rainfall reports use one decimal place. Population figures round to the nearest thousand. False precision — reporting 4.56732 m from a tape measure reading — is misleading.

**Estimation** means rounding before you calculate to predict the answer. To estimate 48.7 × 21.3, round to 50 × 20 = 1 000. The exact answer (1 037.31) is close, confirming the computation is reasonable. Estimation catches order-of-magnitude errors like misplaced decimal points.

**Worked example.** You buy 2.5 kg of apples at $4.80/kg and 1.75 kg of bananas at $3.20/kg. Estimate: 2.5 × $5 + 2 × $3 = $18.50. Exact: $12.00 + $5.60 = $17.60. If the receipt says $18.60, something is wrong — the estimate helped you spot the error.

**Accumulated rounding error.** Rounding each intermediate value and then adding can distort the total. In financial and scientific work, keep extra decimal places through the calculation and round only the final answer.

**Victorian and cultural context.** The Bureau of Meteorology (Melbourne) rounds rainfall and temperature to one decimal place daily. First Nations resource management uses practical estimation — judging water levels, fire intensity and seasonal availability through intergenerational observation. Market traders at Queen Victoria Market round prices mentally every transaction.

## Memorable takeaways
- 5 or more rounds up; less than 5 rounds down.
- Context dictates precision — money to cents, rain to one decimal place.
- Estimate first, then calculate, then compare.
- Round the final answer, not every step.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More decimals is always better." False precision misleads. Match precision to the measurement tool.
- "My calculator must be right." Calculators do not catch input errors. Estimate first.
- "Rounding and truncating are the same." Truncating 3.87 gives 3.8; rounding gives 3.9.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Round 3.847 to 1 d.p.* 3.8 (the next digit is 4, round down).
- *Round 12.0462 to 2 d.p.* 12.05 (the third decimal digit is 6, round up).
- *Estimate 9.8 × 31.2.* 10 × 31 = 310. Exact: 305.76. Reasonable.
- *Why round only the final answer?* To avoid accumulated rounding error distorting the result.
- *Is reporting 87.3% as "approximately 90%" reasonable?* Borderline — it overstates by 2.7 percentage points. "Approximately 87%" or "nearly 90%" with the exact figure would be fairer.
