## Core content

**1. Discrete and continuous variables.** A **discrete** variable takes separate, countable values (number of siblings, goals scored). A **continuous** variable can take any value within a range (height, temperature, time). Both types produce numerical data sets that can be summarised. *Quick check:* Is "the number of pets a student owns" discrete or continuous?

**2. The mean.** Add all values and divide by how many there are: **mean = sum of values ÷ number of values**. The mean uses every data point, which makes it sensitive to **outliers** — one extreme value can drag it up or down. *Quick check:* Find the mean of 3, 5, 7, 9, 11.

**3. The median.** Order the values from smallest to largest. The **median** is the middle value. If there is an even number of values, the median is the average of the two middle values. The median is **resistant to outliers** — extreme values do not move it much. *Quick check:* Find the median of 2, 4, 6, 8, 100.

**4. The mode.** The **mode** is the most frequently occurring value. A data set can have no mode, one mode, or more than one mode (bimodal, multimodal). The mode is the only measure of centre that works for categorical data too, but here we focus on numerical data. *Quick check:* Find the mode of 3, 5, 5, 7, 8, 8, 8, 10.

**5. The range.** The **range** is the difference between the largest and smallest values: range = maximum − minimum. It measures **spread**, not centre. A large range suggests the data is spread out; a small range suggests the data is clustered. *Quick check:* What is the range of 12, 15, 18, 22, 30?

**6. Worked example — choosing the right measure for a school fundraiser.**
A Year 7 class records how much each student raised: $5, $8, $10, $10, $12, $15, $10, $200.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Mean = (5 + 8 + 10 + 10 + 12 + 15 + 10 + 200) ÷ 8 = 270 ÷ 8 = $33.75.
- Median: order the data — 5, 8, 10, 10, 10, 12, 15, 200. Middle values are 10 and 10. Median = $10.
- Mode = $10 (occurs three times).
- Range = 200 − 5 = $195.
- The mean ($33.75) is dragged up by the $200 outlier and does not represent a typical student. The median ($10) and mode ($10) both show that most students raised around $10. For this data, the **median or mode** gives a more useful picture of the typical student.

**7. When to use which measure.** Use the **mean** when the data is roughly symmetric with no extreme outliers — it uses all the data. Use the **median** when the data is skewed or has outliers — it resists being pulled by extremes. Use the **mode** when you want the most common value or when working with categorical data. Always report the **range** alongside a measure of centre to show how spread out the data is. *Quick check:* House prices in a suburb are mostly around $600 000 but one mansion sold for $5 000 000. Which measure of centre best represents a typical house price?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You track your daily screen time for a week: 45, 60, 50, 55, 300, 40, 50 minutes. Calculate the mean, median and mode. Which best represents your typical screen time? Explain.
2. **Civic.** The City of Greater Dandenong publishes average household sizes for planning. Explain why the median might be more useful than the mean if some households are very large.
3. **Vocational.** A café owner records daily coffees sold: 80, 85, 90, 82, 88, 150, 84. The 150 was a festival day. Should the owner use the mean or median to plan regular stock? Justify.
4. **Ethical.** A charity reports that its donors give "an average of $500." The data is: $10, $10, $15, $20, $25, $2 920. Is this claim misleading? Which measure would be more honest?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Two classes collect data on how many languages each student speaks. Class A: 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3. Class B: 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4. Compare the mean, median and mode of each class and discuss what the differences reveal.

## Memorable takeaways
- Mean: add and divide — uses all data, sensitive to outliers.
- Median: middle value — resistant to outliers.
- Mode: most frequent value — can have more than one.
- Range: max − min — measures spread, not centre.
- Choose the measure that fits the data shape and the question.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Assuming the mean is always best.** It is only best when the data is roughly symmetric with no extreme outliers.
- **Forgetting to order data before finding the median.** The median requires sorted data — an unsorted middle value is meaningless.
- **Ignoring outliers.** An outlier can make the mean misleading. Always check the data for extreme values.
- **Confusing range with centre.** The range tells you about spread, not about where the data clusters.
