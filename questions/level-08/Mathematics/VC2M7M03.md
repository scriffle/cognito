# VC2M7M03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M7M03. Describe the relationship between π and the circumference, radius and diameter of a circle.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Measurement).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Every circle shares the same ratio of circumference to diameter — that ratio is π.
  - π connects measurement to reasoning about all circles, from coins to orbits.
  - Understanding π lets you move confidently between circumference, diameter and radius.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Sustainability (resource estimation).

## What you are learning
- You are learning how **π links the circumference, diameter and radius** of every circle.
- You are learning to use π to calculate unknown measurements and to explain why the relationship works.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define π as the ratio of circumference to diameter.
- State and use C = πd and C = 2πr.
- Estimate and calculate circumference given diameter or radius, and vice versa.
- Explain why π is the same for every circle.

## Core content

**1. What is π?** Take any circle. Divide its circumference (the distance around) by its diameter (the distance across through the centre). The answer is always the same number: **π ≈ 3.14159…** It never terminates and never repeats — it is an **irrational number**. *Quick check:* If you wrapped a string around a circular lid and then stretched it across the diameter, roughly how many diameters would fit along the string?

**2. Circumference, diameter and radius.** The **diameter** (d) runs from one side of the circle to the other through the centre. The **radius** (r) runs from the centre to the edge, so d = 2r. The **circumference** (C) is the perimeter of the circle. *Quick check:* A circle has radius 5 cm. What is its diameter?

**3. The circumference formula.** Because π = C ÷ d, rearranging gives **C = πd**. Since d = 2r, this is the same as **C = 2πr**. These two forms are interchangeable — choose whichever suits the information you have. *Quick check:* Write the circumference formula you would use if you are given the radius.

**4. Calculating circumference.** Substitute the known value into the formula. A wheel has diameter 70 cm: C = π × 70 ≈ 219.9 cm. Always state your rounding and include units. *Quick check:* A circular garden bed has radius 3 m. What is its circumference? (Use π ≈ 3.14.)

**5. Finding diameter or radius from circumference.** Rearrange: d = C ÷ π, and r = C ÷ (2π). A running track's inner circle has circumference 400 m: d = 400 ÷ π ≈ 127.3 m. *Quick check:* A bike wheel has circumference 2.1 m. What is its diameter?

**6. Worked example — measuring a Birrarung trail marker.**
A circular interpretive sign along the Birrarung (Yarra River) trail has a circumference of 188.5 cm. What is its diameter?

*Follow the thinking:*
- Identify the known: C = 188.5 cm.
- Choose the formula: d = C ÷ π.
- Substitute: d = 188.5 ÷ π ≈ 188.5 ÷ 3.14159 ≈ 60.0 cm.
- State the answer with units: the sign has a diameter of approximately 60 cm.

**7. Why π is constant.** All circles are similar — scale one up or down and every length scales by the same factor. Because both circumference and diameter scale equally, their ratio stays fixed. This is what makes π a universal constant, not just a handy shortcut. *Quick check:* If you double the diameter of a circle, what happens to the circumference?

Engineers at the Australian Synchrotron in Clayton work with circular particle paths where precise circumference calculations matter.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Circular forms appear throughout First Nations art, story and design — stone arrangements, shield shapes, and the engineered circular fish-trap pools at Budj Bim on Gunditjmara Country. While π as a named constant comes from Western mathematics, the practical knowledge of circular measurement has deep roots in many traditions. - **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The concept of π has a rich global history — Babylonian, Egyptian, Chinese and Indian mathematicians all approximated it centuries before the modern symbol was adopted. Students in Victorian schools bring diverse mathematical heritages into the classroom.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You are buying a protective cover for a circular trampoline. The label says the trampoline has a diameter of 3.7 m. Calculate the circumference so you can check the cover will fit around the frame.
2. **Civic.** Maribyrnong City Council is painting a new circular bike roundabout. The painted circle must have a circumference of 47.1 m. What diameter should the painters mark out?
3. **Vocational.** A carpentry apprentice needs to bend a steel strip into a hoop for a barrel with radius 35 cm. How long must the strip be before bending?
4. **Ethical.** A classmate says "π = 3.14 and that's exact." Explain why this is not correct and why rounding matters in real-world measurement.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research one non-European historical approximation of π (for example, from Indian mathematician Madhava or Chinese mathematician Zu Chongzhi). Explain what method was used and how accurate the approximation was.

## Memorable takeaways
- π is the ratio of circumference to diameter — the same for every circle.
- C = πd = 2πr. Two forms, one idea.
- π is irrational: it never terminates or repeats.
- Rearrange to find any missing measurement.
- Always state units and rounding.

## Stretch yourself
A landscape architect is designing a circular memorial garden in Footscray Park. The garden must have a circumference of exactly 31.4 m. A gravel path 0.5 m wide will run around the outside of the garden. Calculate the diameter of the garden, the radius of the outer edge of the path, and the circumference of the outer edge. Show all working and explain each step. Then discuss: if the path width were doubled, would the outer circumference also double? Justify your answer using the properties of π. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Confusing radius and diameter.** The diameter is twice the radius. Substituting one for the other halves or doubles your answer.
- **Saying π = 3.14 exactly.** 3.14 is a two-decimal approximation. π is irrational and continues forever.
- **Forgetting units.** A circumference of "219.9" means nothing without cm, m or mm.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep π in your working as long as possible; round only at the final step.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *How many diameters fit along the string?* Just over three — approximately 3.14, which is π.
2. *Diameter when radius is 5 cm.* d = 2 × 5 = 10 cm.
3. *Formula when given radius.* C = 2πr.
4. *Circumference of garden bed with r = 3 m.* C = 2 × 3.14 × 3 = 18.84 m.
5. *Diameter of bike wheel with C = 2.1 m.* d = 2.1 ÷ 3.14 ≈ 0.669 m (about 66.9 cm).
6. *Birrarung sign diameter.* d = 188.5 ÷ π ≈ 60.0 cm (worked through in the example).
7. *Double the diameter, what happens to C?* The circumference also doubles, because C = πd and π is constant.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Trampoline cover.* C = π × 3.7 ≈ 11.6 m. The cover must be at least 11.6 m around.
2. *Bike roundabout.* d = 47.1 ÷ π ≈ 15.0 m.
3. *Steel strip for barrel.* C = 2π × 35 ≈ 219.9 cm. The strip must be about 220 cm long.
4. *π is not exact at 3.14.* π is irrational — 3.14 is rounded to two decimal places. In precise work (engineering, science), more decimal places reduce error.
5. *Non-European approximation.* Example: Zu Chongzhi (5th century CE, China) calculated π ≈ 355/113 ≈ 3.1415929, accurate to six decimal places — a record that stood for nearly a thousand years.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Garden diameter = 31.4 ÷ π ≈ 10.0 m, so radius = 5.0 m. Outer radius = 5.0 + 0.5 = 5.5 m. Outer circumference = 2π × 5.5 ≈ 34.56 m. Doubling the path to 1.0 m gives outer radius 6.0 m and C = 2π × 6.0 ≈ 37.70 m — not double 34.56 m, because the added circumference depends on the added radius (C increases by 2π for every 1 m added to r), not on a multiplicative factor of the path width.
