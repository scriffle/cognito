# VC2M8N05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M8N05. Solve problems involving the use of percentages, including percentage increases and decreases and percentage error, with and without digital tools.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Number).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A percentage is a rate per hundred, useful for comparing parts of different wholes.
  - Percentage increase uses multiplier (1 + r); decrease uses (1 − r).
  - Percentage error measures how far a value sits from the accepted value.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to solve problems involving **percentage increase, decrease and error**.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Calculate percentages of quantities and express one quantity as a percentage of another.
- Use multipliers for percentage increase and decrease.
- Calculate percentage error.

## Core content

A percentage means "per hundred." To find 35 % of 240, convert to a decimal and multiply: 0.35 × 240 = 84. To express one quantity as a percentage of another, divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100.

For a **percentage increase**, use the multiplier (1 + r). A $60 item marked up by 15 %: 60 × 1.15 = $69. For a **percentage decrease**, use (1 − r). A $120 jacket discounted by 25 %: 120 × 0.75 = $90.

**Successive changes** require multiplying multipliers, not adding percentages. A 10 % increase then a 10 % decrease: 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99, which is a 1 % loss overall — not zero.

**Percentage error** measures accuracy: (|measured − accepted| ÷ accepted) × 100. If you estimate a length at 95 m but the true value is 102 m, the error is (7 ÷ 102) × 100 ≈ 6.9 %. Always divide by the accepted value.

A Victorian council's rates notice shows a property valued at $520,000 increasing by 6.5 %. The multiplier is 1.065, giving a new valuation of $553,800 — an increase of $33,800.

Percentage error matters because a 2 cm error on 10 cm (20 %) is far more significant than 2 cm on 200 cm (1 %). Context determines whether an error is acceptable.

Percentages appear everywhere: GST at 10 %, rental increase caps, VicHealth statistics, Yoorrook Justice Commission data comparing outcomes across population groups of different sizes.

## Memorable takeaways
- Increase multiplier: (1 + r). Decrease multiplier: (1 − r).
- Successive percentages multiply — they do not add.
- Percentage error = (|measured − accepted| ÷ accepted) × 100.
- Percentages allow fair comparison across different-sized groups.

## Watch out for these traps
- Adding successive percentages (30 % up then 20 % down ≠ 10 % up; it is 1.30 × 0.80 = 1.04, a 4 % increase).
- Dividing by the measured value instead of the accepted value for percentage error.
- Confusing "20 % of 80" (= 16) with "80 increased by 20 %" (= 96).

## Model responses
1. *12 % of 450:* 0.12 × 450 = 54.
2. *$200 up by 8 %:* 200 × 1.08 = $216.
3. *$480 down by 30 %:* 480 × 0.70 = $336.
4. *20 % up then 20 % down:* 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96 — a 4 % decrease, not zero.
5. *Percentage error (350 g estimated, 400 g actual):* (50 ÷ 400) × 100 = 12.5 %.
