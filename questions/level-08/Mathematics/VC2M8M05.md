# VC2M8M05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot

- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics (Measurement).
- **Big ideas:**

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **recognise and use rates** to solve problems that compare two related quantities with **different units of measure**.
- You are learning to set up, calculate and interpret rates in practical contexts.

By the end of this you should be able to:

- Calculate rates from given information.
- Use rates to solve comparison and conversion problems.

## Core content

**1. What is a rate?** A **rate** compares two quantities measured in **different units**. Speed is a rate (km per hour). Price is a rate (dollars per kilogram). A heart rate is beats per minute. The key word is "per" — it signals division. A **ratio**, by contrast, compares quantities with the same unit (e.g. 3 parts sand to 1 part cement). *Quick check:* Is "$4.50 per litre" a rate or a ratio?

**2. Writing rates.** Rates are written as one quantity per one unit of the other: 60 km/h means 60 kilometres for every 1 hour. To find a **unit rate**, divide the first quantity by the second: 240 km in 3 hours → 240 ÷ 3 = 80 km/h. *Quick check:* A car travels 350 km in 5 hours. What is its speed as a unit rate?

**3. Calculating with rates.** Once you know the rate, you can find either quantity:
- Distance = speed × time. If speed = 80 km/h and time = 2.5 h, distance = 200 km.
- Time = distance ÷ speed. 200 km at 80 km/h → 200 ÷ 80 = 2.5 hours.
- Cost = price rate × quantity. $3.20/kg × 4 kg = $12.80.

*Quick check:* Water flows at 15 litres per minute. How much flows in 8 minutes?

**4. Comparing rates.** To compare, express each as a **unit rate**. Shop A: 2 kg of apples for $7.00 → $3.50/kg. Shop B: 3 kg for $9.90 → $3.30/kg. Shop B is cheaper per kilogram. *Quick check:* Worker A paints 12 m² in 3 hours. Worker B paints 20 m² in 5 hours. Who is faster?

**5. Converting rates.** Sometimes you need to change the units. 60 km/h → how many m/s? Convert km to m (×1000) and h to s (÷3600): 60 × 1000 ÷ 3600 ≈ 16.7 m/s. Take it one step at a time and check that units cancel correctly. *Quick check:* Convert 5 m/s to km/h.

**6. Worked example — comparing water usage rates.**
Two Victorian households track their water use over a week. Household A uses 1 400 litres for 4 people. Household B uses 1 050 litres for 3 people.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Household A rate = 1 400 ÷ 4 = 350 L per person per week.
- Household B rate = 1 050 ÷ 3 = 350 L per person per week.
- Both households use water at the same per-person rate.
- To make a fair comparison, you must use the same unit rate — total litres alone is misleading because household sizes differ.

**7. Rates in context.** Rates appear in wages ($25/hour), fuel efficiency (7.5 L/100 km), population density (people/km²), data speeds (Mbps), and rainfall (mm/day). In every case, ask: what two quantities are being compared? What are the units? What does the rate tell me? *Quick check:* A car's fuel efficiency is 8 L/100 km. How much fuel for a 350 km trip?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** VicRoads posts speed limits (km/h) across the state. Melbourne Water tracks household consumption rates (litres per person per day) and publishes usage targets. The Transport Accident Commission uses stopping-distance calculations based on speed rates. The Melbourne train network timetable is built on distance-time rates.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations land management involves practical rate thinking — burn rates for cultural burning (hectares per day under specific conditions), flow rates for river management, and harvest rates to ensure sustainability. Dja Dja Wurrung and other Victorian Traditional Owner groups work with Parks Victoria on cultural-burn planning that requires precise rate calculations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students encounter rates in everyday family contexts — currency exchange rates, cooking quantities (grams per serve), mobile data plans (GB per month), and remittance costs (fees per dollar sent). These real-world connections ground abstract rate reasoning.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You run 2.4 km in 12 minutes. Your friend runs 3.5 km in 20 minutes. Express each as a rate in km/min, then convert to km/h. Who ran faster?
2. **Civic.** Melbourne Water asks households to use no more than 155 litres per person per day. A family of five used 5 600 litres last week. Are they within the target? Show your working.
3. **Vocational.** A plumber charges $95 per hour plus a $70 call-out fee. Write an expression for the total cost of h hours. A job takes 2.5 hours — what is the total cost?
4. **Ethical.** A mobile plan advertises "unlimited data at $1 per GB." The fine print says speed drops to 1.5 Mbps after 40 GB. Discuss whether the advertised rate is misleading. What rate information is missing?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Compare the price of rice at two shops — one sells 5 kg bags and one sells 2 kg bags. Set up unit rates ($/kg) and explain which is better value, noting any assumptions.

## Memorable takeaways
- A rate compares two quantities with different units — look for the word "per."
- Unit rate = first quantity ÷ second quantity.
- Use the unit rate to find missing quantities: multiply or divide.
- To compare fairly, convert to the same unit rate.
- Always include both units when writing a rate (e.g. km/h, not just "80").

## If you need more support

## Stretch yourself
A family is driving from Melbourne to Adelaide (729 km). Their car uses fuel at 7.8 L/100 km. Petrol costs $1.95/L in Melbourne and $2.10/L in the country town where they plan to refuel (halfway). They start with a full 55 L tank. Calculate: how much fuel the full trip requires, whether they can make it halfway on a full tank, how much fuel they need to buy at the country town, the total fuel cost, and the overall cost rate in dollars per kilometre. Then compare this to a train ticket at $65 per person for a family of four. Which is cheaper, and at what family size does driving become the better option? Show all working. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting to use the same units when comparing.** You cannot compare km/h to m/s directly — convert first.
- **Confusing rate and ratio.** A ratio compares same-unit quantities (3 : 1). A rate compares different-unit quantities (km/h).
- **Dividing in the wrong order.** Speed = distance ÷ time, not time ÷ distance. Check that your answer makes sense.
- **Ignoring the "per" unit.** Writing "80" instead of "80 km/h" loses critical information.

## Try drawing this
Draw a speed-distance-time triangle: distance on top, speed and time on the bottom. Show: D = S × T, S = D ÷ T, T = D ÷ S. Then draw a second triangle for cost-rate-quantity problems: total cost on top, unit price and quantity on the bottom.

A note on certainty. Rates are exact when the relationship is constant (e.g. a fixed price per kg). In the real world, rates often vary — speed changes along a journey, water use fluctuates day to day. Calculated answers assume a constant rate unless stated otherwise. Always check whether that assumption is reasonable.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Running speed.* You: 2.4 ÷ 12 = 0.2 km/min = 12 km/h. Friend: 3.5 ÷ 20 = 0.175 km/min = 10.5 km/h. You ran faster.
2. *Melbourne Water target.* Family rate = 5 600 ÷ 7 days ÷ 5 people = 160 L/person/day. Target is 155. They are slightly over (by 5 L/person/day).
3. *Plumber.* Total = 70 + 95h. For h = 2.5: 70 + 95(2.5) = 70 + 237.50 = $307.50.
4. *Mobile plan.* The plan is not truly "$1/GB" at full speed after 40 GB — the effective rate of usable data drops dramatically. Missing information: the speed-throttled rate and what "unlimited" means in practice.
5. *Rice comparison.* Answers vary. Key: calculate $/kg for each, note that larger bags are often (but not always) cheaper per kg.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Total fuel = 7.8 × 729 ÷ 100 = 56.9 L. Half trip = 364.5 km needs 28.4 L — a full 55 L tank covers this easily. At halfway the tank has 55 − 28.4 = 26.6 L. Need another 28.4 L for the second half, so buy 28.4 − 26.6 = 1.8 L minimum (buy more for safety, say 5 L). Fuel cost: first half from the 55 L filled in Melbourne costs 55 × $1.95 = $107.25 for the tank. Country top-up: 5 × $2.10 = $10.50. Total fuel ≈ $117.75 (approximate, depends on exactly how much bought). Cost rate ≈ $117.75 ÷ 729 ≈ $0.16/km. Train: 4 × $65 = $260. Driving is cheaper for 4 people. Break-even: $117.75 ÷ $65 ≈ 1.8 — driving is cheaper for 2+ people.
