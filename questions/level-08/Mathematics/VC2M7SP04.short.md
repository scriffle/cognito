# VC2M7SP04 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Design algorithms involving a sequence of steps and decisions that will sort and classify sets of shapes according to their attributes, and describe how the algorithms work.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** An algorithm is a step-by-step procedure that always produces a correct result. Decision points sort shapes into categories. A well-designed algorithm is clear enough for anyone to follow.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **design algorithms** that sort and classify shapes by their **attributes**.
- You should be able to represent your algorithm as a flowchart or numbered steps and test it on a range of shapes.

## Core content

An **algorithm** is a finite sequence of clear steps that solves a problem every time. For shape classification, each step asks a yes/no question about a measurable **attribute** — number of sides, side lengths, angle sizes, parallel sides.

**Decision points** narrow the possibilities. "Does it have exactly 3 sides?" separates triangles from everything else. The order of questions matters — ask the most discriminating question first.

A **flowchart** makes the logic visible: ovals for start/end, diamonds for decisions (yes/no branches), rectangles for outputs.

**Worked example — classifying triangles.** Step 1: Count sides. If not 3 → "Not a triangle." Step 2: All sides equal? → "Equilateral." Step 3: Exactly two equal? → "Isosceles." Step 4: → "Scalene." Test: sides 5, 5, 5 → Step 2 yes → Equilateral. Sides 3, 4, 5 → Step 2 no, Step 3 no → Scalene. *Follow the thinking:* order questions from most to least restrictive → test with examples → confirm every path gives a correct output.

**Testing and debugging.** Run your algorithm on shapes from every category, including edge cases. A square must be correctly handled by a quadrilateral algorithm — check that the most specific category is tested first.

**Cultural context.** Aboriginal classification systems organise Country, kinship and ecological knowledge through structured rules — moiety and skin-name systems assign categories using inherited and relational decisions, representing sophisticated algorithms developed over tens of thousands of years. Melbourne Museum exhibits, ACMI workshops and Victoria's Digital Technologies curriculum all link algorithmic design to mathematical reasoning.

## Memorable takeaways
- An algorithm always produces the correct result for every valid input.
- Decision points ask yes/no questions about measurable attributes.
- Order questions from most to least discriminating.
- Always test with edge cases.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Any question order works." Order matters — poor ordering misclassifies shapes.
- "A square fails the rectangle test." A square meets all rectangle requirements; check the most specific category first.
- "Testing one shape is enough." Test every category and edge cases to confirm correctness.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Algorithm vs guess.* An algorithm follows defined steps and always works; a guess is unreliable.
- *Question to separate triangles.* "Does the shape have exactly 3 sides?"
- *Flowchart decision shape.* A diamond.
- *Why test edge cases.* They reveal where decision order or wording causes misclassification.
- *Triangle algorithm test.* Sides 5, 5, 8 → Step 2 no (not all equal), Step 3 yes (two equal) → Isosceles. Correct.
