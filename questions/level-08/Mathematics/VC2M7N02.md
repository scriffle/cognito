## What you are learning
- You are learning to **represent natural numbers in expanded notation using powers of 10** and as **products of powers of prime numbers** using exponent notation.
- You are learning that two different representations — place-value expansion and prime factorisation — each reveal different things about a number.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Write any natural number in expanded form using powers of 10.
- Identify whether a number is prime or composite.
- Find the prime factorisation of a natural number using a factor tree or repeated division.
- Express a prime factorisation using exponent notation.

## Core content

**1. Place value and powers of 10.** Our number system is base 10. Each place is worth 10 times the place to its right. We can write these place values as powers of 10: 10⁰ = 1, 10¹ = 10, 10² = 100, 10³ = 1 000, 10⁴ = 10 000. *Quick check:* What is 10⁵ as a whole number?

**2. Expanded notation using powers of 10.** The number 4 703 means 4 × 10³ + 7 × 10² + 0 × 10¹ + 3 × 10⁰. Each digit is multiplied by its matching power of 10. This is **expanded notation**. You can leave out terms with a zero coefficient: 4 × 10³ + 7 × 10² + 3 × 10⁰. *Quick check:* Write 58 012 in expanded notation using powers of 10.

**3. Prime and composite numbers.** A **prime number** has exactly two factors: 1 and itself (e.g. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13). A **composite number** has more than two factors (e.g. 12 = 2 × 2 × 3). The number 1 is neither prime nor composite. *Quick check:* Is 29 prime or composite? Explain.

**4. Prime factorisation.** Every composite number can be broken into a product of prime factors. This is called **prime factorisation**, and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic guarantees it is unique (ignoring order). *Quick check:* Why can't 15 = 5 × 3 be broken down any further?

**5. Factor trees and repeated division.** A **factor tree** starts with the number and branches into factor pairs until every leaf is prime. Alternatively, divide repeatedly by the smallest available prime. Both methods reach the same set of prime factors. *Quick check:* Draw a factor tree for 60.

**6. Worked example: expanded notation and prime factorisation of 3 600.**

*Follow the thinking:*
- Expanded notation: 3 600 = 3 × 10³ + 6 × 10² + 0 × 10¹ + 0 × 10⁰ = 3 × 10³ + 6 × 10².
- Prime factorisation by repeated division:
  - 3 600 ÷ 2 = 1 800
  - 1 800 ÷ 2 = 900
  - 900 ÷ 2 = 450
  - 450 ÷ 2 = 225
  - 225 ÷ 3 = 75
  - 75 ÷ 3 = 25
  - 25 ÷ 5 = 5
  - 5 ÷ 5 = 1
- Collect: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 × 5
- Exponent notation: 2⁴ × 3² × 5²

**7. Why two representations matter.** Expanded notation shows place value — useful for rounding, ordering and estimation. Prime factorisation shows the building blocks — useful for finding HCF, LCM and simplifying fractions. Different representations suit different purposes. *Quick check:* Which representation would you use to find the highest common factor of two numbers?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Australian Synchrotron in Clayton works with numbers spanning many orders of magnitude, expressed using powers of 10 — from nanometre wavelengths (10⁻⁹ m) to gigaelectronvolt energies. AMSI in Melbourne publishes free resources on number representation. The Bureau of Meteorology (Melbourne headquarters) reports rainfall data in powers-of-10 scales.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Many First Nations counting and grouping systems use structured bases — some Torres Strait Islander systems group in twos and fives, demonstrating that base systems are a human design choice, not an inevitability. Research by linguists such as Claire Bowern documents the diversity of Aboriginal number systems. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The base-10 system has roots in ancient India, transmitted through Arabic-speaking scholars to Europe. Melbourne's multicultural classrooms can explore how different civilisations — Babylonian (base 60), Mayan (base 20) — chose different bases.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Australia's population is approximately 26 400 000. Write this in expanded notation using powers of 10. How many digits does it have?
2. **Civic.** The City of Melbourne's annual budget is roughly $600 000 000. Express this as 6 × 10⁸. Your council in Maribyrnong allocates $2 400 000 for parks. Write this in expanded notation and find its prime factorisation.
3. **Vocational.** A warehouse manager in Dandenong South receives 720 identical boxes to stack in equal layers. Find the prime factorisation of 720 and use it to identify three different rectangular arrangements.
4. **Ethical.** A classmate says "1 is a prime number." Explain clearly and respectfully why 1 is excluded from the definition of primes and why this matters for unique factorisation.
5. **Cross-cultural.** You are researching Babylonian base-60 number systems for a Melbourne Museum assignment. Convert 3 661 (base 10) into expanded notation using powers of 10, then discuss why the Babylonians might have preferred base 60 for measuring time and angles.

## Memorable takeaways
- Place value is powers of 10 in disguise.
- 10⁰ = 1 — every base raised to zero is 1.
- Every natural number > 1 is either prime or a unique product of primes.
- Factor trees and repeated division both reach the same prime factorisation.
- Choose expanded notation for place-value questions, prime factorisation for factor questions.

## Stretch yourself
The number 5 040 is sometimes called a "highly composite number" because it has more factors than any smaller positive integer. Find its prime factorisation using exponent notation. Then use the factorisation to calculate exactly how many factors 5 040 has (hint: if n = p^a × q^b × r^c, the number of factors is (a + 1)(b + 1)(c + 1)). Explain why highly composite numbers are useful for dividing things into equal groups. Show all working. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "10⁰ = 0." No — any non-zero base raised to the power 0 equals 1. 10⁰ = 1.
- "1 is a prime number." 1 has only one factor (itself), so it does not meet the definition of prime (exactly two distinct factors).
- "The prime factorisation of 12 is 2 × 6." 6 is not prime — keep factoring until every factor is prime: 12 = 2² × 3.
- "Order matters in prime factorisation." The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic says the factorisation is unique regardless of order.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *What is 10⁵?* 100 000.
2. *58 012 in expanded notation.* 5 × 10⁴ + 8 × 10³ + 0 × 10² + 1 × 10¹ + 2 × 10⁰ (or omitting the zero term: 5 × 10⁴ + 8 × 10³ + 1 × 10¹ + 2 × 10⁰).
3. *Is 29 prime?* Yes — it has exactly two factors: 1 and 29. No integer from 2 to 5 (up to √29) divides it evenly.
4. *Why can't 15 = 5 × 3 be broken down further?* Both 5 and 3 are prime numbers, so the factorisation is complete.
5. *Factor tree for 60.* 60 → 2 × 30 → 2 × 2 × 15 → 2 × 2 × 3 × 5. So 60 = 2² × 3 × 5.
6. *Which representation for HCF?* Prime factorisation — compare the shared prime factors.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Australia's population.* 2 × 10⁷ + 6 × 10⁶ + 4 × 10⁵. It has 8 digits.
2. *Maribyrnong parks budget.* 2 400 000 = 2 × 10⁶ + 4 × 10⁵. Prime factorisation: 2 400 000 = 2⁷ × 3 × 5⁵.
3. *Warehouse boxes.* 720 = 2⁴ × 3² × 5. Arrangements could be 8 × 90, 16 × 45, 10 × 72 (among many others).
4. *Why 1 is not prime.* Prime requires exactly two distinct factors. 1 has only one factor (1 itself). Including 1 would break the uniqueness of prime factorisation.
5. *Babylonian base 60.* 3 661 = 3 × 10³ + 6 × 10² + 6 × 10¹ + 1 × 10⁰. Base 60 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, making it practical for fractions in time (60 minutes) and angles (360°).

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** 5 040 = 2⁴ × 3² × 5 × 7. Number of factors = (4+1)(2+1)(1+1)(1+1) = 5 × 3 × 2 × 2 = 60 factors. Highly composite numbers are useful for equal-group divisions because they divide evenly in many ways — 5 040 items can be split into 60 different equal-group sizes.
