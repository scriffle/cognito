# VC2M7A04 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Investigate, interpret and describe relationships between variables represented in graphs of functions developed from authentic data.
- **Year / Area:** Years 7–8, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** A graph makes the relationship between two variables visible. Authentic data grounds algebra in real situations. Reading a graph means describing what happens as one variable changes.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **investigate, interpret and describe** relationships between variables shown in graphs built from **authentic data**.
- You should be able to move between a table, a graph and a verbal description of the same relationship.

## Core content

Every graph of a function shows how a **dependent variable** (vertical axis) changes as the **independent variable** (horizontal axis) changes. Time almost always goes on the horizontal axis.

**Tables to graphs.** Plot each pair of values as a point *(x, y)* on the **Cartesian plane**. Connect the points for continuous data (temperature over time); leave them as dots for discrete data (goals per round).

**Linear relationships** form a straight line — the rate of change is constant. A phone plan charging $0.10 per text produces a straight-line cost graph. **Non-linear relationships** curve — the rate of change varies. A ball thrown upward traces a curve, not a line.

**Reading the story.** Describe trends in plain language: "As time increases, temperature rises to a peak at noon, then drops." Look for starting values, peaks, flat sections and steep sections. A flat section means the variable is not changing.

**Worked example — Birrarung water level.** Melbourne Water records hourly water levels. Plotted: time (hours) on the horizontal axis, water level (metres) on the vertical. The graph rises gradually, steepens during a rain event, peaks at hour 14, then declines slowly. *Follow the thinking:* label axes → plot points → connect → describe the trend in context.

**Comparing data sets.** Plot two functions on the same axes (e.g., Melbourne vs Mildura temperatures) to make differences visible. Use the same scale so comparisons are fair.

**Cultural context.** Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung knowledge of Birrarung seasonal flow and Gunaikurnai knowledge of Gippsland Lakes water cycles represent long-held understanding of variable relationships in Country. Bureau of Meteorology stations, Melbourne Water gauging data and PTV patronage statistics all provide authentic Victorian data for graphing.

## Memorable takeaways
- A graph makes a relationship between two variables visible.
- Label axes and include units — without them, a graph is meaningless.
- Linear means straight line; non-linear means curved.
- Always describe the graph in the context of the real data.

## Watch out for these traps
- "The graph goes up, so it's good." Rising only means increasing — context determines meaning.
- "Correlation means causation." Two variables moving together does not prove one causes the other.
- "All graphs are straight lines." Many real-world relationships are curved.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Temperature graph axes.* Time on horizontal, temperature on vertical — temperature depends on time.
- *Goals per round — connect dots?* No — discrete data uses separate points.
- *Constant rate of change.* A straight line — equal horizontal steps give equal vertical steps.
- *Flat section.* The dependent variable stays constant while the independent variable increases.
- *Birrarung graph.* Gradual rise, steep rain-event section, peak at hour 14, slow decline — authentic data shows real-world trends.
