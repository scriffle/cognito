## Core content

A **compound event** combines two or more simple events. Flipping two coins gives 2 × 2 = 4 outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT. Use **tree diagrams** or **two-way tables** to list outcomes systematically.

**Theoretical probability:** P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. For two dice, P(sum of 7) = 6/36 = 1/6, because six pairs produce 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1).

**Simulation** runs an experiment digitally many times. In Python, you can simulate rolling two dice 10,000 times and count how often the sum equals 7. At 10 trials the result is noisy; at 10,000 it settles close to 0.1667 — the **long-run frequency** effect.

A spreadsheet approach uses =RANDBETWEEN(1,6) for each die, copies down for hundreds of rows, and =COUNTIF() to tally successes. Experimental probability = successes ÷ trials.

For **independent** compound events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). For a die roll of 5 or 6 *and* a heads on a coin: P = (2/6) × (1/2) = 1/6 ≈ 0.1667.

When reporting results, state the event, number of trials, experimental probability, and theoretical probability. More trials means closer convergence.

Traditional First Nations games from south-eastern Australia — including guessing and prediction games documented by researchers such as John Morieson — embed compound-event thinking within cultural practice.

## Memorable takeaways
- Compound events: multiply the number of outcomes across stages.
- P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) for independent events.
- Simulations estimate probability — more trials means better accuracy.
- Tree diagrams and two-way tables prevent missing outcomes.

## Watch out for these traps
- Adding probabilities instead of multiplying for "and" events.
- Forgetting HT and TH are different outcomes (order matters).
- Expecting small samples to match theory exactly — convergence requires many trials.
