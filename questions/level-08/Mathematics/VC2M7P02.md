## Core content

**1. Relative frequency.** After running an experiment, the **relative frequency** of an outcome = (number of times it occurred) ÷ (total trials). If you roll a die 50 times and get twelve 4s, the relative frequency of 4 is 12/50 = 0.24. The theoretical probability is 1/6 ≈ 0.167. The difference is due to randomness in a small sample. *Quick check:* You flip a coin 20 times and get 13 heads. What is the relative frequency of heads?

**2. The law of large numbers (intuitive version).** As you repeat an experiment more and more times, the relative frequency settles closer to the theoretical probability. 10 flips might give 70% heads; 100 flips might give 55%; 10 000 flips will likely be very close to 50%. *Quick check:* Why do 10 coin flips often look "unfair" while 10 000 look "fair"?

**3. Running a simulation with digital tools.** A **simulation** models a chance experiment electronically. In a spreadsheet: use =RANDBETWEEN(1,6) to simulate a die roll, copy the formula 1 000 times, then use COUNTIF to tally results. Online tools and apps can simulate millions of trials instantly. *Quick check:* What spreadsheet function would you use to simulate flipping a coin?

**4. Comparing predicted and observed results.** Predicted frequency = P × trials. Observed frequency = count from experiment. The comparison reveals whether the model fits reality. If they differ greatly, either the sample is too small or the model is wrong (e.g. the die is biased). *Quick check:* A die is rolled 600 times. You predict 100 sixes. You observe 130. Is the die likely fair?

**5. The effect of sample size.** Small samples produce high **variation** — results jump around. Large samples produce low variation — results stabilise. This is why scientific studies need large samples and why a single cricket match does not prove who is the better team. *Quick check:* Which gives a more reliable estimate of P(Head): 20 flips or 2 000 flips?

**6. Worked example: simulation of a spinner.**

A spinner has three sectors: Red (50%), Blue (30%), Green (20%). A student simulates 1 000 spins using a spreadsheet.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Predicted: Red = 500, Blue = 300, Green = 200.
- Observed (from spreadsheet): Red = 487, Blue = 312, Green = 201.
- Differences: Red is 13 below prediction, Blue is 12 above, Green is 1 above.
- Interpretation: all differences are small relative to 1 000 trials. The observed results are consistent with the theoretical model. Variation this size is expected.

**7. When observed ≠ predicted — what to investigate.** If differences are large (e.g. 180 sixes in 600 rolls), consider: is the sample too small? Is the equipment biased? Is the model wrong? Repeating the experiment with more trials helps distinguish between randomness and a genuine mismatch. *Quick check:* What would you do if your simulation showed 400 heads in 500 flips?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You flip a coin 30 times and get 21 heads. Is the coin biased? Describe what you would do next to investigate, using the concept of sample size.
2. **Civic.** The City of Monash surveys 50 households about recycling and finds 68% recycle correctly. The state average is 55%. Could the difference be due to sample size? Suggest a more reliable survey design.
3. **Vocational.** A quality-control technician at a Melbourne bakery predicts that 2% of loaves will be underweight. In a batch of 200, she finds 8 underweight (4%). Should she stop the production line? Discuss sample size and expected variation.
4. **Ethical.** A gaming company claims their loot box has a 10% chance of a rare item. A player opens 20 boxes and gets zero rares. Is this evidence of false advertising? Use probability and sample-size reasoning.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Your class runs a simulated dice experiment: Group A rolls 30 times, Group B rolls 3 000 times using a spreadsheet. Both groups expect 1/6 for each face. Compare the likely variation in each group's results and explain which provides better evidence about the die's fairness.

## Memorable takeaways
- Relative frequency = observed count ÷ total trials.
- More trials → relative frequency → theoretical probability (law of large numbers).
- Small samples are noisy; large samples are stable.
- Simulations let you run thousands of trials in seconds.
- If observed ≠ predicted, ask: small sample, biased equipment, or wrong model?

## Watch out for these traps
- "My experiment gave different results from the theory, so the theory is wrong." Small samples always vary. Check sample size before doubting the model.
- "More trials guarantee the exact theoretical probability." They get closer but never guarantee exact equality. 1 000 flips might give 49.8% heads — close to 50% but not exactly 50%.
- "Simulations are better than real experiments." Simulations are faster but assume the model is correct. Real experiments test whether the model matches reality.
- "A single unusual result proves bias." One run of 10 heads in a row can happen with a fair coin (P = 1/1 024). Multiple replications are needed to identify genuine bias.

## Model responses

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Relative frequency of 13 heads in 20 flips.* 13/20 = 0.65 = 65%.
2. *Why 10 flips look unfair.* In a small sample, random variation is large relative to the total. A single "extra" head in 10 flips shifts the proportion by 10%, but in 10 000 flips it shifts by 0.01%.
3. *Simulate a coin flip in a spreadsheet.* =RANDBETWEEN(0,1) where 0 = Tail and 1 = Head (or =IF(RAND()<0.5,"H","T")).
4. *130 sixes in 600 rolls — fair die?* Expected: 100. Observed: 130. Difference = 30, which is quite large (30% above prediction). Warrants further investigation — run more trials or test the die's physical balance.
5. *20 flips vs 2 000 flips.* 2 000 flips gives a more reliable estimate because larger samples reduce the effect of random variation.
6. *400 heads in 500 flips.* Relative frequency = 0.80, far from 0.50. This is strong evidence of bias — investigate the coin or the flipping method. Re-run with a different coin to confirm.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *21 heads in 30 flips.* Relative frequency = 0.70. Possibly just random variation in a small sample. Next step: flip 200 more times. If heads remain near 70%, the coin may be biased; if it settles near 50%, the initial result was sample noise.
2. *Monash recycling survey.* 50 households is a small sample. The 68% vs 55% difference could be random variation. A larger sample (500+ households, randomly selected) would give a more reliable estimate.
3. *Bakery quality control.* Predicted defective: 2% of 200 = 4. Observed: 8. This is double the prediction, but in a batch of 200, a difference of 4 loaves could occur by chance. Test another batch before stopping the line.
4. *Loot box.* P(zero rares in 20 boxes) = 0.9²⁰ ≈ 0.12 = 12%. Getting zero rares is unlucky but not improbable (12% chance). Not strong evidence of false advertising — need many more openings or many players' data.
5. *Group comparison.* Group A (30 rolls): expect large variation — each face might appear 3–7 times instead of the expected 5. Group B (3 000 rolls): expect each face to appear close to 500 times (perhaps 480–520). Group B provides much better evidence about fairness because the larger sample reduces relative variation.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Sample space for two dice: 36 outcomes. Sums of 7: {(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)} = 6 outcomes. P(7) = 6/36 = 1/6. Predicted in 5 000 rolls: 833. Simulation might show 821, 847, 839, 828 across four runs. Percentage differences: within ±2% of prediction. Conclusion: observed frequencies cluster near the theoretical value; variation between runs is small because 5 000 is a large sample; the law of large numbers is demonstrated.
