# VC2S8U08 — Science Understanding

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8U08. Distinguish physical changes from chemical changes by looking for signs like colour change, gas production, temperature change or precipitate formation.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Chemical Sciences: Particle Theory & Mixtures).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Physical changes do not produce new substances.
  - Chemical changes do.
  - Tell-tale signs help you decide which has happened.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Numeracy; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **distinguish** physical changes from **chemical changes** by looking for **signs** like colour change, gas production, temperature change or precipitate formation.
- You are learning to back your judgement with evidence.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **physical change**, **chemical change** and **precipitate** in your own words.
- List four common signs of a chemical change.
- Decide whether a real-world change is physical or chemical and explain why.
- Identify one ambiguous case and discuss what test would settle it.

## Core content

**1. Physical changes change form, not substance.** A **physical change** alters appearance, state or shape, but the substance stays the same. Melting ice, dissolving salt and tearing paper are physical changes. *Quick check:* Why is dissolving sugar in tea a physical change?

**2. Chemical changes produce new substances.** A **chemical change** rearranges atoms into new compounds with new properties. Burning, rusting and digestion are chemical changes. *Quick check:* What new substances form when iron rusts?

**3. Sign 1: colour change.** A clear, persistent colour change often signals a chemical change. Iron turning orange-brown when it rusts, or copper turning green over time. *Quick check:* Is a banana turning brown a chemical change?

**4. Sign 2: gas production.** Bubbles can mean gas. If gas appears that was not there before, like fizzing when bicarbonate meets vinegar, a chemical reaction has likely occurred. Boiling water also makes bubbles, but that is a physical change. *Quick check:* How would you tell those two situations apart?

**5. Sign 3: temperature change.** Reactions that release heat are **exothermic**. Reactions that absorb heat are **endothermic**. A noticeable temperature change without an external heater can signal a chemical change. *Quick check:* Why do hand warmers warm up after you snap them?

**6. Sign 4: precipitate formation.** A **precipitate** is a solid that forms when two solutions mix. Mixing silver nitrate with sodium chloride produces a white precipitate of silver chloride. *Quick check:* Is precipitate formation a physical or chemical change?

**7. Worked example: candle burning.** Light a candle. Several changes happen at once. Wax melts (physical, state change). Liquid wax is drawn up the wick (physical). The wax vaporises (physical). The vapour reacts with oxygen in air to produce carbon dioxide and water (chemical). Energy is released as heat and light. *Follow the thinking:* observe each step → assign physical or chemical → list the evidence used.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Industries across Victoria, from cheese-making in the Goulburn Valley to steel-making at Western Port, rely on careful control of physical and chemical changes. EPA Victoria monitors emissions from chemical reactions in industry.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge of cooking, fire and material processing has long used both physical and chemical changes. Heating ochre changes its colour through chemical change. Bunya nuts are processed through physical and chemical steps to remove toxins. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant home cooks across Melbourne use both kinds of change daily. Vietnamese caramelisation, Italian baking, Sudanese fermentation and Indian tempering each rely on chemical reactions. Recognising the chemistry adds to, not replaces, cultural skill.

## Memorable takeaways
- Physical changes the form. Chemical changes the substance.
- Look for colour, gas, temperature, precipitate.
- One sign hints; multiple signs confirm.
- Boiling bubbles is physical. Fizzing reaction bubbles is chemical.
- Most everyday events involve both kinds of change.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Bubbles always mean a chemical change." No. Boiling water bubbles, but that is physical.
- "Colour change always means a chemical change." No. Tinted ice melting may shift apparent colour without a chemical change.
- "Reversible means physical." Often, but not always. Some chemical changes can be reversed.
- "If it looks the same afterwards, no change occurred." No. Many reactions produce small or hidden changes.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why is dissolving sugar in tea physical?* The sugar particles spread among water particles but their chemical structure is unchanged. You could recover the sugar by evaporation.
2. *New substances when iron rusts.* Iron(III) oxide and hydrated forms of iron oxide. The atoms have rearranged with oxygen and water from the air.
3. *Banana browning.* Chemical. Enzymes in the banana react with oxygen, producing brown pigments.
4. *Boiling vs fizzing bubbles.* Boiling produces bubbles of the same substance you started with (water vapour) and stops when heat is removed. Fizzing comes from a new gas (often CO₂) and continues without heating, with other signs (temperature, smell, colour) sometimes present.
5. *Hand warmers.* Iron, salt and water react with oxygen to form iron oxide, releasing heat. The temperature rise without external heating signals a chemical change.
6. *Precipitate formation.* Chemical. New substances are produced when two solutions mix and form an insoluble solid.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Egg in hot pan.* Egg white turns from clear to white (chemical change, denatured proteins). Steam rises from the pan (physical change, water boils to vapour). Smell of cooked egg (chemical change, new compounds released). Evidence: colour change, change of state, new odours.
2. *Whittlesea compost.* Decomposers break down organic matter, producing heat (chemical) and releasing CO₂ (chemical). Water evaporates from the heap (physical). The compost gradually crumbles as fibres break down (mostly chemical at the molecular level, with physical breakdown by macroinvertebrates).
3. *Brunswick baker.* Yeast metabolises sugars, producing CO₂ (chemical change). The CO₂ inflates pockets in the dough (physical, gas expanding). Heat in the oven sets the proteins (chemical). Steam rises (physical).
4. *Fire tornado.* Risks: open flame, flammable vapours, lack of containment. Alternatives: controlled candle combustion under a fume hood, or a video demonstration paired with a discussion.
5. *Wadawurrung clay heating.* Heating clay drives off water (physical) and rearranges minerals (chemical). Discuss with the Elder's permission and credit, using only published, community-approved material.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Demonstration: drop pieces of magnesium ribbon into dilute hydrochloric acid in a test tube.* Signs: gas bubbles (hydrogen), temperature rise (exothermic), colour fades as ribbon dissolves. Equipment: small ribbon strips, dilute HCl, tongs, eye protection, fume hood. Method: add ribbon to acid in a tube, observe for two minutes. Safety: low concentration, small quantity, eye protection, ventilation. Predicted observations stated and explained.
