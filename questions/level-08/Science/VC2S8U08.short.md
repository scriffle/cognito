# VC2S8U08 — Science Understanding (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Distinguish physical changes from chemical changes by looking for signs like colour change, gas production, temperature change or precipitate formation.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Science.
- **Big ideas:** Physical changes the form. Chemical changes the substance. Tell-tale signs help you decide.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **distinguish** physical changes from **chemical changes** by looking for **signs** like colour change, gas, temperature change and precipitates.
- You should be able to explain your judgement with evidence.

## Core content

A **physical change** alters appearance, state or shape but the substance stays the same: melting ice, dissolving salt, tearing paper. A **chemical change** rearranges atoms into new compounds with new properties: burning, rusting, digestion.

Four common signs of a chemical change: a clear colour change, production of a new gas, a temperature change without external heating (exothermic releases heat, endothermic absorbs it), and a **precipitate**, which is a solid formed when two solutions mix.

**Worked example.** A burning candle. Wax melts (physical, state change). Liquid wax travels up the wick (physical). Wax vaporises (physical). The vapour reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water (chemical), releasing heat and light. Several changes happen at once.

**Cultural context.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge of cooking, fire and material processing has long used both physical and chemical changes. Heating ochre changes its colour through chemical change. Bunya nuts are processed to remove toxins. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Physical changes the form. Chemical changes the substance.
- Look for colour, gas, temperature, precipitate.
- One sign hints; multiple signs confirm.
- Most everyday events involve both kinds of change.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Bubbles always mean a chemical change." No. Boiling water bubbles, but that is physical.
- "Colour change always means chemical." No. Tinted ice melting may shift apparent colour.
- "Reversible means physical." Often, but not always.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why is dissolving sugar in tea physical?* The sugar's chemical structure is unchanged. You could recover it by evaporation.
- *Banana browning: physical or chemical?* Chemical. Enzymes react with oxygen to produce brown pigments.
- *Boiling vs fizzing bubbles.* Boiling makes bubbles of the same substance you started with. Fizzing makes a new gas, often with other signs.
- *Why do hand warmers warm up?* Iron, salt and water react with oxygen to form iron oxide, releasing heat. The temperature rise without external heating signals a chemical change.
- *Short summary of a burning candle.* Several changes at once: wax melts, rises, vaporises (physical), then reacts with oxygen to produce CO₂ and water (chemical), releasing heat and light.
