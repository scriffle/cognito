# VC2S8I06 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I06. Evaluate scientific methods and conclusions by identifying assumptions, sources of error, conflicting evidence and unanswered questions.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Evaluating is part of doing science, not afterthought.
  - Assumptions are silent until you name them.
  - Conflicting evidence and unanswered questions point the way forward.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate** scientific methods and conclusions by **identifying** assumptions, sources of error, conflicting evidence and unanswered questions.
- You are learning to read science writing, including your own, with a fair but tough eye.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **assumption**, **systematic error** and **conflicting evidence** in your own words.
- Identify two assumptions in a typical school experiment.
- Distinguish a methodological problem from a real-world limitation.
- Suggest a follow-up question that responds to a finding.

## Core content

**1. Evaluation is built in.** Good scientists evaluate their own work as they go, not just at the end. Asking "what could go wrong?" is part of the method. *Quick check:* Why is evaluation done before publishing better than after?

**2. Naming assumptions.** An **assumption** is a belief you carry into a study without testing it. Common ones: that the instrument is calibrated, that conditions are stable, that participants are typical. Naming assumptions makes them testable. *Quick check:* What is one assumption when measuring temperature with a phone app?

**3. Sources of error revisited.** **Random errors** are reduced by repeating. **Systematic errors** are reduced by calibration and design. Sample-size limits, observer bias and equipment drift all show up in real work. *Quick check:* What might cause systematic error in a stopwatch experiment?

**4. Worked example: school cafeteria food-waste study.** Method: weigh leftover food on five lunchtimes. Assumptions: similar menus each day, similar attendance, the scales work. Errors: random (some students hide food), systematic (scale not calibrated). Conflicting evidence: a student survey says waste is mostly bread, but the weight data say it is mostly fruit. Unanswered question: is the difference because students underreport one item? *Follow the thinking:* state the question → list assumptions → name errors → spot conflicts → propose next study.

**5. Conflicting evidence is informative.** Two studies can disagree because of method, sample or chance. Comparing the methods is the first step. Studies that look at the same question with different designs together produce stronger conclusions. *Quick check:* Why is one strong study sometimes less convincing than three medium ones?

**6. Unanswered questions are a feature.** Every study leaves something open. A good conclusion lists those questions. They guide future work and signal honesty. *Quick check:* Why does a finished science report still end with questions?

**7. Self-evaluation in plain language.** When writing your evaluation, use phrases like "this suggests", "appears to", "within the conditions tested". Avoid "proves" unless your study is designed to test causation rigorously, which most school studies are not. *Quick check:* Replace "this proves that exercise improves grades" with a more honest version.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** EPA Victoria publishes peer-reviewed reports that include explicit lists of method limitations and assumptions. Reading one of these reports is a model for student work.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Country Needs People and Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish program evaluations that include cultural protocols and limitations. These reports show how Western evaluation frameworks can sit alongside community-led review processes. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Researchers from Greek, Lebanese and Anglo-Australian backgrounds at Melbourne hospitals have evaluated their own diabetes screening trials and updated them when early results were biased toward English-speaking patients. The willingness to publish limitations is part of trustworthy science.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A friend claims a new sleep app helped them sleep better. List two assumptions in that claim and one piece of evidence that would change your view.
2. **Civic.** A council in Geelong concludes from one survey that residents oppose new bike lanes. Identify a limitation and propose a stronger evaluation.
3. **Vocational.** A junior food-tech in Footscray reports a new packaging extends shelf life. Propose two follow-up questions that would lift the reliability of the claim.
4. **Ethical.** A study on student attention used videos of classmates without consent. Identify the ethical issue and explain how it weakens the conclusion.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung rangers and a university team find different conclusions about waterbird recovery on a wetland. Suggest a fair process for combining the evidence and naming what is unanswered.

## Memorable takeaways
- Evaluate as you go, not only at the end.
- Name assumptions to make them testable.
- Random repeats. Systematic calibrates.
- Conflicts and gaps point to next steps.
- Hedge honestly. "Suggests" beats "proves".

## Watch out for these traps
- "Evaluation is just self-criticism." No. It is what makes a finding trustworthy.
- "Assumptions are weaknesses." No. Naming them is a strength. Hidden ones are weaknesses.
- "If two studies disagree, one is wrong." No. They may be measuring different things or have different methods.
- "A finished report has no open questions." No. Honest reports name what remains.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why evaluate before publishing?* It strengthens the work and saves the reader time. After-the-fact corrections damage trust and waste effort.
2. *Phone temperature app assumption.* That the phone sensor is accurate, calibrated and not affected by case heat or charging.
3. *Stopwatch systematic error.* Consistent reaction-time delay in starting and stopping. Average reaction time can shift the recorded time by tenths of a second.
4. *Why three medium studies can outweigh one strong study.* Independent replication reduces the chance that a single odd result is driving the conclusion.
5. *Why finished reports still end with questions.* No study answers everything. Naming gaps directs the next study and shows the limits of what has been shown.
6. *Honest version of "this proves exercise improves grades".* "In this small sample, students who exercised regularly tended to score higher, suggesting a possible link worth further study."

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Sleep app claim.* Assumptions: app measurements are accurate, sleep quality is captured by the app's metric. Evidence that would change your view: a controlled comparison with a wearable and a sleep diary across two weeks.
2. *Geelong bike-lane survey.* Limitation: one survey may not represent residents who do not respond. Stronger evaluation: stratified sample by suburb and age, multiple methods including in-person and online, plus pilot lane usage data.
3. *Footscray food packaging.* What was the storage temperature range? Was the test repeated across product batches and seasons? Were sensory and microbial checks both used?
4. *Student attention study.* Issue: filming without consent breaches privacy and may bias behaviour. The breach also undermines trust, which weakens recruitment for any follow-up.
5. *Wadawurrung waterbird disagreement.* Hold a joint review meeting with both teams, table the methods, sample sites and sampling windows, identify shared and different assumptions, write a joint summary listing agreement, disagreement and unanswered questions.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Article: an EPA Victoria report on PFAS in groundwater.* Two assumptions: laboratory detection limits are stable across the sampling period; sites sampled represent broader catchment risk. Source of error: variability between sampling teams. Conflicting evidence: a community-collected dataset shows a higher level at one site. Unanswered question: how do PFAS levels change across seasons in this catchment?
