## Core content

**1. What an energy audit is.** An **energy audit** is a structured check of how energy enters and is used in a building. It looks at appliances, lighting, heating, cooling, hot water, insulation and behaviour. *Quick check:* Why is auditing better than guessing where energy goes?

**2. Kilowatt-hours and the bill.** A **kilowatt-hour (kWh)** is a unit of energy: one kilowatt of power used for one hour. Bills usually show daily average kWh, peak/off-peak rates, supply charges and any solar export. *Quick check:* If a 2 kW heater runs for 3 hours, how many kWh does it use?

**3. Big appliances dominate.** In Melbourne homes, heating, cooling, hot water and large appliances like fridges and dryers usually use the most energy. Lighting and small electronics are often smaller shares than people guess. *Quick check:* Which is likely to use more energy in winter, a heat-pump heater or a halogen lamp left on?

**4. Building design matters.** **Insulation** in walls, ceilings and floors slows heat moving in or out. Window orientation, shading and double glazing reduce summer heat gain and winter heat loss. Older Victorian terraces were built before strict efficiency rules. *Quick check:* Why do shaded north-facing windows perform differently in summer and winter in Melbourne?

**5. Worked example: an energy audit at a Werribee home.** Step 1: read 12 months of bills. Step 2: list main appliances with rated power and typical hours used per week. Step 3: walk through and check insulation, draughts, hot-water settings. Step 4: rank improvements by saving and cost. Common winners: replacing a gas heater with a reverse-cycle heat pump, sealing draughts, switching to LED lighting, installing rooftop solar. *Follow the thinking:* measure → analyse → rank → recommend.

**6. Season and climate.** Melbourne has cool wet winters and warm dry summers, with hot extremes. Heating dominates winter use. Cooling spikes during heatwaves. In Mildura or Wodonga, cooling can dominate. Each climate zone calls for different priorities. *Quick check:* Why might a Mornington house and a Mildura house have different audit recommendations?

**7. Behaviour adds to design.** Even an efficient home can use more energy if heaters run all day or fridges sit at 1°C. Simple habits, like programming heaters and washing in cold water, can cut bills meaningfully. *Quick check:* Why is "set and forget" thermostat settings often more efficient than constant adjustment?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Sustainability Victoria runs the Victorian Energy Upgrades program. The Victorian Government publishes minimum standards for rentals, including efficient heating. Solar Victoria offers rebates that change over time.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander shelter design across Australia historically used materials, orientation and seasonal practice to manage temperature with minimal external energy. Researchers including the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish on these traditions. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant families across Melbourne adapt traditional cooking and food-preservation methods that change household energy patterns: Vietnamese rice-cooker use, Italian preserving days and Greek summer outdoor kitchens. These habits link culture and energy.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Switching off lights solves the bill." No. It helps but it is small compared to heating, cooling and hot water.
- "More expensive appliances are always more efficient." No. Look at the energy rating and rated power.
- "Solar panels eliminate the need to be efficient." No. Efficiency reduces what you need to generate or store.
- "Old houses cannot be made efficient." No. Many low-cost retrofits work for older homes, including draught proofing, floor insulation and curtains.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why audit instead of guess.* Guessing misses the largest users and overstates the small ones. Measurement directs effort to the biggest savings.
2. *kWh from 2 kW × 3 hours.* 6 kWh.
3. *Heat pump vs halogen lamp.* Heat pump. It moves much more energy per hour than a lamp uses. Lighting matters but is usually a smaller share.
4. *North-facing windows in summer vs winter.* In summer, the high sun is easily shaded by eaves so heat gain is limited. In winter, the lower sun reaches further into the room, providing free heating if not blocked.
5. *Mornington vs Mildura priorities.* Mornington: heating, draught sealing, ceiling insulation. Mildura: cooling, shading, evaporative or efficient air-conditioning, reflective roof.
6. *Set and forget thermostats.* Modern controls maintain a steady temperature with less energy than constantly chasing comfort with manual changes.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Weekend audit.* Read the last bill. Walk through the home noting big appliances and draughts. Estimate hours per week for the heater, fridge and dryer. Rank by potential saving.
2. *Banyule rental upgrades.* Draught-proof doors and windows, install LED lighting, and add ceiling insulation. Each is low cost relative to savings and possible under most rental agreements.
3. *Geelong school audit.* Heating and cooling controls and zoning. Lighting on motion sensors. Hot water systems and timer use. Address the largest energy users first.
4. *Renter and landlord issue.* Issue: comfort and health, plus emissions. Practical actions: consult Consumer Affairs Victoria on minimum rental standards, request a written response, and apply low-cost personal measures (heavy curtains, draught stoppers).
5. *Wadawurrung shelter design.* Discuss with permission and credit. Compare orientation, shading and material choices with current Australian design standards. Use only published, community-approved material.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Three suburbs:* Werribee (cool wet winters, hot summers): heating, cooling, shading. Hampton (coastal): humidity, condensation control. Healesville (cool, treed): heating, ventilation, bushfire-aware design. Two recommendations per zone with reasoning, citing climate priorities.
