# VC2S8I05 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I05. Analyse processed data to identify patterns, trends, relationships and anomalies.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Analysis turns numbers into meaning.
  - Patterns, trends and relationships are different things.
  - Anomalies deserve attention, not deletion.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse** processed data to **identify** patterns, trends, relationships and anomalies.
- You are learning to read your own graphs and tables critically.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **pattern**, **trend**, **relationship** and **anomaly** in your own words.
- Describe a trend in plain language with direction, magnitude and timeframe.
- Identify anomalies and propose at least two possible causes.
- Compare two datasets to spot a relationship.

## Core content

**1. Pattern, trend, relationship and anomaly.** A **pattern** is a repeating feature, like cycles in tides. A **trend** is a steady change over time, like rising city temperatures. A **relationship** links two variables, like more shade with cooler surfaces. An **anomaly** is a point or period that does not fit. *Quick check:* Which of these terms applies to a yearly summer peak in ice-cream sales?

**2. Reading a trend properly.** Describe direction (up, down, flat), magnitude (how much) and timeframe (how long). "Temperatures rose by about 1.2°C between 1960 and 2020 in Melbourne" beats "It got hotter". *Quick check:* Rewrite "the line goes up" as a proper trend statement using made-up numbers.

**3. Spotting relationships.** Plot one variable against another and look at the shape. Positive relationships rise from left to right. Negative ones fall. Curves and clusters tell their own story. *Quick check:* What does a horizontal scatter cloud suggest about a relationship?

**4. Worked example: heatwave deaths in Melbourne.** Real data from the 2009 Melbourne heatwave shows hospital admissions and excess deaths spiking on the hottest days. Pattern: yearly peaks in summer. Trend: rising frequency of extreme heat days over decades. Relationship: high temperatures correlate with hospital admissions. Anomaly: a sudden, larger-than-usual spike in late January. *Follow the thinking:* identify pattern → describe trend → quantify relationship → flag anomaly → ask why.

**5. Anomalies are clues.** Anomalies can be measurement errors, recording errors, real outliers, or signals of an event. Investigate before deleting. A spike in air-quality readings might be a faulty sensor or a nearby fire. *Quick check:* Why is the rule "investigate, do not delete" important?

**6. Comparing two datasets.** Lining up two datasets side-by-side can reveal links. Rainfall and grass length, sleep and exam scores, housing density and tree cover. Strong-looking links still need a check for cause and chance. *Quick check:* Why does correlation not always mean causation?

**7. Tools to help analysis.** Spreadsheet functions like AVERAGE, MEDIAN, MAX, MIN and STDEV speed up analysis. Sorting and filtering reveal hidden structure. Use formulas, not eyeballing, where possible. *Quick check:* What does standard deviation tell you that an average does not?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Department of Health publishes weekly hospital admissions data, and the Bureau of Meteorology Australia publishes daily weather. Together these allow students to study heatwave health relationships using real Melbourne datasets.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Long-running observation of seasons and species across Country supports detection of change. Reports from the Indigenous Knowledge Institute and Country Needs People note shifts in flowering times, fish runs and bird arrivals identified by Traditional Owner ranger groups, including in the Wadawurrung and Gunaikurnai regions. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Vietnamese-Australian doctors at Footscray Hospital have analysed asthma and diabetes patterns in their patient population to design culturally appropriate care. Indian-Australian researchers at Melbourne University analyse traffic noise patterns near densely populated suburbs to support better planning.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You record your sleep duration and Maths test scores for a term. Describe how you would check for a relationship and what one anomaly might look like.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Yarra Ranges has tree-canopy data from 2010 and 2024 by suburb. Suggest a method to identify suburbs with the strongest negative trend, and what to recommend.
3. **Vocational.** A junior environmental analyst sees a sudden drop in dissolved oxygen at one creek site. Outline three possible causes before declaring it a real ecological event.
4. **Ethical.** A study compares student data across schools. The strongest "relationship" is between postcode and grades. Identify the ethical issue and propose a more useful question to investigate.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Yorta Yorta rangers and ecologists analyse decades of bird-arrival dates on the Murray. Outline how to describe the pattern, trend and any anomaly together respectfully.

## Memorable takeaways
- Patterns repeat. Trends climb or fall. Relationships link.
- Anomalies are clues, not litter.
- Direction, magnitude, timeframe.
- Correlation is a hint. Causation needs more.
- Use formulas, not eyeballs.

## Stretch yourself

## Watch out for these traps
- "Two things moving together must be linked." No. Coincidence is common, especially with short timeframes.
- "Anomalies are mistakes." No. Investigate first.
- "Strong correlation proves cause." No. Cause needs a mechanism and ruling out other factors.
- "Bigger numbers always mean stronger evidence." No. Sample size, scatter and study design also matter.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Yearly summer peak in ice-cream sales.* Pattern. It repeats reliably each year.
2. *Proper trend statement.* "Between 2015 and 2024, average daily downloads rose by about 200 per year, an overall increase of around 40%."
3. *Horizontal scatter cloud.* No clear relationship. Changes in one variable do not predict the other.
4. *Why "investigate, do not delete".* Deleting hides faults or real signals. Investigating gives you a reason that improves the work.
5. *Why correlation is not causation.* Two variables can move together by chance, by sharing a common cause, or by reverse direction. A mechanism and controlled tests are needed to claim cause.
6. *What standard deviation adds.* It tells you how spread out the values are around the mean. Two datasets with the same mean can have very different reliability.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Sleep and Maths scores.* Make a scatter plot, calculate the correlation, and note any anomaly such as a high score on a low-sleep day. Possible cause of an anomaly: a familiar topic or a poorly written test item.
2. *Yarra Ranges canopy.* For each suburb, calculate the change in canopy cover from 2010 to 2024. Rank by largest negative change. Recommend planting in the most affected suburbs and protecting remaining canopy with planning rules.
3. *Creek dissolved oxygen drop.* Sensor fault, recent rainfall flushing organic matter, or upstream contamination from runoff. Verify by checking the calibration log and comparing with nearby sites before reporting.
4. *Postcode and grades.* Issue: confounding by income, school resources and language background. Better question: how does access to in-school tutoring affect grades within similar postcodes?
5. *Yorta Yorta bird arrivals.* Describe the long-term pattern of seasonal arrival, the trend of earlier arrival in recent decades, and any anomaly such as a missed year. Acknowledge ranger observations as primary evidence and align language with the corporation's own reports.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Dataset: Melbourne mean annual rainfall, 1900–2024.* Pattern: clear seasonal cycle in monthly data. Trend: small overall decline in annual totals since the 1990s, with high year-to-year variability. Anomaly: the 2010–2011 La Niña years are unusually wet. Relationship: drier years align with hotter years in temperature data, suggesting a heat-rainfall link. Next step: pair with bushfire frequency to test for compounding effects.
