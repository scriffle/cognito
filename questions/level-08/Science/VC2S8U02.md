## What you are learning
- You are learning that **cells** are the basic **building blocks** of all life, and that different types of cells have **specialised** structures that carry out **specific functions**.
- You are learning to link a cell's structure to what it does.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **cell**, **organelle** and **specialisation** in your own words.
- Name three organelles and what each does.
- Compare a plant cell with an animal cell.
- Describe how the structure of one specialised cell suits its function.

## Core content

**1. Cells are the unit of life.** A **cell** is the smallest unit that can carry out the processes of life. Some organisms are a single cell, like bacteria and yeast. Others, like humans, are made of trillions. *Quick check:* What is the smallest organism you can think of, and how do you know it is alive?

**2. Inside a cell: organelles.** **Organelles** are tiny structures inside cells that do specific jobs. The nucleus stores DNA. Mitochondria release energy. The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves. Plant cells also have chloroplasts and a cell wall. *Quick check:* Which organelle would be busiest in a muscle cell during a sprint?

**3. Plant vs animal cells.** Both have a nucleus, mitochondria and a membrane. Plant cells add a rigid cell wall, chloroplasts for photosynthesis and a large central vacuole. *Quick check:* Why do plant cells have a cell wall and animal cells do not?

**4. Specialisation: structure matches function.** A **specialised** cell has a structure that suits its job. Red blood cells are small, flexible and have no nucleus, so they can carry oxygen efficiently. Nerve cells are long, with branches, so they can carry signals quickly. *Quick check:* What does the long shape of a root hair cell help it do?

**5. Worked example: comparing red blood cells and palisade cells.** Red blood cells: biconcave disc, no nucleus, full of haemoglobin, carry oxygen. Palisade cells: tall, packed with chloroplasts, sit near the leaf surface, carry out photosynthesis. *Follow the thinking:* what is the job → what shape helps → what organelles are needed → how does the cell sit in its tissue.

**6. Microscopes opened cells up.** Light microscopes magnify around 1000 times and revealed cells in the 1600s. Modern electron microscopes magnify millions of times and show organelles in detail. Many discoveries followed each leap in tools. *Quick check:* Why is "we cannot see it" not the same as "it does not exist"?

**7. Common misreadings of pictures.** Textbook diagrams simplify cells. Real cells are crowded, three-dimensional and dynamic. Use diagrams to learn the parts, but remember they are models. *Quick check:* Name one feature of a real cell that a 2D textbook diagram cannot show.

- **Victorian anchor.** Bionic Vision Australia and the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute, both based in Melbourne, study specialised cells in the eye and immune system. Their public outreach explains how cell structure relates to disease.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health programs in Victoria, including those run by the Victorian Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisation, support science and health-careers pathways. Aboriginal-led health research recognises that culturally responsive care improves outcomes for chronic disease, where cell-level mechanisms matter. ## Watch out for these traps
- "All cells look the same." No. Cells are highly varied because functions are varied.
- "Plant cells have everything animal cells have, plus extra." No. Animal cells lack chloroplasts and cell walls but have features plant cells lack, like specialised muscle structures.
- "If we cannot see it, it does not matter." No. Subcellular structures drive most of what cells do.
- "Diagrams show real cells exactly." No. They simplify on purpose.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Smallest living organism.* Bacteria, single-celled. They consume nutrients, grow, reproduce and respond, the basic signs of life.
2. *Busiest organelle in a sprinting muscle cell.* Mitochondria. They release energy from glucose for the muscle to contract repeatedly.
3. *Why plant cells have a wall and animal cells do not.* Plants do not move and depend on the wall for shape and support. Animals move and need flexibility, so a wall would be a barrier.
4. *Long shape of a root hair cell.* Increases the surface area in contact with soil for absorbing water and nutrients.
5. *Why "we cannot see it" is not "it does not exist".* Many real things are too small for the human eye. Tools that magnify or detect them prove they are there.
6. *One feature a 2D diagram cannot show.* Three-dimensional crowding and movement of organelles inside the cell.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Cheek cell under microscope.* You should see the cell membrane, the cytoplasm, and the nucleus. You would not see individual mitochondria clearly under a school light microscope.
2. *Council cells night.* A neuron exhibit on signal transmission, a chloroplast exhibit on photosynthesis, an immune cell exhibit on infection response. Each links structure to function.
3. *Pathology slide staining.* Stains bind selectively to parts of the cell, raising contrast. The nucleus often appears purple after one common stain, making it easy to count and check.
4. *Student blood ethics.* Issue: medical risk and consent. Fix: use prepared slides or computer simulations to study red blood cells.
5. *Yorta Yorta unit on cells.* Begin with permission and partnership with the Yorta Yorta Nation Aboriginal Corporation. Co-design the unit. Use only published, community-approved material on plants. Credit knowledge holders. Frame the unit around what Yorta Yorta people choose to share.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Three cells: human white blood cell, eucalyptus palisade cell, yeast cell.* Comparison table covers job, key structure, key organelle, and a researcher anchor (e.g., Walter and Eliza Hall Institute for the white blood cell, La Trobe plant biology for palisade cells, Monash microbiology for yeast). Notes one limit of textbook diagrams in showing real cell behaviour.
