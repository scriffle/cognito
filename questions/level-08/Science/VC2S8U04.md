## What you are learning
- You are learning that matter and energy **flow** through ecosystems via **food webs** and **food pyramids**, and that changes to any part of the system can **affect** the whole ecosystem.
- You are learning to trace one energy pathway and one matter pathway through a real Victorian ecosystem.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **producer**, **consumer**, **decomposer** and **trophic level** in your own words.
- Draw a simple food web for a Victorian habitat with at least six species.
- Explain why energy is lost between trophic levels.
- Predict one knock-on effect of removing a species from a web.

## Core content

**1. Producers start the energy.** **Producers** are organisms, mostly plants and algae, that convert sunlight into chemical energy through photosynthesis. They are the base of every ecosystem. *Quick check:* Why does almost all life depend on producers?

**2. Consumers and decomposers.** **Consumers** eat other organisms. **Decomposers**, like bacteria and fungi, break down dead matter and recycle nutrients. Without decomposers, ecosystems would choke on waste. *Quick check:* What happens to a fallen leaf in a healthy forest?

**3. Trophic levels and pyramids.** A **trophic level** is a step in the food chain. Producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, and so on. A **food pyramid** shows the energy or biomass at each level. Energy is lost as heat through respiration at every step, so each level has less energy than the one below. *Quick check:* Why is the top of an energy pyramid usually narrow?

**4. Worked example: a Wadawurrung grassland near Ballarat.** Producers: kangaroo grass and wallaby grass. Primary consumers: Eastern Grey Kangaroos, native bees, grasshoppers. Secondary consumers: brown falcons, eastern brown snakes, magpies. Decomposers: fungi and dung beetles. Energy flows: sunlight → grass → kangaroo → falcon. Matter cycles: nitrogen in soil → grass → grasshopper → magpie → droppings → soil. *Follow the thinking:* identify producers → list consumers by level → name decomposers → trace one energy and one matter pathway.

**5. Food webs are networks, not chains.** Most species eat or are eaten by several others. A **food web** is a connected network of food chains. Removing one species pulls multiple threads at once. *Quick check:* Why is a single food chain usually a misleading picture?

**6. Matter cycles, energy flows.** Carbon, nitrogen and water move in cycles. They are reused. Energy flows one direction: in as sunlight, out as heat. The Sun keeps the system running. *Quick check:* Why is "energy is recycled" wrong, while "carbon is recycled" is right?

**7. Disturbance and resilience.** Fire, drought, invasive species and clearing all disturb ecosystems. Resilient systems recover. Some changes pass tipping points and shift to a new state. *Quick check:* Name one Victorian disturbance from the last 20 years and one species that responded.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Parks Victoria manages ecosystems across the state. The Western Treatment Plant at Werribee has become a Ramsar-listed wetland used by tens of thousands of migratory birds, an example of an engineered ecosystem.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wadawurrung, Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Gunaikurnai, Yorta Yorta and other Victorian peoples have managed ecosystems through cool burning, water management and seasonal harvesting for thousands of years. Researchers including Bruce Pascoe and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute have published peer-reviewed work on this. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Sri Lankan-Australian, Vietnamese-Australian and Italian-Australian community gardeners across Melbourne practise composting and recycling that mirror natural matter cycles. Their gardens host pollinators that link to native ecosystems.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You watch a magpie eat a worm in your backyard. Trace the energy back to its source and the matter back to soil.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Hobsons Bay debates removing invasive blackberry along a creek. Predict two ecosystem effects, including one positive and one trade-off.
3. **Vocational.** A junior ranger at Point Nepean must explain how rabbits affect the food web. Outline three changes the team should expect after a rabbit population falls sharply.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to build a closed terrarium with native plants and insects. Identify two ethical considerations and propose a humane design.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Energy is recycled in ecosystems." No. Energy flows one way: sunlight in, heat out.
- "Top predators are the most important." No. Producers and decomposers are equally critical.
- "Removing pests has no downsides." No. Even invasive species often have unexpected roles.
- "Aboriginal land management is unscientific." No. It is observation-based ecological knowledge developed over millennia, increasingly published in peer-reviewed work.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why does almost all life depend on producers?* Producers convert sunlight into chemical energy. Without them, no other trophic level has an energy supply.
2. *Fate of a fallen leaf.* Decomposers, mostly fungi and bacteria, break it down. Nutrients return to soil and become available to plants.
3. *Why energy pyramids narrow at the top.* Energy is lost as heat at each level, so less energy is available to support fewer top consumers.
4. *Why a single food chain is misleading.* Most species eat several others. A chain hides links and overstates the impact of removing any one species.
5. *Why "energy is recycled" is wrong but "carbon is recycled" is right.* Energy leaves the system as heat and must be replaced by sunlight. Carbon atoms move between living and non-living parts of the system without leaving.
6. *Victorian disturbance and a responder.* The 2009 Black Saturday fires shifted vegetation in mountain ash forests; the Leadbeater's possum population dropped sharply because hollow-bearing trees were lost.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Magpie eats worm.* Energy: sunlight → grass and leaves → microbes and detritus eaten by worms → magpie. Matter: soil minerals → plants → litter → worm → magpie → droppings back to soil.
2. *Hobsons Bay blackberry.* Positive: native plant regeneration along the creek; better habitat for native birds and frogs. Trade-off: short-term loss of cover for some birds, possible erosion if removal is not staged.
3. *Point Nepean rabbits.* Vegetation may recover and increase plant cover. Predator species that rely on rabbits, like raptors, may decline. Soil erosion from grazing may slow.
4. *Native terrarium ethics.* Sourcing should not deplete local populations; collection often needs permits. Design must include suitable habitat and food, plus a humane release plan if conditions deteriorate.
5. *Yorta Yorta wetland restoration.* Re-establish native producers (reeds, lilies), reintroduce flow that supports decomposers and primary consumers (invertebrates), and monitor secondary consumers (waterbirds). Engage rangers in design and monitoring under a partnership agreement that credits their knowledge.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Ecosystem: alpine bog on Bogong High Plains.* Producers: sphagnum moss, alpine grasses. Primary consumers: bogong moths, native rodents. Secondary consumers: feral cats, ravens, mountain pygmy-possums. Decomposers: peat-forming microbes. Disturbance: warming reducing moth migration. Knock-on effects: pygmy-possum food collapse, predator pressure shift, soil drying altering peat formation. Mechanisms named for each.
