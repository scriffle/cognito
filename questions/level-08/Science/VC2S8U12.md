# VC2S8U12 — Science Understanding

## What you are learning
- You are learning that the **changing positions** of Earth, the Sun and the Moon cause **predictable cycles** including **seasons**, **eclipses** and **tides**.
- You are learning to use a simple geometry to explain what you see in the sky and at the coast.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **orbit**, **rotation** and **eclipse** in your own words.
- Explain why Melbourne is colder in July than in January.
- Distinguish a solar eclipse from a lunar eclipse.
- Describe why high tides happen roughly twice a day at most coasts.

## Core content

**1. Earth's spin and orbit.** Earth **rotates** on its axis once every 24 hours, giving day and night. Earth **orbits** the Sun once every 365.25 days. Both are predictable. *Quick check:* Why do we add a day every four years to the calendar?

**2. Why we have seasons.** Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5°. As Earth orbits the Sun, the tilt points the southern hemisphere toward the Sun in December (summer in Melbourne) and away in June (winter). Distance to the Sun changes only slightly across a year and is not the cause. *Quick check:* Why is Melbourne colder in June even though Earth is closest to the Sun in early January?

**3. The Moon's monthly cycle.** The Moon orbits Earth about every 27 days. Its phases come from how much of the lit half we can see from Earth. New moon, first quarter, full, third quarter and back. *Quick check:* Where is the Moon, relative to Earth and Sun, at full moon?

**4. Eclipses.** A **solar eclipse** happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth. A **lunar eclipse** happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon. Eclipses are rare because the Moon's orbit is slightly tilted. *Quick check:* Why are total solar eclipses visible only along a narrow path?

**5. Worked example: tides at Phillip Island.** Tides at Phillip Island rise and fall about twice each day. The Moon's gravity pulls water on the Earth-facing side, creating a high-tide bulge. A second bulge appears on the opposite side, where the Moon's pull on Earth is weakest. As Earth rotates, beaches pass through both bulges, giving two highs and two lows per day. The Sun adds a smaller pull. When Sun and Moon align (new and full moons), tides are larger (spring tides). When they are at right angles (first and third quarters), tides are smaller (neap tides). *Follow the thinking:* draw the geometry → explain bulges → predict changes with phase.

**6. Day length over the year.** In summer in Melbourne, day length is about 14.5 hours; in winter, about 9.5 hours. Higher latitudes show bigger swings. *Quick check:* Why does Hobart have longer summer days than Melbourne?

**7. Predictability and uncertainty.** These cycles are precise enough to predict eclipses centuries in advance. Local weather and ocean conditions add uncertainty to tide and sky observations day by day. *Quick check:* Why might a calculated tide be 30 cm higher on a stormy night?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Planetarium at Scienceworks runs public sessions on the night sky. The Bureau of Meteorology Australia publishes daily tide charts for ports including Williamstown, Geelong and Lakes Entrance.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander astronomy includes detailed knowledge of the Sun, Moon and stars. The Yolŋu people of north-east Arnhem Land have publicly shared a description of lunar tides that aligns with Western explanations. Researchers such as Duane Hamacher and Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung knowledge holders have worked together on Victorian sky knowledge. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Lunar calendars shape festivals across Melbourne, including Lunar New Year for Vietnamese, Chinese and Korean communities, Eid moon-sighting for Muslim communities, and Diwali for Hindu communities. Each celebration reflects accurate Moon observation.

## Memorable takeaways
- Day comes from spin. Year comes from orbit.
- Seasons come from tilt, not distance.
- Eclipses are alignment events.
- Tides rise and fall twice a day.
- Lunar New Year and Eid follow the same Moon.

## Watch out for these traps
- "It is summer because Earth is closer to the Sun." No. Tilt explains seasons. Earth is closest to the Sun in early January.
- "The Moon makes its own light." No. The Moon reflects sunlight.
- "Eclipses happen every month." No. The Moon's orbit is tilted, so alignment is rare.
- "Tides are caused only by the Moon." No. The Sun also contributes; their alignment changes tide size.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Leap day.* Earth orbits in 365.25 days. Adding a day every four years keeps the calendar aligned with the orbit.
2. *Cold June despite proximity.* Tilt determines how directly sunlight strikes Melbourne, not the small distance change. In June, the southern hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun.
3. *Full moon location.* On the side of Earth opposite the Sun, so the Earth-facing side of the Moon is fully lit.
4. *Why total solar eclipse paths are narrow.* The Moon casts a small shadow at Earth's distance. Only places inside that shadow see totality.
5. *Hobart vs Melbourne summer days.* Hobart is at higher latitude, so the tilt brings more sunlight hours per day in summer.
6. *Why a stormy tide can be higher.* Storm surge and waves add to predicted tide height.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *St Kilda beach trip.* Use the Bureau tide chart to pick low tide for shallow exploration or high tide for swimming. Aim for daylight hours and check the Moon phase as a rough guide for tide size.
2. *Wellington coastal infrastructure.* Sea walls, drainage and access need to function across the full tide range. Spring tides plus storm surge define the worst case for design and evacuation planning.
3. *Astronomical scheduling.* Avoid full-moon glare for faint targets, schedule when the target is near zenith for clearest viewing, and check weather forecasts for the night.
4. *Sacred-site photos.* Issues: cultural sensitivity and consent. Plan: contact the relevant Traditional Owner corporation, follow their guidance, and use only public viewing areas if specific protocols restrict access.
5. *Wurundjeri sky knowledge.* Discuss with Elders' permission and credit. Pair Wurundjeri seasonal indicators with Western seasonal definitions where appropriate, and use only published, community-approved material.

Factors: weather and light pollution.
