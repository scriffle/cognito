# VC2S8I08 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I08. Communicate scientific ideas, findings and arguments by choosing formats, vocabulary and representations suited to purpose and audience.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The format and words you choose change what people understand.
  - Audience and purpose come before style.
  - Visuals work hardest when paired with clear text.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Literacy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **communicate** scientific ideas, findings and arguments by **choosing** formats, vocabulary and representations **suited** to purpose and audience.
- You are learning to match a message to its audience.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **format**, **register** and **representation** in your own words.
- Choose a format and vocabulary for a stated audience.
- Identify three features of a strong scientific report.
- Adapt one finding for two different audiences.

## Core content

**1. Audience first.** Before you write, name the reader. Year 6 students, council members and lab partners need different examples, length and tone. *Quick check:* Who is your audience for a school assembly versus a lab notebook?

**2. Choose the format.** A **format** is the type of communication: report, poster, slide deck, infographic, video, social post. Each has strengths and limits. A poster summarises. A report explains. A video shows. *Quick check:* Which format suits a short safety message in a workshop?

**3. Vocabulary and register.** **Register** is how formal or technical your language is. Use technical words when they save explanation. Define them on first use. Match register to audience. *Quick check:* When would "transpiration" be the right word, and when would "water moving through plants" be better?

**4. Representations beyond words.** A **representation** is a way to show information: graph, table, diagram, photograph, model. Pair them with a clear caption. Captions should describe what the reader sees and what to take from it. *Quick check:* What two parts should a caption have?

**5. Worked example: school energy-use poster.** Audience: students and parents at the open day. Purpose: persuade the school to install solar. Format: A2 poster. Representations: bar chart of monthly use, photo of the roof, simple infographic on payback time. Vocabulary: define kilowatt-hour on first use. Register: friendly, factual. *Follow the thinking:* who → why → what to show → which words → which images → captions → review with a peer.

**6. Strong scientific reports.** A clear scientific report has a question, a method, results with representations, an evaluation and a conclusion. References cite real sources. Avoid hype. *Quick check:* Why does adding the method make a result more trustworthy?

**7. Honest visuals.** Charts must use full axes unless cropping is justified and explained. Photographs should not be misleadingly framed. State the source of any image. *Quick check:* Why is a chart with a cropped y-axis a communication problem, not just a maths one?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Scienceworks at Museums Victoria publishes accessible explainers for school audiences. The Royal Society of Victoria and CSIRO Education provide model reports and infographics.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Languages Australia and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish guidance on respectful language and visuals when communicating science involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and Country. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung leaders have shaped public messaging on cultural fire in Victorian media. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** SBS Radio communicates science in over 60 languages. During COVID-19, multilingual public-health teams in Melbourne's west adapted formats and vocabulary for Vietnamese, Mandarin, Arabic and Dinka audiences without losing accuracy.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your class did a sound-level survey. Choose a format and three key visuals to show the results to Year 4 students.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Casey is rolling out a tree-planting program. Draft a 70-word public Facebook post that uses one fact, one image idea and one call to action.
3. **Vocational.** You are a junior media officer at a Victorian environment NGO. Write the first three lines of a media release on river-quality findings, accurate and free of hype.
4. **Ethical.** A class graphs a single year's data with a cropped axis to make change look bigger. Identify the problem and fix it without losing the message.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung rangers and a science class want to share fire-management findings with three audiences: peers, council, and the local primary school. Outline the format and one tweak for each.

## Memorable takeaways
- Audience first, format second.
- Define jargon on first use.
- Pair representations with captions.
- Honest visuals beat dramatic ones.
- One message, many audiences, many tweaks.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "Audience: ___. Purpose: ___. Format: ___. Key data: ___. Caption: ___."
- **Words to keep close.** audience, purpose, format, register, representation, caption, hype.
- **Try this picture.** Sketch a simple table titled "Audience, Format, Tweaks" and fill it in for one piece of work in three different audiences.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Record a 60-second pitch of one finding to a younger sibling, then to a teacher, and listen for what changes.

## Stretch yourself
Take one of your own science findings. Produce three short artefacts on the same finding: a 280-character social post, a one-paragraph media release lead and a one-slide infographic outline. Justify each change in two sentences.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Big words sound smart." No. Plain language sounds clear.
- "More charts means better communication." No. Each chart must earn its space.
- "Captions are decoration." No. Captions guide the reader's eye.
- "Communication is one-way." No. Good science communication invites questions and updates.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Assembly vs lab notebook.* Assembly: peers and teachers, short, big visuals, simple language. Lab notebook: future-you and the teacher marking, detailed, technical, dated and signed.
2. *Format for a workshop safety message.* A short poster or printed signage with icons. Quick to read while moving.
3. *When to use "transpiration" vs "water moving through plants".* Use "transpiration" when the audience already knows the term or you define it once. Use the phrase for a Year 4 audience or a non-specialist reader.
4. *Two parts of a caption.* What the reader sees, and what they should take from it.
5. *Why method makes a result more trustworthy.* It lets the reader judge how the data were produced and decide whether to trust the result.
6. *Why a cropped y-axis is a communication problem.* It misleads the reader by exaggerating differences and hides the true scale of change.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Year 4 sound-level survey.* A short slide-show with three visuals: a friendly bar chart in colour, a photograph of the loudest spot, and an icon-based scale comparing levels to known sounds (whisper, talk, traffic).
2. *Casey tree-planting post.* "We are planting 500 native trees across Casey this winter. Local studies show street trees can cool footpaths by several degrees on hot days. Join us at Cranbourne Park, 9 am Saturday. RSVP at the link."
3. *Environment NGO media release.* "A six-month survey of the Goulburn River shows water quality is improving at three of five sites. The findings are released today by [organisation]. Researchers say targeted action on stormwater appears to be supporting the gains."
4. *Cropped-axis fix.* Reset the axis to start at zero. Add a callout circle that highlights the absolute change with a value and units. Caption explains the size of the change in plain language.
5. *Wurundjeri fire-management findings.* Peers: a short video walking through a site. Council: a one-page brief with one chart and one quote. Primary school: an illustrated picture-book page with one map and one short story drawn from published, community-approved material. Common element: the same headline finding stated simply.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Finding: school surface temperatures vary by up to 18°C between asphalt and grass at midday in summer.* Social post: short headline, one number, one image idea, one call to action. Media release lead: one sentence on what was found, one on why it matters, one on what next. Infographic outline: a thermometer-styled bar comparing surfaces, with a caption that names the dataset and the date. Justification names the audience and the action each piece is asking for.
