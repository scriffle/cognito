# VC2S8U13 — Science Understanding

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8U13. Simple machines such as levers, pulleys, wedges and screws change the direction or size of forces to make work easier.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Physical Sciences: Forces & Energy).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Simple machines trade distance for force, or vice versa.
  - The total work done is conserved.
  - The right tool turns hard tasks into possible ones.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that **simple machines** like levers, pulleys, wedges and screws **change** the direction or size of forces to make **work** easier.
- You are learning to identify the simple machine in everyday tools and explain how it works.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **force**, **work** and **mechanical advantage** in your own words.
- Identify the simple machine in five common tools.
- Explain why a longer lever needs less force.
- State the trade-off in any simple machine.

## Core content

**1. Force and work.** A **force** is a push or pull. **Work** is done when a force moves something through a distance: Work = Force × distance. *Quick check:* If you push a box 2 m with 50 N of force, how much work do you do?

**2. The lever.** A **lever** is a rigid bar that pivots at a fulcrum. Push down on one side; the other side lifts. A longer lever arm reduces the force needed but increases the distance moved. *Quick check:* Why does a longer crowbar make prying easier?

**3. The pulley.** A **pulley** is a wheel and rope. A single fixed pulley changes direction (you pull down to lift up). A movable pulley reduces the force needed but doubles the rope you must pull. *Quick check:* Why does adding pulleys reduce the force you feel?

**4. The wedge.** A **wedge** is two inclined planes back to back. Axes, knives and chisels all use wedges. A long thin wedge needs less force but moves further to split. *Quick check:* Why is a sharper knife easier to use?

**5. The screw.** A **screw** is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. Each turn moves it forward by the thread spacing. Tight threads need less force but more turns. *Quick check:* Why is a fine-thread screw easier to drive but slower?

**6. Worked example: lifting a 60 kg engine block with a pulley.** A 60 kg block weighs about 600 N. A single fixed pulley still needs 600 N but lets you pull down. A 2-pulley block-and-tackle reduces the force to 300 N, but you must pull twice as much rope. Total work is unchanged. *Follow the thinking:* identify the load → choose mechanical advantage → calculate force and distance trade-offs.

**7. Mechanical advantage.** **Mechanical advantage** is how much a machine multiplies your effort. A 2:1 mechanical advantage means half the effort, double the distance. No machine multiplies effort without trading distance, in an ideal case. *Quick check:* Why is "free force" impossible?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Boatmakers along the Mornington Peninsula and forklift drivers at the Port of Melbourne use simple machines daily. The Royal Children's Hospital in Parkville uses pulley and lever-based therapy equipment.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander tool-making includes wedges (stone axes from quarries such as Mount William on Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Country), levers (digging sticks) and ropes (woven fibre lines used for weights). The Indigenous Knowledge Institute publishes work on this engineering tradition. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Vietnamese-Australian boat repairers in Footscray, Greek-Australian car mechanics in Oakleigh and Italian-Australian carpenters across Melbourne all rely on simple machines daily. Family workshops are rich with examples.

## Memorable takeaways
- Machines change force size or direction.
- No free lunch: less force, more distance.
- Lever, pulley, wedge, screw, ramp.
- Mechanical advantage = old force / new force.
- The right tool turns hard into possible.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Machines create energy." No. They redistribute force and distance, but total work is conserved (and in real life, friction wastes some).
- "Bigger lever is always better." No. Long levers are unwieldy and can break.
- "Pulleys give free lifting." No. You pull more rope to lift less effort.
- "Wedges only mean axes." No. Knives, doorstops, plough blades and even teeth are wedges.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Work calculation.* 50 N × 2 m = 100 J of work.
2. *Why a longer crowbar makes prying easier.* Greater lever arm length reduces the input force needed for the same output force at the load end.
3. *Why pulleys reduce the force you feel.* The rope shares the load across multiple sections, so each section supports a fraction of the total weight.
4. *Why a sharper knife is easier.* A longer, thinner wedge has a steeper mechanical advantage. The cutting edge converts a small input force into a large splitting force per unit of contact.
5. *Why fine-thread screws are easier but slower.* Smaller pitch means less force per turn, but more turns to advance the same distance.
6. *Why "free force" is impossible.* Energy is conserved. Less input force always means more input distance.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Tight bottle cap.* A wide-handle gripper acts as a lever. A wedge-style bottle opener uses an inclined plane and a lever to pry the cap off.
2. *Hume playground.* Seesaw is a lever. Slide is an inclined plane. Pulleys on flag poles and shade sails. Each example shows direction or size of force changing.
3. *Hydraulic jack.* Force applied to a small piston creates pressure in fluid. The same pressure on a larger piston produces a much larger force. A hand pump can lift a car because the area ratio gives mechanical advantage.
4. *Brick lifting demo.* Issues: foot injury and strain. Safer plan: use foam bricks or scale-model demos with light blocks; show the same principle without risking injury.
5. *Wurundjeri digging sticks.* Discuss with Elder permission and credit. Explain lever physics: shaft acts as a lever, hand position is the fulcrum, applied force at one end lifts soil at the other. Use only published, community-approved material.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Workplace: bike shop.* Simple machines: lever (chain whip), pulley (cable derailleur system), wedge (wheel-truing spokes turning), screw (handlebar adjustments), inclined plane (workshop ramps). Mechanical-advantage calculation example: a 200 mm chain whip on a 50 mm sprocket gives roughly 4:1 leverage at the cog edge. Trade-off: the user moves the chain whip handle four times as far as the cog rotates.
