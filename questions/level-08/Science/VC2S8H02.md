# VC2S8H02 — Science as a Human Endeavour

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8H02. Advancing scientific knowledge involves people from different backgrounds, perspectives and worldviews working together.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Science as a Human Endeavour).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Modern science is a team effort across borders and disciplines.
  - Different perspectives spot different problems and solutions.
  - Many knowledge systems contribute to what we know about the natural world.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures; Asia and Australia's Engagement with Asia; Intercultural Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that advancing scientific knowledge **involves** people from different backgrounds, perspectives and worldviews **working together**.
- You are learning to describe how diverse teams improve what science can do.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **collaboration**, **discipline** and **perspective** in your own words.
- Give one example of an Australian science project where diverse teams advanced knowledge.
- Explain why different worldviews can lead to different research questions.
- Describe one risk of a scientific team that lacks diversity.

## Core content

**1. Science is a team sport.** A modern study often has authors from many countries. They share data, code and samples. **Collaboration** means people contributing different skills toward a shared question. *Quick check:* Why is one researcher rarely enough for a complex problem?

**2. Disciplines bring different lenses.** A **discipline** is a field of expertise such as chemistry, ecology or statistics. A bushfire study may need fire ecologists, climatologists, engineers and Indigenous land managers. Each lens catches things others miss. *Quick check:* Name two disciplines you would put on a team studying microplastics in Port Phillip Bay.

**3. Perspectives shape the questions.** A **perspective** is the angle a person brings, shaped by training, culture and lived experience. A medical researcher who has lived with a disability may notice barriers a colleague misses. *Quick check:* Why does the question you ask matter as much as the method you use?

**4. Worldviews can be complementary.** A **worldview** is a broad set of beliefs about how the world fits together. Western experimental science and Aboriginal knowledge of Country can answer different but related questions about the same landscape. Combining them, with consent, can produce richer results. *Quick check:* Are these worldviews competing or complementary in this example?

**5. Worked example: hot fire and cool fire.** In Western Arnhem Land and parts of Victoria, Aboriginal land managers and university scientists have studied **cool burning**, low-intensity burns set early in the dry season. Rangers brought generations of observation. Scientists measured smoke, carbon and biodiversity. Together they showed cool burns reduced severe summer wildfires and emissions. The result has shifted government policy. *Follow the thinking:* Different knowledge → shared question → joint method → measured outcome → policy change.

**6. Diverse teams reduce blind spots.** When a team is uniform, it may agree too quickly. Mixed teams ask harder questions and check each other. Studies on medical devices, for instance, have found errors when designs were tested only on one demographic. *Quick check:* What is one risk of a uniform research team?

**7. Communicating across difference is a skill.** Working across languages, cultures and disciplines takes patience. Good teams agree on shared terms early, name power differences, and credit contributions clearly. *Quick check:* Why is naming who contributed what part of doing science well?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The CSIRO and the University of Melbourne run joint projects on platypus health in the Yarra catchment. Teams include geneticists, vets, river ecologists and citizen-science volunteers from local councils.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** On Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and Wadawurrung Country, Traditional Owner corporations partner with Parks Victoria and university teams on fire, water and biodiversity. Authors such as Bruce Pascoe and researchers at the Indigenous Knowledge Institute have published peer-reviewed work on these collaborations. Specific cultural knowledge in any project should only be read through published, community-approved material and used with consent.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** In Melbourne's south-east, researchers from Vietnamese, Sri Lankan and Anglo-Australian backgrounds have worked together on dengue and malaria modelling, drawing on language skills and overseas networks to design fieldwork. In Western Australia, scientists with Chinese and Aboriginal heritage have collaborated on iron-ore site rehabilitation. Diverse backgrounds widened both the questions and the access.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A school in Bendigo is starting a frog-monitoring project. List three different kinds of expertise the team should include and one community partner who could add a perspective the school does not have.
2. **Civic.** A council in Footscray is planning a heat-health response. Suggest five voices that should be on the working group, including at least two outside science. Explain why each one matters.
3. **Vocational.** You are an early-career water engineer joining a team in Mildura. Describe two ways you would learn from senior Aboriginal rangers without speaking over them.
4. **Ethical.** A pharmaceutical trial in Melbourne enrols only English-speaking participants. Identify two ethical risks and one practical fix that would not slow the trial much.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A research group from Tokyo wants to partner with a Yorta Yorta organisation on river health on the Murray. Outline three steps to set up the partnership respectfully and one shared output that benefits both sides.

## Memorable takeaways
- Many minds beat one for big problems.
- Different lenses see different details.
- Worldviews can complement, not just compete.
- Diversity catches blind spots.
- Credit shared work clearly.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Real science is done by lone geniuses." No. Almost all current science is collaborative.
- "Aboriginal knowledge and Western science cannot work together." No. They have, with consent and respect, on fire, water and biodiversity for decades.
- "Diversity slows research down." No. It often improves the questions, the data and the uptake.
- "Citizen scientists do not count." No. Volunteers contribute large datasets that professionals could not collect alone.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why is one researcher rarely enough?* Big problems span more skills and data than one person can master. Teams cover more ground and check each other's work.
2. *Two disciplines for microplastics in Port Phillip Bay.* Marine ecology and analytical chemistry. You could also defend oceanography or public health.
3. *Why does the question matter as much as the method?* The question decides what counts as data and what gets ignored. A different perspective often reveals a better question.
4. *Are the worldviews competing or complementary in cool burning?* Complementary. They answer different parts of the same question and produce a richer result together.
5. *One risk of a uniform team.* Shared blind spots. The team may not see problems that affect groups not represented in the room.
6. *Why credit who did what?* It is honest, it lets others build on the work, and it respects the contributions of communities and junior team members.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Bendigo frogs.* A frog ecologist, a data analyst and a local teacher cover field, numbers and community. A community partner could be the local Landcare group or the Dja Dja Wurrung Clans Aboriginal Corporation, who add long-term knowledge of the waterways.
2. *Footscray heat-health.* Public health researcher (risk data), urban planner (shade and cool roofs), GP (frontline care), local Vietnamese community leader (reach and trust), aged-care worker (vulnerable residents). Each voice catches a risk the others miss.
3. *Mildura water engineer.* Spend the first weeks listening on Country with rangers before proposing designs. Ask Traditional Owner corporations to review your modelling assumptions, and credit their input in any report.
4. *English-only trial.* Risks: results may not generalise to Australia's population, and non-English speakers face unequal access. Fix: translate consent forms and use bilingual research nurses for two key clinic days a week.
5. *Tokyo and Yorta Yorta on river health.* Step 1: contact the Yorta Yorta Nation Aboriginal Corporation and seek a partnership agreement before any data collection. Step 2: agree shared terms, data ownership and benefit sharing in writing. Step 3: co-design field methods with rangers. Shared output: a public dashboard that supports both Yorta Yorta management and Japanese river research.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Project: 2024 Great Southern Bioblitz Victoria.* Question: how does urban biodiversity vary across Greater Melbourne? Disciplines: ecology, data science, science communication. Community partner: local Friends-of groups across multiple suburbs. Outcome: a public dataset and a council-ready summary that informed planting choices in two LGAs. Note one limit: short sampling window biases toward visible species.
