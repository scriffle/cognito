# VC2S8H04 — Science as a Human Endeavour

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8H04. Communicating scientific knowledge to the public plays an important role in shaping people's views and government policies.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Science as a Human Endeavour).
- **Big ideas:**
  - How science is told changes what people do about it.
  - Good communication is accurate, clear and honest about uncertainty.
  - Audiences differ, so messages and channels must differ too.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Literacy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that **communicating** scientific knowledge to the public plays an important role in **shaping** people's views and government policies.
- You are learning to judge and produce science communication that is accurate, clear and audience-aware.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **audience**, **framing** and **misinformation** in your own words.
- Identify three features of strong science communication.
- Explain how science communication has shaped one Australian policy decision.
- Adapt one science message for two different audiences.

## Core content

**1. The same fact, told two ways, lands differently.** Science is not just discovered, it is communicated. The choice of words, images and headlines shapes what people remember and decide. *Quick check:* Why does the same study sound different in a tabloid headline and a CSIRO briefing?

**2. Audience first.** An **audience** is the people you are trying to reach. Year 6 students, hospital nurses and a parliamentary committee need different examples, vocabulary and length. *Quick check:* What changes if your audience shifts from peers to Year 3 students?

**3. Framing shapes understanding.** **Framing** means the angle and emphasis in a message. "Vaccine prevents 9 in 10 cases" feels different from "1 in 10 vaccinated still get sick", though both are true. Honest framing chooses the angle that matches the evidence as a whole. *Quick check:* Give one phrase that frames the same fact more positively and more negatively.

**4. Worked example: COVID-19 daily updates.** Through 2020 and 2021, Victorian press conferences shared data on cases, hospitalisations and reproduction numbers. Charts, maps and plain-English explanations supported decisions on lockdowns and vaccines. Some communication worked well, like clear case maps. Some failed, like changing definitions without flagging the change. *Follow the thinking:* identify what was being communicated → who was the audience → what action was being asked for → what built or lost trust over time.

**5. Communication can shape policy.** Public concern, fed by communicated evidence, has shifted Australian policy on smoking, lead in petrol, asbestos and seatbelt laws. Scientists, journalists and advocates worked together. Without communication, evidence sits in journals. *Quick check:* Why is "the evidence speaks for itself" rarely true?

**6. Misinformation and disinformation.** **Misinformation** is wrong information shared without intent to deceive. **Disinformation** is wrong information shared on purpose. Both can spread faster than corrections, especially online. Good communicators pre-bunk common false claims rather than only debunking after. *Quick check:* What is the difference between mis- and disinformation?

**7. Tools and habits of strong communication.** Use plain language, real numbers with context, named uncertainty, and visuals tied to data. Cite sources. Update when evidence updates. Match the medium to the message: a short video for social, a detailed brief for policymakers. *Quick check:* Which two of these habits would most improve a typical health TikTok?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Museums Victoria's Scienceworks runs public exhibitions and the "Particle" podcast. Their work translates research from the University of Melbourne, Monash and CSIRO for school audiences. The Royal Society of Victoria hosts free public lectures.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Languages Australia and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish guidance on respectful science communication that involves Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and Country. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung leaders have shaped public messaging on cultural fire and cool burning in Victorian media. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** SBS Radio broadcasts public-health information in over 60 languages. During COVID-19, Vietnamese, Mandarin, Arabic and Dinka community leaders translated and reframed messages in Melbourne's west. Without that work, key advice would not have reached many households.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A friend shares a viral post claiming sunscreen causes cancer. Reply in three sentences using one fact, one source pointer and one calm question.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Frankston is considering more shade trees to reduce heat. Draft a 70-word public Facebook post that explains the health evidence and invites comment.
3. **Vocational.** You are a junior media officer at a Victorian hospital. A new asthma drug is approved. Write the first three lines of a press release that is accurate, clear and free of hype.
4. **Ethical.** A scientist is asked to soften the certainty in a climate report so it sells better as a magazine article. Argue what they should do, naming what they owe the public and the data.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A health campaign on flu shots needs to reach Vietnamese, Somali and Wadawurrung communities in Geelong. Suggest one channel and one message tweak for each, and one common element that should not change.

## Memorable takeaways
- Audience first, message second.
- Plain language, real numbers, named uncertainty.
- Frame honestly. Pick the angle the evidence supports.
- Pre-bunk before you debunk.
- Update your message when the evidence updates.

## Watch out for these traps
- "If the science is right, communication doesn't matter." No. Poor communication can stall good science. Good communication can speed up good policy.
- "Plain language means dumbed down." No. Plain language means clear and accurate.
- "Showing uncertainty undermines trust." No. Naming what you do and do not know builds trust over time.
- "Correcting misinformation always works." No. Repeated, calm pre-bunking and trusted messengers work better than late corrections.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Tabloid vs CSIRO briefing.* Different audiences and goals. Tabloids aim for clicks and emotion. CSIRO aims for accuracy and decision support, so wording, length and visuals differ.
2. *Peers to Year 3.* Shorter sentences, simpler words, more concrete examples and pictures, slower pace, more chances to repeat the main idea.
3. *Positive and negative framing.* Positive: "9 in 10 vaccinated avoid serious illness." Negative: "1 in 10 vaccinated may still get serious illness." Both are true, the angle differs.
4. *Why "evidence speaks for itself" is rarely true.* People hear evidence through the words, images and trust they bring. Without translation and reach, evidence does not move debate.
5. *Misinformation vs disinformation.* Misinformation is wrong without intent to deceive. Disinformation is wrong on purpose to mislead.
6. *Two habits to improve a typical health TikTok.* Cite a real source on screen and name the uncertainty in a short caption.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Sunscreen post.* Sunscreen reduces UV damage and skin-cancer risk, according to the Cancer Council of Australia. The viral post cherry-picks one early study. What source is the post drawing on, and is it peer-reviewed?
2. *Frankston shade trees.* Heat-related illness rises sharply in Melbourne summers. Studies in Australian cities show street trees can cool footpaths by several degrees. Council is considering 200 new trees on hot streets. Have your say at the link below.
3. *Hospital asthma press release.* "A new asthma medication has been approved by the TGA after Australian-led trials. The drug reduces severe attacks in children aged 6 to 12. Specialists say it is one option among several, and access will roll out through Victorian respiratory clinics from October."
4. *Soften certainty for a magazine.* They should refuse to soften beyond what the evidence supports. They owe the public an accurate picture and they owe the data its real range. They can make the article more readable without overstating or understating.
5. *Flu campaign in Geelong.* Vietnamese: trusted GP video on Vietnamese-language Facebook. Somali: short audio for community radio with a respected Elder. Wadawurrung: a co-designed flyer through Wathaurong Aboriginal Co-operative. Common element: the same simple call to action and clinic locations.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Story: AFL injury research at La Trobe University.* Year 5 headline: "What footy players' brains are teaching scientists." Year 5 lede: simple sentence on what concussion is, one number, one safety rule. MP headline: "Concussion research and policy implications for Victorian sport." MP lede: one sentence on study design, one on policy options, one on cost. Two-sentence justification names audience needs and the action each piece is asking for.
