# VC2S8H04 — Science as a Human Endeavour (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Communicating scientific knowledge to the public plays an important role in shaping people's views and government policies.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Science.
- **Big ideas:** How science is told changes what people do. Audiences differ. Honest framing is part of the science.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that **communicating** science **shapes** people's views and policies.
- You should be able to judge science communication and adapt a message for two audiences.

## Core content

An **audience** is the people you are trying to reach. **Framing** is the angle and emphasis you choose. **Misinformation** is wrong information shared without intent to deceive; **disinformation** is wrong information shared on purpose. Strong communication uses plain language, real numbers with context, named uncertainty and visuals tied to data.

Public concern, fed by communicated evidence, has shifted Australian policy on smoking, lead in petrol, asbestos and seatbelt laws. Without communication, evidence sits in journals.

**Worked example.** Through 2020 and 2021, Victorian press conferences shared data on COVID-19 cases, hospitalisations and reproduction numbers. Charts and plain-English explanations supported decisions on lockdowns and vaccines. Some communication built trust, like clear case maps. Some lost trust, like changing definitions without flagging the change. The same evidence shaped action because it was communicated.

**Cultural context.** SBS Radio broadcasts public-health information in over 60 languages. During COVID-19, Vietnamese, Mandarin, Arabic and Dinka community leaders translated and reframed messages in Melbourne's west. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung leaders have shaped public messaging on cultural fire. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Audience first, message second.
- Plain language, real numbers, named uncertainty.
- Frame honestly to match the evidence.
- Update the message when the evidence updates.

## Watch out for these traps
- "If the science is right, communication doesn't matter." No. Poor communication can stall good science.
- "Plain language means dumbed down." No. Plain language means clear and accurate.
- "Showing uncertainty undermines trust." No. Naming what you do and do not know builds trust over time.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *What is framing?* The angle and emphasis chosen for a message. Two true statements about the same fact can lead audiences to different conclusions.
- *Misinformation vs disinformation.* Misinformation is wrong information shared without intent to deceive. Disinformation is wrong information shared on purpose.
- *Why is "the evidence speaks for itself" rarely true?* People hear evidence through the words, images and trust they bring. Without translation and reach, evidence rarely moves debate.
- *Two habits to improve a health social-media post.* Cite a real source and name the uncertainty.
- *Short summary of COVID-19 communication.* Daily Victorian briefings turned data into decisions. Clear visuals built trust. Unflagged definition changes cost trust. The same evidence had to be communicated to act on it.
