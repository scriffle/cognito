# VC2S8U09 — Science Understanding

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8U09. Earth's resources can be renewable or non-renewable, and how we extract and use them involves both benefits and risks to sustainability.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Earth & Space Sciences).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Renewable resources can be replenished within a human timeframe.
  - Non-renewable resources cannot.
  - Every extraction has benefits and trade-offs.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Sustainability; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures; Ethical Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that Earth's resources can be **renewable** or **non-renewable**, and that how we **extract** and **use** them involves both **benefits** and **risks** to sustainability.
- You are learning to weigh those benefits and risks for real Australian resources.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **renewable**, **non-renewable** and **sustainability** in your own words.
- Sort five Australian resources into renewable and non-renewable.
- Identify two benefits and two risks of one extraction project.
- Suggest how to manage a resource more sustainably.

## Core content

**1. Renewable resources.** A **renewable** resource is replenished naturally on a timescale similar to its use. Sunlight, wind, fresh water (in many places), forests and fish (if managed well) are renewable. *Quick check:* Why is fresh water sometimes treated as both renewable and limited?

**2. Non-renewable resources.** A **non-renewable** resource forms over geological timescales, far longer than human use. Coal, oil, gas and most metals are non-renewable. *Quick check:* Why is coal called fossilised sunlight?

**3. Sustainability.** **Sustainability** is meeting present needs without preventing future generations from meeting theirs. It applies to renewables (overuse can deplete them) and non-renewables (use today removes options tomorrow). *Quick check:* Can a resource be renewable but unsustainable?

**4. Worked example: brown coal in the Latrobe Valley.** Victorian brown coal has powered the state for decades. Benefits: cheap electricity, jobs, baseload power. Risks: high carbon emissions, air-quality impacts, mine fires (Hazelwood 2014), site rehabilitation costs. As Victoria transitions, the question is how to balance reliability, cost, environment and worker support. *Follow the thinking:* identify resource → benefits → risks → trade-offs → who pays the cost.

**5. The energy transition.** Victoria is investing in solar, wind, batteries and pumped hydro. Each renewable has its own benefits and trade-offs: solar needs land or roof space, wind needs sites and supply chains, batteries need lithium. Trade-offs do not disappear, they change shape. *Quick check:* Why does "renewable" not mean "no environmental impact"?

**6. Water as a critical resource.** Victoria's drinking water comes from the Thomson, Upper Yarra, Cardinia and other catchments. Drought and population growth raise pressure. The desalination plant near Wonthaggi adds resilience but uses energy. *Quick check:* Why is water-saving still important even with a desalination plant?

**7. Recycling and circular use.** Aluminium, steel, glass and many plastics can be recycled, reducing both extraction and waste. Recycling needs energy and clean streams. Done well, it stretches non-renewable resources further. *Quick check:* Why is recycling not a free pass to keep extracting?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Latrobe Valley brown-coal mines, the Bass Strait gas fields, the Wonthaggi desalination plant and Bald Hills wind farm are all Victorian examples of resource use, each with documented benefits and risks.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have managed Country sustainably for tens of thousands of years through fire, water and harvest practices. The Gunaikurnai, Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Wadawurrung and other Victorian peoples have published joint-management agreements for parks, water and forests. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant communities across Melbourne support recycling and circular practices in their own languages. Vietnamese, Greek and Sudanese community organisations partner with councils on waste education. Indian-Australian researchers at RMIT lead work on lithium-ion battery recycling.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You buy a phone. Identify three Earth resources used in its making and classify each.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Bass Coast considers a new offshore wind farm. Outline three benefits and three risks, and one condition you would attach.
3. **Vocational.** A junior environmental officer visits a goldmine in Bendigo. List three sustainability questions to ask the operator.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates fracking for gas. Identify two ethical issues and explain how trade-offs should be weighed.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Gunaikurnai rangers and your class plan a forest harvest study. Outline how their knowledge of long-term sustainability informs the design.

## Memorable takeaways
- Renewable: replenished within human time.
- Non-renewable: built over geological time.
- Every option has trade-offs.
- "Sustainable" is a verb, not a label.
- Recycling extends, it does not replace.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Renewable means clean." No. Solar panels and wind turbines need mining and recycling.
- "Non-renewable will run out next year." No. Some non-renewables last decades or centuries, but the question is what we leave for the future.
- "We can recycle everything." No. Some products mix materials in ways that make recycling impractical.
- "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander land management is just history." No. It is current practice that is shaping modern policy.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why is fresh water both renewable and limited?* Rainfall replenishes it, but not always where or when it is needed. Overuse can outpace replenishment in a region.
2. *Coal as fossilised sunlight.* Coal formed from ancient plants that captured solar energy through photosynthesis. Burning it releases that stored energy.
3. *Renewable but unsustainable?* Yes. A forest is renewable, but cutting trees faster than they regrow is unsustainable.
4. *Why "renewable" is not "no impact".* Producing solar panels, wind turbines and batteries needs mined materials, energy and land. Impacts shrink but do not vanish.
5. *Why save water even with a desalination plant?* Desalination uses energy and infrastructure. Saving water reduces emissions and keeps the plant for true emergencies.
6. *Why recycling is not a free pass.* Recycling is not 100% efficient. It still requires energy, transport and clean streams, and not all materials are recyclable.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Phone.* Lithium for the battery (non-renewable). Aluminium for the case (non-renewable, recyclable). Energy from the grid for charging (mix of renewable and non-renewable). Each can be made more sustainable by recycling and choosing low-carbon power.
2. *Bass Coast wind farm.* Benefits: lower emissions, regional jobs, energy resilience. Risks: visual amenity, wildlife impact, supply-chain emissions. Condition: community ownership share and biodiversity offsets.
3. *Bendigo goldmine.* What is your water use and discharge plan? How will the site be rehabilitated? What is your tailings storage and worker safety record?
4. *Fracking debate.* Issues: groundwater contamination risks, climate emissions and Traditional Owner consent. Trade-offs should be weighed against alternatives that meet the same energy or jobs need with lower harm.
5. *Gunaikurnai forest harvest study.* Their knowledge of cool burning, seasonal harvest cycles and species recovery times informs cycle length, area limits and species selection. Co-design the study under a partnership agreement and credit ranger contributions.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Resource: lithium.* Mined in Western Australia, processed in Asia, used worldwide in EV batteries. Communities affected: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Traditional Owners on mining sites, mining workers in Pilbara towns, and battery factory workers overseas. Sustainability improvement: a domestic battery-recycling plant to recover lithium from end-of-life batteries, reducing both extraction and waste.
