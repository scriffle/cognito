## Core content

**1. Atomic theory in one paragraph.** All matter is made of **atoms**. Atoms of the same type form an **element**. Atoms of different elements join to form **compounds**. The properties of a compound are different from the properties of the elements that made it. *Quick check:* Why is sodium chloride safe to eat but sodium and chlorine alone are dangerous?

**2. Element vs compound vs mixture.** An **element** has only one type of atom (e.g. iron, Fe). A **compound** has two or more elements chemically bonded (e.g. water, H₂O). A **mixture** has two or more substances physically combined that keep their own properties (e.g. air). *Quick check:* Where does brass sit in this list?

**3. Reading the periodic table.** The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number. Each square shows a symbol (1 or 2 letters) and the atomic number. Symbols sometimes come from Latin: gold is Au from *aurum*. *Quick check:* What is the symbol for iron, sodium and potassium?

**4. Chemical formulas explained.** A **chemical formula** uses symbols and numbers to show the elements and their ratios in a compound. H₂O means two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. CO₂ means one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms. *Quick check:* What does CaCO₃ tell you about calcium carbonate?

**5. Worked example: building water from hydrogen and oxygen.** Hydrogen (H) is a flammable gas. Oxygen (O) supports combustion. When two hydrogen atoms bond with one oxygen atom, the result is liquid water at room temperature. New properties emerge: it is no longer flammable and not a combustion supporter. *Follow the thinking:* identify the elements → bond them → state the compound formula → list properties of components and product.

**6. Counting atoms.** In 2H₂O, the 2 in front means two whole molecules of water. The subscript 2 in H₂ tells us there are two hydrogen atoms in each molecule. So 2H₂O has four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms in total. *Quick check:* How many oxygen atoms are in 3CO₂?

**7. Why this shorthand matters.** Formulas and symbols are an international language. Scientists in Tokyo, Berlin and Bendigo all read H₂SO₄ the same way. They speed up communication and prevent ambiguity. *Quick check:* Why is "table salt" a less precise name than NaCl in a science context?

## Memorable takeaways
- Atoms make elements. Elements make compounds. Compounds and mixtures are not the same.
- Properties change when atoms bond.
- Symbols are an international shorthand.
- Subscript counts atoms in a molecule. Coefficient counts molecules.
- Precision in names prevents accidents.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Formulas are like word abbreviations." No. They give exact ratios of atoms, not just shorter names.
- "Compounds are mixtures of elements." No. Compounds are chemically bonded. Mixtures are physically combined.
- "Capital and lowercase do not matter in symbols." No. Co is cobalt; CO is carbon monoxide. Spelling matters.
- "Air is one substance." No. Air is a mixture of gases including N₂, O₂, Ar and CO₂.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why is sodium chloride safe but sodium and chlorine dangerous?* Bonding changes properties. The bonded compound is stable and edible; the elements alone are highly reactive.
2. *Where does brass sit?* Mixture. It is an alloy of copper and zinc whose proportions can vary.
3. *Symbols for iron, sodium and potassium.* Fe, Na, K.
4. *What CaCO₃ tells you.* Calcium carbonate has one calcium atom, one carbon atom and three oxygen atoms in each formula unit.
5. *Oxygen atoms in 3CO₂.* Six. Two oxygen atoms per CO₂ molecule, multiplied by three molecules.
6. *Why "table salt" is less precise than NaCl.* "Table salt" can include additives like iodide and anti-caking agents. NaCl points to one specific compound.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Label classification.* NaCl is a compound (sodium and chlorine bonded). Sucrose is a compound (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen). Citric acid is a compound. The whole product (a mix of these substances) is a mixture.
2. *Mornington Peninsula water testing.* Formulas record exactly what is being measured (e.g. NO₃⁻, PO₄³⁻). Without precise notation, two tests could mean different things, and trends over time would be unreliable.
3. *Pharmacy technician.* The formula confirms identity and concentration; it ties to the right safety data; and it supports correct labelling and dosing.
4. *Mixing household chemicals.* Issues: serious chemical hazards (e.g. bleach + ammonia produces toxic gas); legal and ethical responsibility for safety. Safer alternative: use prepared lab kits with known reagents and full risk assessment.
5. *Red ochre.* Fe₂O₃ is iron(III) oxide, two iron atoms to three oxygen atoms. Discuss how the formula relates to colour and properties only with the Elder's permission and credit, using published, community-approved material.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Five products: salt (NaCl), baking soda (NaHCO₃), vinegar (acetic acid CH₃COOH in water), aluminium foil (Al), brass keychain (alloy of Cu and Zn).* Each classified, properties named that depend on the chemistry (cleaning power, conductivity, reactivity), with one formula or symbol per item.
