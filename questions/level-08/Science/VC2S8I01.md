# VC2S8I01 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I01. Develop investigable questions, predictions and hypotheses that guide investigations and help identify patterns and test relationships.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A good question makes the answer testable.
  - A prediction states what you expect to see.
  - A hypothesis links two variables in a testable way.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy; Numeracy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop** investigable questions, predictions and hypotheses that **guide** investigations and **help identify** patterns and **test** relationships.
- You are learning to write each of these so a peer could carry the work out.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **investigable question**, **prediction** and **hypothesis** in your own words.
- Turn a vague wondering into an investigable question.
- Write an "If… then… because…" hypothesis with named variables.
- Distinguish a question that looks for a pattern from one that tests a relationship.

## Core content

**1. Wonderings versus investigable questions.** A **wondering** is an interesting curiosity. An **investigable question** is one your equipment, time and ethics can answer. "Why is the sky blue?" is a wondering. "How does the height of a ramp affect the distance a toy car travels?" is investigable. *Quick check:* Turn "Why are some leaves bigger?" into an investigable question.

**2. Variables, the building blocks.** A **variable** is anything that can change. The **independent variable** is the one you change on purpose. The **dependent variable** is the one you measure. **Controlled variables** are kept constant so they do not muddy the result. *Quick check:* Identify the independent and dependent variables in the ramp question above.

**3. Predictions are specific guesses.** A **prediction** states what you expect to see, ideally with a direction. "The car will travel further from a higher ramp" is more useful than "Something will happen". *Quick check:* Write a prediction for the leaf question.

**4. Hypotheses propose a relationship.** A **hypothesis** is a testable statement linking two variables and proposing why. "If ramp height increases, then the car travels further, because more gravitational potential energy is converted to kinetic energy." *Quick check:* What three parts must a hypothesis contain?

**5. Worked example: heat-island study at school.** Wondering: "Is the school car park hotter than the lawn?" Investigable question: "How does ground surface (asphalt, grass, mulch) affect surface temperature at midday?" Variables: independent = surface, dependent = temperature, controlled = time of day, weather, instrument. Prediction: "Asphalt will be the hottest, then mulch, then grass." Hypothesis: "If the surface is asphalt, then the midday temperature will be highest, because dark surfaces absorb more solar radiation than reflective ones." *Follow the thinking:* widen → narrow → name variables → predict → state mechanism.

**6. Pattern-seeking versus testing relationships.** Some studies look for a **pattern** (does temperature change across the school grounds?). Others **test a relationship** (does shade cause temperature drops?). Both are valid. The wording of the question signals which you are doing. *Quick check:* Is "Where do magpies nest in the schoolyard?" a pattern or a relationship study?

**7. Common pitfalls.** Questions that are too broad, predictions with no direction, and hypotheses with no mechanism are common. Asking "could a peer reproduce my study?" exposes weak wording fast. *Quick check:* What is one weakness in "Plants grow better with music"?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Citizen-science programs run by Museums Victoria, such as Bug Blitz and FrogID, ask students to write investigable questions about local biodiversity. Data are pooled across the state.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Wadawurrung and Bunurong/Boon Wurrung knowledge of seasons rests on long observation that asks investigable questions about Country, such as "When do silver wattles flower this year, and what does that signal?" Researchers including Bruce Pascoe and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute have published work on observation-based inquiry. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** In Melbourne's south-east, Vietnamese, Cambodian and Anglo-Australian community gardeners ask investigable questions about which heirloom vegetables grow best in local soil. Their record-keeping is itself a form of inquiry. Indian-Australian medical students at Monash run small studies on diabetes in South Asian communities, asking carefully worded questions to avoid stereotyping.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your sibling claims their phone battery drains faster on cold mornings. Write an investigable question, name three variables, and write a one-line prediction.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Hume wants to know if shade sails reduce surface temperature in playgrounds. Write the investigable question, identify variables, and write a hypothesis with a mechanism.
3. **Vocational.** A horticulturalist in Werribee wants to know if a new mulch retains soil moisture better than bark chips. Write an investigable question, a prediction with direction, and one risk to the study's reproducibility.
4. **Ethical.** A student wants to test whether classmates focus better with caffeine. Write a question that avoids the ethical problem, and explain how you reframed it.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A team of Yorta Yorta and CSIRO scientists studies how flow changes in the Goulburn River affect native fish. Write one investigable question that respects Traditional Owner priorities and one hypothesis with a mechanism.

## Memorable takeaways
- A wondering is curiosity. A question is testable curiosity.
- Independent changes. Dependent measures. Controlled stays still.
- Predictions point a direction. Hypotheses propose a why.
- "If… then… because…" beats "I think it will work".
- A peer reproduction test exposes weak wording.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "Investigable question: How does ___ affect ___? Prediction: As ___ increases, ___ will ___. Hypothesis: If ___, then ___, because ___."
- **Words to keep close.** investigable, variable, prediction, hypothesis, mechanism.
- **Try this picture.** Draw a simple "input → process → output" arrow. Label the input as your independent variable, the output as your dependent variable, and write controlled variables underneath.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Record yourself walking through the heat-island worked example aloud, naming each step.

## Stretch yourself
Take a current local issue (heat, water, traffic, biodiversity). Write three investigable questions at three scales: a single class period, a school term and a year-long citizen-science project. For each, name the variables and one possible mechanism. 200 words or one labelled diagram.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Big questions are better." No. Useful science questions are narrow enough to test in your time and conditions.
- "A prediction is the same as a hypothesis." No. A prediction states what you expect. A hypothesis adds a relationship and a reason.
- "Hypotheses must always be proven right to count." No. A well-written hypothesis is useful even when the data do not support it.
- "Aboriginal knowledge is just stories, not inquiry." No. It rests on careful, long-term observation tested across generations.

## Try drawing this
A "question funnel": wide wondering at the top, narrowing to a focused investigable question at the bottom, with side notes naming the independent, dependent and controlled variables.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Investigable version of "Why are some leaves bigger?"* "How does light availability affect the average leaf area of *Eucalyptus viminalis* saplings in the schoolyard?"
2. *Variables in the ramp question.* Independent: ramp height. Dependent: distance the car travels. Controlled: car, surface, release method.
3. *Prediction for the leaf question.* "Saplings in full sun will have larger average leaf area than saplings in shade."
4. *Three parts of a hypothesis.* Independent variable, predicted change in the dependent variable, and a brief mechanism explaining why.
5. *Magpies nest study.* Pattern. It maps where, not what causes nest choice.
6. *Weakness of "Plants grow better with music".* "Better" and "music" are vague. No mechanism. Needs a defined sound type, dose and a measured plant trait, plus a plausible reason why sound would affect growth.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Phone battery in cold.* "How does ambient temperature affect phone battery drain rate during identical use?" Variables: temperature (independent), drain in % per hour (dependent), phone model and use pattern (controlled). Prediction: "Battery drain rate will increase as temperature drops below 10°C."
2. *Hume shade sails.* "How does the presence of a shade sail affect midday surface temperature on synthetic grass playgrounds?" Variables: shade sail present or not (independent), temperature in °C (dependent), time of day, weather, instrument (controlled). Hypothesis: "If a shade sail is present, then midday surface temperature will be lower, because the sail blocks direct solar radiation."
3. *Werribee mulch.* "How does mulch type affect soil moisture content over 14 days under identical irrigation?" Prediction: "The new mulch will retain more soil moisture than bark chips, with a measurable difference by day 7." Reproducibility risk: differences in initial soil moisture or rainfall during the test.
4. *Caffeine focus.* Reframe to avoid dosing peers: "How does time of day affect performance on a 5-minute attention task in Year 8 students?" The reframe removes the substance issue while still testing factors that affect focus.
5. *Goulburn River.* "How does seasonal flow variation affect the abundance of Murray cod young-of-year in selected reaches of the Goulburn River?" Hypothesis: "If natural flow patterns are restored, then young-of-year abundance will increase, because high flows trigger spawning and provide habitat connectivity."

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Topic: urban heat in your suburb.* Class-period question: "How does surface type affect midday surface temperature at three sites on the school grounds?" Term question: "How does canopy cover relate to mean afternoon air temperature across 10 streets in the local LGA?" Year-long question: "How does seasonal canopy cover and surface type predict heat-stress days in vulnerable suburbs?" Mechanisms: shade reduces radiative load, transpiration cools air, dark surfaces absorb more solar radiation.
