# VC2S8I07 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I07. Construct evidence-based arguments to support conclusions or evaluate claims, including considering ethical issues when using secondary data.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A scientific argument links claim, evidence and reasoning.
  - Secondary data needs evaluation before use.
  - Ethics shapes what you can responsibly say.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Literacy; Ethical Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct** evidence-based arguments to **support** conclusions or **evaluate** claims, including **considering** ethical issues when using **secondary data**.
- You are learning to write a clear "claim, evidence, reasoning" response to a question or claim.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **claim**, **evidence**, **reasoning** and **secondary data** in your own words.
- Build a CER paragraph that responds to a question.
- Evaluate the source of secondary data before using it.
- Identify one ethical issue when using someone else's data and a way to handle it.

## Core content

**1. Claim, evidence, reasoning.** A **claim** is what you assert. **Evidence** is the data that support it. **Reasoning** is the logic linking the two. A scientific argument needs all three. *Quick check:* What is missing from "Plants grew 12 cm. Therefore light helps growth"?

**2. Strong claims are specific.** "Climate is changing" is too broad. "Average annual temperatures in Melbourne rose by about 1.2°C between 1960 and 2020" is testable and supportable. *Quick check:* Rewrite "exercise is good for you" as a more specific claim.

**3. Evidence has to fit the claim.** A claim about long-term change needs long-term data. A claim about a relationship needs paired measurements. Mismatched evidence weakens your argument. *Quick check:* What kind of evidence would best support "shade reduces playground temperature"?

**4. Reasoning shows the link.** Reasoning explains why the evidence supports the claim. It usually invokes a mechanism. "Trees transpire water, which cools nearby air, which is why measurements are lower in shaded streets." *Quick check:* Why is "evidence speaks for itself" usually false?

**5. Secondary data and where it comes from.** **Secondary data** is data collected by someone else. Bureau of Meteorology rainfall, ABS census figures and EPA Victoria air-quality logs are common sources. Evaluate the source: who, when, how, sample, peer review. *Quick check:* Why is the date of secondary data important?

**6. Worked example: was 2024 a hot year in Melbourne?** Claim: "2024 was warmer than the 1991–2020 average." Evidence: BoM monthly mean temperatures for Melbourne airport for 2024 and the 1991–2020 baseline. Reasoning: comparing both means, and noting the difference is larger than the year-to-year noise. Ethics: cite the source, quote dates, do not crop a graph to exaggerate. *Follow the thinking:* claim → choose data → compare → explain → cite.

**7. Ethics with secondary data.** Even open data carries responsibilities. Cite sources. Do not anonymise data poorly so individuals can be re-identified. Do not present numbers from one community as a stereotype. Be wary of using cultural data without consent. *Quick check:* Name two ethical actions when using publicly available data.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** EPA Victoria, Bureau of Meteorology Australia and Department of Health Victoria publish open datasets that students can use to build evidence-based arguments. Peer-reviewed reports are good models.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish guidance on respectful use of Indigenous data, including the CARE principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics). Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material, and Indigenous datasets need community consent for re-use.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Researchers at the Murdoch Children's Research Institute, with Greek-Australian and Vietnamese-Australian communities in Melbourne, have shown how using community datasets for arguments about health requires consent and clear feedback to those communities.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A friend claims their phone runs faster after restarting. Build a CER paragraph using a short experiment you could run with two timed app loads.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Hume claims new shade trees reduce heat-related illness. Outline the evidence you would expect to support this and one ethical consideration when using hospital data.
3. **Vocational.** A junior data analyst at AusNet uses secondary energy-use data to argue for new infrastructure. List three things to check about the dataset before using it.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to use sleep data from a fitness tracker without telling family members. Identify the issue and propose a fix.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A team uses ranger-collected wildlife data for a conference talk. Outline three ethical steps to take before publishing.

## Memorable takeaways
- Claim, evidence, reasoning. Three legs, one stool.
- Specific claims earn stronger arguments.
- Match evidence to claim type.
- Cite sources, dates and methods.
- Open data is not a free pass.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "Claim: ___. Evidence: ___ from ___ on ___. Reasoning: this supports the claim because ___."
- **Words to keep close.** claim, evidence, reasoning, primary data, secondary data, citation, consent.
- **Try this picture.** Draw a three-panel "CER ladder": claim at the top, evidence in the middle, reasoning at the bottom. Write one strong sentence per rung before paragraphing.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Record a 60-second podcast where you say each part of CER aloud about a current claim in the news.

## Stretch yourself
Pick a claim from a current Victorian news story. Find one piece of secondary data that supports it and one that complicates it. Write a 200-word CER response that handles the conflict honestly and cites sources by name and date.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More evidence is always better." No. Relevant, well-cited evidence beats a long list.
- "If a graph is in the news, it is reliable." No. Always check source, date and method.
- "Open data has no ethics." No. Re-identification, stereotyping and misuse remain risks.
- "Reasoning is just opinion." No. Reasoning explains the mechanism that links the data to the claim.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Missing from "Plants grew 12 cm. Therefore light helps growth".* Reasoning. The argument needs to explain why 12 cm of growth supports the claim, including any comparison to a control.
2. *More specific version of "exercise is good for you".* "Adolescents who do at least 60 minutes of moderate physical activity most days have lower rates of depressive symptoms in studies of Australian school students."
3. *Best evidence for "shade reduces playground temperature".* Paired temperature measurements at the same surface in sun and shade across the same hour, with multiple sites and trials.
4. *Why "evidence speaks for itself" is usually false.* Without reasoning, two readers can interpret the same data differently. Reasoning makes the link explicit and testable.
5. *Why the date of secondary data matters.* Conditions and methods change. Old data may not represent current conditions, and new data may use different definitions.
6. *Two ethical actions when using public data.* Cite the source with the access date, and check that no individual could be re-identified by combining variables.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Phone restart claim.* Claim: my phone loads selected apps faster after a restart. Evidence: average load time of three apps over five trials, before and after a restart. Reasoning: a restart clears cached processes and frees memory, which reduces app launch time.
2. *Hume shade trees.* Evidence: hospital admission rates for heat-related conditions before and after planting, paired with canopy-cover and weather data. Ethical consideration: use only de-identified, aggregated hospital data and report at suburb level.
3. *AusNet energy data.* Source and authority of the dataset, the time period and any gaps, the metering method and known errors.
4. *Sleep tracker data without family consent.* Issue: privacy and consent. Fix: ask family members for explicit consent before using their data, anonymise it in any report, and limit the data to what is necessary.
5. *Ranger-collected wildlife data.* Seek written permission from the Traditional Owner corporation, agree on co-authorship and data ownership, and follow the CARE principles when reporting.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Claim: Victoria's renewable energy generation increased substantially between 2014 and 2024.* Supporting data: Australian Energy Regulator generation reports for Victoria. Complicating data: variability in capacity factor data showing high seasonal variation. CER response names the trend, the size of the change, the limits in capacity factor and the methodological notes from the regulator. Sources cited with publication date.
