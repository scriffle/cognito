# VC2S8I03 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I03. Select appropriate equipment and use it to generate and record data with attention to precision.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The right tool for the job changes what you can measure.
  - Precision and accuracy are different things.
  - Recording habits make or break a dataset.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **select** appropriate equipment and **use** it to **generate** and **record** data with attention to **precision**.
- You are learning to match instruments to measurements and keep careful records.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **precision**, **accuracy** and **resolution** in your own words, and tell them apart.
- Choose appropriate equipment for a specific measurement.
- Record data with units, uncertainty and a clear time stamp.
- Identify a source of measurement error and one way to reduce it.

## Core content

**1. Match the tool to the question.** A ruler is fine for centimetres. A digital calliper is needed for millimetres. A spring balance is fine for whole grams. An electronic balance is needed for tenths of a gram. Always ask, "what is the smallest difference I need to detect?" *Quick check:* What instrument would you choose to measure the thickness of a 5-cent coin?

**2. Precision is not accuracy.** **Precision** is how close repeated measurements are to each other. **Accuracy** is how close they are to the true value. A miscalibrated thermometer can be very precise and very inaccurate. *Quick check:* Five readings of 21.2°C from a fridge thermometer that should read 4°C are precise but what?

**3. Resolution is the smallest division.** **Resolution** is the smallest unit your instrument can show. A ruler with millimetre marks has higher resolution than one with only centimetres. Do not record more decimal places than your instrument supports. *Quick check:* A standard 30-cm ruler has what resolution?

**4. Worked example: density of a steel cube.** You need volume and mass. Volume: measure each side with a digital calliper to 0.1 mm and cube it. Mass: use an electronic balance to 0.01 g. Record three independent measurements and average them. Calculate density and report units. *Follow the thinking:* what to measure → which instrument has the right resolution → repeat to test precision → check the result against the known density of steel for a sense of accuracy.

**5. Recording habits that survive marking.** Every record needs a label, a number, a unit, a time and the instrument used. Pre-printed tables prevent missing columns. A neat record is a usable record. *Quick check:* What is wrong with "23, 24, 22.5"?

**6. Sources of error.** **Random error** comes from small, unpredictable variations and is reduced by repeating. **Systematic error** comes from a bias such as a miscalibrated instrument and is reduced by checking against a known reference. *Quick check:* Which kind of error does averaging help fix?

**7. Digital tools have their own rules.** Sensors, data loggers and apps need calibration before use. Always note the firmware or app version. A pulse from a phone-based sensor is only as good as its setup. *Quick check:* Why might two phone temperature apps give different readings in the same room?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The CSIRO STEM Education programs and the Victorian Department of Education's Science Specialist Centres provide schools with shared kits, including data loggers and microscopes, often used by classes in Bendigo, Geelong and Shepparton.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander rangers across Victoria use a mix of traditional observation and modern instruments. The Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation reports on programs that pair satellite data with ranger logs. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** In Melbourne's manufacturing south-east, technicians from Sri Lankan, Vietnamese and Anglo-Australian backgrounds use precision instruments daily. Many schools partner with these workplaces for student visits, where students see micrometres, vernier callipers and CNC measurements in action.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to know if your morning toast is consistent in mass. Choose an instrument, propose three trials, and write a recording table.
2. **Civic.** A council near Werribee is mapping noise from traffic. Suggest two instruments, name one source of error each, and one control to reduce it.
3. **Vocational.** You shadow a hospital pathologist. They use a pipette to add 0.5 mL of reagent. Explain why a graduated cylinder would not be appropriate for that step.
4. **Ethical.** A team uses a phone app to estimate heart rate during PE without explaining its accuracy. Identify the issue and propose a small fix.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung rangers and your class plan to record native fish lengths in a creek using callipers. Outline how to keep records consistent across multiple sampling teams.

## Memorable takeaways
- Match instrument resolution to the question.
- Precision is repeatable. Accuracy is right.
- Repeat to fight random error.
- Calibrate to fight systematic error.
- Label, number, unit, time, instrument.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "I need to measure ___ to within ___. I will use ___ because its resolution is ___. I will record ___, ___, ___ each time."
- **Words to keep close.** precision, accuracy, resolution, calibration, random error, systematic error.
- **Try this picture.** Draw a target: shots tightly grouped off-centre = precise but inaccurate; shots spread around the centre = accurate on average but imprecise; shots tight on centre = both. Use this to check your data.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Photograph your recording table after one trial and annotate what each column means.

## Stretch yourself
Plan a simple density measurement of three regular objects. Choose instruments to two different resolutions. Predict the limit of detection for each setup, then run three trials and compare. 200 words or one annotated table.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More decimal places means more accurate." No. Adding zeros beyond your instrument's resolution is misleading.
- "Precise data is always good data." No. Precise but inaccurate readings hide a calibration problem.
- "One reading is enough if it looks reasonable." No. Repeats expose random error.
- "Digital must be better than manual." No. A poorly calibrated digital sensor can be worse than a careful ruler.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Thickness of a 5-cent coin.* A digital calliper or micrometre. A standard ruler does not have the resolution.
2. *Five readings of 21.2°C in a fridge that should read 4°C.* Precise but inaccurate. The thermometer has a calibration problem.
3. *Resolution of a standard 30-cm ruler.* 1 mm.
4. *What is wrong with "23, 24, 22.5"?* Missing units, missing time, missing instrument and missing labels for what is being measured.
5. *Which error does averaging help fix?* Random error. Averaging cancels small unpredictable variations across repeats.
6. *Why two phone temperature apps differ.* Different sensors, different calibrations, different sampling rates and different firmware.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Toast mass consistency.* Use an electronic balance with 0.1 g resolution. Three trials at the same toast setting and slice. Table columns: trial number, mass (g), time, balance ID. Calibrate the balance with a known mass before starting.
2. *Werribee traffic noise.* Sound-level meter and a smartphone app. Sound-level meter error: wind noise on the microphone, control with a foam shield. App error: phone microphone variability, control by calibrating against the meter at the start.
3. *Pipette vs cylinder.* The pipette delivers 0.5 mL with high precision and small uncertainty. A graduated cylinder's resolution is usually 1 mL, so it cannot deliver 0.5 mL accurately enough for a clinical reagent.
4. *PE heart-rate app.* Issue: results may be inaccurate and students may rely on them. Fix: cross-check the app against a manual pulse for 30 seconds at the start of class and report the difference.
5. *Native fish lengths with rangers.* Use the same calliper model across teams, calibrate against a reference rod each morning, agree on the measurement point on each fish, and use a shared digital form with required fields for time, site, calliper ID and team.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Three objects: a die, a marble, a small block.* Setup A: ruler and triple-beam balance. Setup B: digital calliper and electronic balance. Predicted detection limit: A about 1 mm and 0.1 g, B about 0.1 mm and 0.01 g. Run three trials each. Compare measured densities and standard deviations. The improvement should be visible in tighter ranges with setup B and smaller relative uncertainty in the calculated density.
