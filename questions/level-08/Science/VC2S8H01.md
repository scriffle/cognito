# VC2S8H01 — Science as a Human Endeavour

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8H01. Scientific knowledge, including models and theories, can change when new evidence comes to light.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Science as a Human Endeavour).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Scientific knowledge is provisional, not fixed.
  - New evidence can refine, extend or replace a model.
  - Long-observation knowledge systems also update over time.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that scientific knowledge **can change** when new evidence comes to light.
- You are learning to explain how a specific model or theory was revised.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **model**, **theory** and **evidence** in your own words.
- Give one example where new evidence changed an accepted scientific idea.
- Explain why provisional knowledge is a strength of science, not a weakness.
- Tell the difference between a small refinement and a full theory replacement.

## Core content

**1. What scientists mean by a theory.** A **theory** is a well-tested explanation that ties many observations together. It is not a guess. Theories survive only while they fit the evidence. *Quick check:* Is "theory" in science closer to "hunch" or "best current explanation"?

**2. What counts as evidence.** **Evidence** is data collected through observation or experiment that others can repeat. One striking result is not enough. Many independent results pointing the same way are stronger. *Quick check:* Why would you trust many studies over one?

**3. Models are useful, not perfect.** A **model** is a simplified version of something complex, used to predict and explain. The Bohr model of the atom is a famous example. It works well for simple atoms but breaks down for heavier ones, so chemists use a quantum model instead. *Quick check:* Name one thing the Bohr model gets right and one it gets wrong.

**4. Worked example: stomach ulcers.** Until the 1980s, doctors blamed stomach ulcers on stress and spicy food. Australian researchers Barry Marshall and Robin Warren found *Helicobacter pylori* bacteria in ulcer tissue. To test the link, Marshall drank a culture of the bacteria and developed gastritis. Repeated trials confirmed the result. Treatment shifted to antibiotics. They received the 2005 Nobel Prize. *Follow the thinking:* Old idea (stress) → anomaly (bacteria seen in tissue) → bold test (self-infection) → replication → new theory (infection causes most ulcers) → changed practice.

**5. Refinement vs replacement.** Most changes are refinements. The germ theory of disease was extended, not thrown out, when viruses were identified. Sometimes a theory is replaced. The idea that continents sat still gave way to **plate tectonics** in the 1960s after seafloor mapping. *Quick check:* Is updating the periodic table when a new element is confirmed a refinement or a replacement?

**6. Why this matters when you read the news.** Headlines often say "scientists were wrong". A better reading is "scientists updated the model with new evidence". Look for the size of the study, whether it has been repeated, and who funded it. *Quick check:* What three questions help you judge a science story?

**7. Uncertainty is named, not hidden.** Good science states the size of its error bars and the limits of its claims. Climate models, for instance, give ranges, not single numbers. That is honest, not weak. *Quick check:* Why does a range of predictions show care, not confusion?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Melbourne-based Walter and Eliza Hall Institute regularly updates its understanding of immune cells as new imaging tools appear. You can track recent research summaries on the Institute's public site.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal astronomical knowledge held by peoples across Victoria, including Boorong knowledge from the Wergaia Country in the north-west, has been observed, tested and refined across many generations. Researchers such as Duane Hamacher have worked with Elders to publish on this in peer-reviewed journals. The point is that long-running, evidence-based knowledge systems exist outside Western science and also update over time. Source pointer: Hamacher and Frew, *Journal of Astronomical History and Heritage*, on Boorong astronomy. Specific star knowledge should only be studied through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** In Melbourne's western suburbs, students from Vietnamese and Sudanese backgrounds may bring family knowledge of traditional medicines. Some treatments have been studied scientifically and supported, others not. The skill is the same: ask what evidence supports a claim, and from where. Compare with the Indian Ayurvedic tradition, parts of which are now being tested in clinical trials in Australia and overseas.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your grandparent says, "We were told butter was bad, then good, then bad again." Explain in three sentences why dietary advice changes, using the words **evidence** and **model**. End with one tip for reading nutrition headlines.
2. **Civic.** A councillor in Geelong proposes banning a common pesticide after one local study found bee deaths. Imagine you are a Year 8 advisor. Write a 60-word note explaining what extra evidence the council should ask for before deciding.
3. **Vocational.** A trainee nurse hears two senior staff disagree about whether to ice or heat a sprain. Outline how she could find current best-evidence guidance, and what she should do if the guidance updates next year.
4. **Ethical.** Marshall infected himself to test his ulcer hypothesis. Was that ethical? List one argument for and one against, then say what today's ethics committees would likely require instead.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung rangers near Ballarat use long-term observations of seasonal indicators to time cool burns. A visiting scientist wants to add satellite data. Suggest how the two knowledge sources could be combined respectfully and what each adds.

## Memorable takeaways
- Theories are best current explanations, not final truths.
- Evidence updates models. That is science working.
- One study is a clue. Many studies are a case.
- Refinement is common. Replacement is rare.
- Uncertainty stated is honesty, not weakness.

## Watch out for these traps
- "A theory is just a guess." No. A scientific theory is a well-tested, evidence-supported explanation.
- "If scientists changed their mind, they were wrong before." No. They had the best explanation for the evidence available then.
- "New always replaces old." No. Most updates refine the existing model.
- "Indigenous knowledge is static and ancient." No. It is observation-based and continually updated by communities.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Theory: hunch or best current explanation?* Best current explanation. A scientific theory is well-tested and supported by many lines of evidence.
2. *Why prefer many studies over one?* One result can be a fluke, a fraud or a one-off context. Repeated independent results make a coincidence unlikely.
3. *Bohr model — one strength, one weakness.* Strength: it explains why hydrogen gives off specific colours of light. Weakness: it predicts the wrong arrangements for atoms heavier than helium.
4. *Refinement or replacement when a new element is added to the periodic table?* Refinement. The structure of the table holds. A new entry extends it.
5. *Three questions for a science headline.* How big was the study? Has the result been repeated by other groups? Who funded the work and could that bias it?
6. *Why does a range of predictions show care?* It states the limits of the data and the model. A single number would hide that uncertainty.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Everyday — butter.* Dietary advice changes because new **evidence** from larger and longer studies updates the **model** of how fats affect the heart. The science was not lying before. It was working with what it had. Tip: trust review articles and national guidelines over single headlines.
2. *Civic — Geelong pesticide.* The council should ask for repeat studies in different locations, a comparison with control sites, dose data and review by an independent toxicologist. One local study is a useful flag, not a basis for a ban. Decide on the weight of evidence, not the loudness of the claim.
3. *Vocational — ice or heat.* Check current Australian clinical guidelines, for example the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners or HealthDirect. If guidance changes next year, the trainee follows the new guidance and tells colleagues why it changed. Updating practice on better evidence is part of the job.
4. *Ethical — Marshall self-experiment.* For: he risked only himself and the result helped millions. Against: he could not give fully informed consent to himself and set a risky example. Today an ethics committee would require a written protocol, independent review, animal or *in vitro* work first, and consenting adult volunteers with monitoring.
5. *Cross-cultural — Wadawurrung rangers and satellite data.* Combine respectfully by following Wadawurrung leadership of the project, agreeing on data sharing rules, and using satellite data to check and extend ranger observations rather than replace them. Long-term observation gives seasonal nuance. Satellite data gives wide spatial coverage and an audit trail.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Story: 2025 update to Australian dietary fibre recommendations after new microbiome trials.* Prior model: fibre mostly aided bowel transit. New evidence: randomised trials linked specific fibres to gut bacteria changes that affect inflammation. Update: revised intake targets and fibre-type advice. Still uncertain: how individual microbiomes respond. Why this counts as a model update, not a reversal: the old advice was not wrong, it was incomplete.
