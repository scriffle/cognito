# VC2S8I02 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I02. Plan reproducible investigations by identifying variables, managing risks, considering ethical issues and following cultural protocols on Country.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Reproducibility is what turns one result into a finding.
  - Safety, ethics and protocols are part of the method, not optional add-ons.
  - On Country, consent and protocols come before data collection.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures; Ethical Capability; Personal and Social Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan** reproducible investigations by **identifying** variables, **managing** risks, **considering** ethical issues and **following** cultural protocols on Country.
- You are learning to write a method another team could repeat.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **reproducibility**, **risk assessment** and **protocol** in your own words.
- Identify the variables in a planned investigation.
- Write a risk-assessment line that names a hazard, who is at risk, and the control.
- Describe what cultural protocol on Country means in a school study.

## Core content

**1. Why reproducibility matters.** **Reproducibility** means another team, given your method, could get a similar result. If they cannot, your finding is fragile. Method clarity is the difference between "we tried something" and "we tested something". *Quick check:* Why is "we did our best" not enough in a method?

**2. Variables, again, with care.** Identify the independent, dependent and at least three controlled variables before you collect data. Naming them up front prevents drift mid-experiment. *Quick check:* What might happen if you do not name controlled variables before starting?

**3. Risk assessment in three columns.** A simple **risk assessment** lists hazards, who is at risk and the control. For instance, "Hot beaker → hands and clothes → use tongs and lab coat". This is a school version of the same approach used in workplaces. *Quick check:* Write a one-line risk row for "wet floor near the sink".

**4. Ethical issues to consider.** Ethics asks who could be harmed and how you protect them. Working with classmates, animals or community members raises consent, privacy and dignity. Even surveys need ethical thinking. *Quick check:* What is one ethical issue when surveying classmates about sleep?

**5. Cultural protocols on Country.** A **protocol** is a set of agreed rules for behaviour on Country. Acknowledging Country, seeking permission from Traditional Owner corporations, following ranger guidance and respecting culturally significant sites are core. Doing science on Country without protocol is not just impolite, it can damage knowledge systems and relationships. *Quick check:* Why is acknowledging Country only the first step, not the whole protocol?

**6. Worked example: water-quality study at Merri Creek.** On Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Country in northern Melbourne, your class plans to test pH and turbidity at three sites. Reproducible method: sample at the same time of day, with the same calibrated probes, at the same depth, in the same week. Risk assessment: slippery banks, unknown water quality, sun exposure, controls listed for each. Ethics: do not photograph other waterway users without consent. Cultural protocol: contact the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Cultural Heritage Aboriginal Corporation for guidance and follow Friends of Merri Creek site rules. *Follow the thinking:* identify variables → write the method → list hazards and controls → consider ethics → seek protocol guidance → only then sample.

**7. The "could a peer repeat this?" test.** Read your plan aloud. If a stranger could not follow it, rewrite it. Numbers of trials, timings, instrument settings and decision rules should all be explicit. *Quick check:* What is one detail that students often leave out of a method?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Water and the Environment Protection Authority Victoria publish citizen-science guides for sampling waterways. Their templates include risk assessments and reproducibility checklists.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Cultural Heritage Aboriginal Corporation and Bunurong Land Council Aboriginal Corporation publish protocols for working on Country. Researchers such as Aunty Joy Murphy and the AIATSIS code of ethics describe consent and benefit-sharing in detail. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material, and on-Country fieldwork should be cleared with the relevant corporation.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Indian and Sri Lankan-Australian researchers at Deakin work on water quality in Melbourne's south-east. Their fieldwork includes culturally responsive engagement with Tamil-speaking and Vietnamese-speaking communities along the Dandenong Creek. Method clarity helps when teams span multiple languages and institutions.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You plan to test which face-mask material best filters droplets. Write three controlled variables and one risk-assessment row.
2. **Civic.** Your class plans to map litter on a Bayside beach. Write a one-paragraph reproducible method, including the sampling rule and one ethical consideration.
3. **Vocational.** You are a junior environmental tech at a wetland near Werribee. Write three lines of risk assessment and one cultural-protocol step you would follow before fieldwork.
4. **Ethical.** A team wants to weigh classmates to study fitness. Identify two ethical issues and rewrite the design to address them.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung rangers invite your class to test soil moisture under different vegetation types. Outline a four-step plan that respects protocol, lists variables and ends with a shared output.

## Memorable takeaways
- A method another team can repeat is a finding.
- Name variables before you start.
- Hazard, who, control. Three columns, every time.
- Protocol comes before sampling.
- Acknowledging Country is the start, not the whole.

## Stretch yourself
Take an existing simple experiment from class. Rewrite the method so a Year 7 student in another school could reproduce it exactly. Add a risk assessment, two ethical considerations and one protocol step. 200 words or one annotated diagram.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Risk assessments are paperwork." No. They prevent injuries and shape better methods.
- "Ethics only matters with humans." No. Animal and community impacts also need ethical thinking.
- "Acknowledging Country is enough." No. Acknowledgement is one part. Permission, consultation and following ranger guidance follow.
- "If it worked once, it is reproducible." No. Reproducibility is shown by independent repeats, not single successes.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "we did our best" is not enough.* It is unrepeatable. Another team needs the same steps, settings, timings and rules to test the result.
2. *If you do not name controlled variables.* Conditions drift mid-experiment, so any difference in your data could be caused by something you forgot to hold constant.
3. *Risk row for "wet floor near the sink".* "Wet floor near the sink → students walking past → mop spills, place a wet-floor sign, route foot traffic away."
4. *One ethical issue in a sleep survey.* Privacy. Sleep can reveal household stress or health. Use anonymous responses and let students opt out.
5. *Why acknowledging Country is the start.* It signals respect, but the work of consent, consultation and following protocols on the ground is what actually protects Country and relationships.
6. *A detail often left out of a method.* The number of trials, the timing, or the calibration step for the instrument.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Mask material test.* Controlled: airflow rate, distance from droplet source, mask area, droplet size, room temperature. Risk row: "Aerosol from a spray bottle → respiratory exposure → ventilate the room and wear a face shield, dispose of materials in sealed bags."
2. *Bayside litter map.* Walk a 100 m transect at 9 am with two students per side, record every visible item over 2 cm in a shared form, photograph but do not move hazardous items, repeat on three consecutive days. Ethical consideration: do not photograph beachgoers' faces or include identifying details.
3. *Werribee wetland.* Risks: slippery banks (use buddies and grip footwear), wildlife disturbance (stay on paths, no swimming), sun and dehydration (sunscreen, water, hat). Protocol: contact Bunurong Land Council Aboriginal Corporation in advance to confirm any cultural sensitivity at the proposed sampling sites.
4. *Weighing classmates for fitness.* Issues: body image and consent. Rewrite: replace mass with self-rated fitness or a step-test pulse change, allow opt-out, anonymise data, and present only group averages.
5. *Wadawurrung soil moisture.* Step 1: contact the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation for site selection and protocol. Step 2: define variables (vegetation type, time of day, depth) with rangers. Step 3: train students with rangers in safe, low-impact sampling. Step 4: co-author a one-page summary of findings shared with the corporation and the school.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Existing experiment: temperature and dissolution rate of sugar in water.* Rewrite: calibrate the thermometer; use 100 mL of water in identical beakers; weigh 5 g of fine white sugar; stir at 60 rpm with a magnetic stirrer; record dissolution time at 10°C, 20°C, 30°C, 40°C and 50°C; run three trials per temperature and report the mean and range. Risk assessment: hot water (use insulated gloves), broken glass (carry beakers with two hands), spills (wipe immediately). Ethics: handle food-grade materials hygienically. Protocol: not on-Country in this case, but acknowledge the school sits on traditional lands.
