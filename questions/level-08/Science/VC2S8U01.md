## What you are learning
- You are learning that organisms can be **classified** into groups based on **similarities** and **differences**, and that tools like **dichotomous keys** help organise this diversity.
- You are learning to use observable features to place an organism in a group.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **classification**, **species** and **dichotomous key** in your own words.
- List the major levels of biological classification in order.
- Use a simple key to identify a small group of plants or animals.
- Explain why classification supports both science and conservation.

## Core content

**1. Why classify?** Earth holds millions of species. **Classification** sorts them into groups based on shared features so we can study, communicate and protect them. *Quick check:* What is one practical reason to give every species a unique name?

**2. From kingdom to species.** The standard hierarchy is Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species. A **species** is a group of organisms that can breed to produce fertile young. The Eastern Grey Kangaroo is *Macropus giganteus*. *Quick check:* Which two levels are used in a scientific name?

**3. Similarities and differences as evidence.** Classification used to rely only on body shape. Now scientists also use behaviour, biochemistry and DNA. New evidence sometimes moves species between groups. *Quick check:* Why might a group be split when DNA evidence is added?

**4. Worked example: birds at the school grounds.** You record three species: a magpie, a noisy miner and a rainbow lorikeet. All are class Aves. They differ in family and species. Use observable features: size, beak shape, colour and call. A simple **dichotomous key** asks "Is the bird mostly black and white? → magpie. Else, is the beak red? → rainbow lorikeet. Else, mostly grey with a yellow patch? → noisy miner." *Follow the thinking:* observe → choose features → write yes/no questions → test the key with a partner.

**5. Keys with clear features.** A good dichotomous key uses observable features only. Avoid "looks similar to" and other opinion words. Test your key by giving it to someone else. *Quick check:* Why is "feels soft" a poor key feature?

**6. Classification supports conservation.** Knowing what a species is matters for protection laws and recovery plans. Mountain Pygmy-possum, *Burramys parvus*, is endangered, found only in alpine Victoria and New South Wales. Mistaking it for a similar species would weaken protection. *Quick check:* Why does an unclear name slow conservation work?

**7. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge of species.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Wadawurrung and other Victorian peoples have maintained detailed knowledge of plants, animals and habitats for thousands of years. Some species names in Woiwurrung and Boon Wurrung are now used alongside Western scientific names. Researchers including Bruce Pascoe and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute have published on this work. *Quick check:* Why does using both naming systems strengthen biology in Australia?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Atlas of Living Australia hosts millions of records, many from Victorian citizen-science contributors. Programs such as Bird Life Australia surveys collect species records across the state.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Languages Australia and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish guidance on respectful use of Indigenous species names. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Wadawurrung and other Victorian peoples maintain ecological knowledge about Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Vietnamese-Australian, Sri Lankan-Australian and Italian-Australian community gardeners across Melbourne's south-east cultivate edible plants from many regions. Their plant knowledge expands what species are grown locally and supports cross-cultural learning in biology classes.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You have a tray of seeds from four common Australian plants. Build a four-step dichotomous key based on observable features.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Yarra Ranges is mapping local frogs. Suggest three observable features you would put in a frog identification key for citizen scientists.
3. **Vocational.** A junior ranger at Werribee Open Range Zoo prepares a keeper guide for new staff. Draft three steps of a dichotomous key for separating three antelope species using observable features.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to collect insects for a classification project. Identify two ethical issues and propose alternatives that still teach the skill.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung rangers and your class produce a guide to local birds using both Wadawurrung and scientific names. Outline how to gather permission, design the layout and credit contributors.

## Memorable takeaways
- Names enable science, study and care.
- Two-name binomial: genus and species.
- Use observable features in keys.
- DNA can split or merge groups.
- Two naming systems are stronger than one.

## Stretch yourself
Pick a small group of five Victorian native plants or animals. Build a dichotomous key, including the source of each photo. Ask a peer to test it and record any step that confused them. Revise. 200 words or one annotated key.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Common names are good enough." No. They vary by region. Scientific names are unique, which matters in research and law.
- "Classification is fixed forever." No. New evidence, including DNA, can move species between groups.
- "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge is just folklore." No. It is accurate, observation-based ecological knowledge developed over millennia.
- "Keys must use complex features." No. Clear, observable features make better keys.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *One practical reason for unique species names.* So researchers, governments and farmers in different places mean the same thing when they discuss a species.
2. *Two levels in a scientific name.* Genus and species. The genus is capitalised, both italicised.
3. *Why DNA evidence can split a group.* Two animals that look alike can have very different DNA, showing they are not as closely related as their appearance suggests.
4. *Why "feels soft" is a poor key feature.* It is opinion-based and varies between users. Keys need observable features that everyone agrees on.
5. *Why an unclear name slows conservation.* Funding, protection and monitoring need precise targets. The wrong name can mean the wrong species is protected.
6. *Why two naming systems strengthen biology.* They draw on more knowledge holders, more observations and more language for nuance, all of which improve identification and care.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Plant seeds key.* Step 1: is the seed flat? → A or B. If yes, is it brown? → A. If no, is it round? → C or D. Continue with one observable feature per step.
2. *Frog key features.* Skin texture (smooth or warty), call (continuous or pulsed), back colour pattern (plain, striped or spotted).
3. *Antelope key.* Step 1: is the body taller than 1.4 m at the shoulder? Step 2: are the horns spiral? Step 3: is the coat striped? Use observable features at each step.
4. *Insect collection ethics.* Issues: harm to invertebrates, biodiversity impact. Alternatives: photograph and observe in place, use catch-and-release sweep nets, use existing photo collections.
5. *Wadawurrung bird guide.* Seek written permission from the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Co-design layout with rangers. Place Wadawurrung name first, scientific name in italics, and credit knowledge holders by name where they consent.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Five species: red gum, manna gum, swamp gum, river red box, candlebark.* Key uses observable bark, leaf and bud features. Photos credited to Atlas of Living Australia entries with date accessed. Peer-tested with two students, the original step on bark colour was rewritten because shadow changed perception. Revised key shows the change and the reason.
