## Core content

**1. Igneous rocks.** **Igneous** rocks form when molten rock cools and solidifies. Slow cooling deep underground creates large crystals (granite). Fast cooling at the surface creates fine crystals or glassy textures (basalt, obsidian). *Quick check:* Why does granite have larger crystals than basalt?

**2. Sedimentary rocks.** **Sedimentary** rocks form when weathered fragments, dissolved minerals, or organic remains settle in layers and are compacted and cemented over time. Sandstone, mudstone and limestone are examples. They often hold fossils. *Quick check:* Why are fossils almost always found in sedimentary rocks?

**3. Metamorphic rocks.** **Metamorphic** rocks form when existing rocks are exposed to heat and pressure, causing crystals and structures to change without melting. Limestone becomes marble. Mudstone becomes slate, then schist, then gneiss with increasing intensity. *Quick check:* What is one feature you might see in a metamorphic rock?

**4. The rock cycle.** Rocks move between families. An igneous rock can weather into sediment, become a sedimentary rock, then be heated and pressed into a metamorphic rock, then melt and become igneous again. The cycle has no fixed start. *Quick check:* What process turns sediment into a sedimentary rock?

**5. Worked example: a basalt boulder near Mount Macedon.** The Newer Volcanic Province in western Victoria erupted basalts as recently as a few thousand years ago. A basalt boulder shows fine crystals (fast cooling at the surface), small holes from gas bubbles, and dark colour from iron-rich minerals. Over millions of years, weathering breaks it down. River systems transport fragments, which settle as sediment, possibly forming sandstone in a future basin. *Follow the thinking:* origin (lava flow) → cooling rate → minerals → weathering → transport → sediment → new rock.

**6. Timescales differ.** Some igneous rocks form in days from a single eruption. Sediment may take thousands to millions of years to lithify. Metamorphic transformations often unfold over millions of years deep underground. *Quick check:* Why is "the rock cycle takes one timescale" wrong?

**7. Properties tell the story.** Grain size, layering, fossils, foliation and mineral composition each give clues. Reading rocks is like reading a diary. *Quick check:* What might thin parallel layers suggest about a rock's origin?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Hanging Rock formation near Macedon is a remnant of a viscous magma intrusion that cooled to form mamelons. The Twelve Apostles along the Great Ocean Road are sedimentary limestone shaped by waves. The Newer Volcanic Province across western Victoria contains hundreds of young volcanic landforms.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have used rocks for tools, art and ceremony for tens of thousands of years. The Mount William stone-axe quarry on Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Country traded greenstone tools across south-eastern Australia. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Italian-Australian masons in Carlton and North Melbourne built bluestone (basalt) terraces from local quarries. Greek-Australian stonemasons restored historic limestone buildings in Melbourne's CBD. Knowledge of rock properties travelled with these communities.

## Memorable takeaways
- Three rock families: igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic.
- The cycle has no single direction.
- Heat and pressure transform without melting.
- Properties record the rock's past.
- Timescales differ, sometimes by billions.

## Watch out for these traps
- "All rocks form the same way." No. The three families form by different processes.
- "Sedimentary rocks are weak." No. Some sandstones and limestones are extremely durable.
- "The rock cycle is a one-way street." No. Rocks can transform along many possible pathways.
- "Old rocks are at the bottom, always." Often, but tectonics and erosion can flip layers.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why does granite have larger crystals than basalt?* Granite cools slowly underground, allowing crystals more time to grow. Basalt cools quickly at or near the surface, freezing crystals while they are still small.
2. *Why are fossils almost always in sedimentary rocks?* Fossils form when remains are buried in sediment that protects them as it lithifies. Heat and pressure in metamorphic rocks usually destroy fossils, and igneous melt erases them.
3. *Feature in a metamorphic rock.* Foliation, where minerals are aligned in bands, often visible in slate or schist.
4. *Process turning sediment into sedimentary rock.* Lithification, which combines compaction by overlying weight and cementation by minerals from groundwater.
5. *Why "one timescale" is wrong.* Igneous formation can be days; sediment lithification can take millions of years; deep metamorphism can take tens of millions.
6. *Thin parallel layers suggest…* A sedimentary origin where successive layers of sediment settled over time.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Williamstown pebble.* Note grain size, presence of layers or foliation, and any visible crystals or fossils. The rock could have originated from local basalts or imported as fill, and the pebble's roundness shows transport.
2. *Geelong paving.* Basalt is hard and locally available, suiting a heavy-traffic surface. Limestone is softer and slipperier when wet, but lighter and easier to cut. Granite is durable and decorative but more expensive to source and shape.
3. *Otways road-cut.* Layered bedding planes and possibly fossils embedded in the rock. The grain may also tell you how energetic the depositional environment was.
4. *National park samples.* Issues: collecting may be illegal in protected areas and may damage cultural sites. Plan: photograph rocks, view museum collections, or use teacher-supplied legal samples.
5. *Greenstone discussion.* Discuss formation as metamorphosed basalt under permission and credit. Frame cultural significance through Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung-led narratives, using only published, community-approved material.
