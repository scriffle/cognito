# VC2S8U14 — Science Understanding

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8U14. Balanced and unbalanced forces determine whether an object stays still, speeds up, slows down or changes direction, and force diagrams help us represent this.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Physical Sciences: Forces & Energy).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Forces come in pairs and add as vectors.
  - Balanced forces leave motion unchanged.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that **balanced** and **unbalanced** forces determine whether an object **stays still**, **speeds up**, **slows down** or **changes direction**, and that **force diagrams** help us represent this.
- You are learning to draw clear arrows that show forces and their effects.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **net force**, **balanced force** and **acceleration** in your own words.
- Draw a labelled force diagram for an everyday object.
- Predict the effect of changing one force.
- Explain why a moving object can be in balanced-force conditions.

## Core content

**1. Forces in pairs and groups.** A **force** has size and direction. Most situations involve several forces acting at once. Drawing them as arrows from the centre of an object lets us add them. *Quick check:* What two pieces of information does every force arrow need?

**2. Net force.** **Net force** is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces combined. If forces cancel, the net force is zero. *Quick check:* What is the net force on a book sitting still on a table?

**3. Balanced forces, no acceleration.** If forces are **balanced**, the object continues at constant velocity. Standing still is constant velocity. So is cruising at 60 km/h on a flat road. *Quick check:* Why is a falling skydiver at terminal velocity in balanced-force conditions?

**4. Unbalanced forces, change of motion.** An **unbalanced** net force causes **acceleration**: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction. A bigger net force or smaller mass means greater acceleration (Newton's second law). *Quick check:* Why does a small car reach speed faster than a heavy truck with the same engine?

**5. Worked example: pushing a trolley at the supermarket.** A 30 kg trolley is pushed with 60 N. Friction acts back at 20 N. Net force = 60 − 20 = 40 N forward. The trolley accelerates forward. If the customer reduces push to 20 N, friction balances it and the trolley continues at the same speed (or stops if it was at rest). *Follow the thinking:* identify forces → draw arrows to scale → calculate net force → predict motion change.

**6. Free-body diagrams.** A **free-body diagram** shows all the forces on a single object as arrows from a dot. Common forces: gravity (down), normal (up from a surface), friction (against motion), applied force, tension. *Quick check:* What direction does friction usually point on a moving object?

**7. Real motion is often complex.** In real situations, surfaces, air, and uneven loads all add up. A simple model captures the main forces; a more detailed model adds smaller ones. *Quick check:* Why do school physics problems usually ignore air resistance for short drops?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Australian Synchrotron in Clayton uses precisely controlled forces to steer particles. Public Transport Victoria buses and trams demonstrate balanced and unbalanced forces every time they brake or accelerate.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge of throwing and trapping technologies (boomerangs, woomeras, spears, fish traps) reflects deep practical understanding of force, balance and trajectory. The Indigenous Knowledge Institute publishes work on this engineering tradition. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Vietnamese-Australian, Greek-Australian and Iranian-Australian engineering students at Melbourne universities apply force-and-motion principles to driver-assist car safety and prosthetic design.

## Memorable takeaways
- Forces have size and direction.
- Balanced means no change in motion.
- Unbalanced means acceleration.
- Diagrams beat words for forces.
- Constant velocity is balanced.

## Watch out for these traps
- "If something is moving, there must be a net force." No. Constant velocity needs zero net force.
- "Friction always slows things down." Often, but it can also be the reason a wheel can drive forward.
- "Heavier means a stronger force." Weight is a force, but acceleration depends on the net force divided by mass.
- "Force diagrams need every detail." No. They show the relevant forces simply.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Two pieces of information per force arrow.* Size (length) and direction.
2. *Net force on a still book.* Zero. Gravity down balances normal force up.
3. *Skydiver at terminal velocity.* Air resistance up equals weight down. Net force is zero, so acceleration is zero, even though the skydiver is moving fast.
4. *Why a small car accelerates faster.* For the same net force, a smaller mass produces a larger acceleration (a = F/m).
5. *Direction of friction on a moving object.* Opposite to the direction of motion.
6. *Why physics problems often ignore air resistance for short drops.* The effect is small for slow speeds and short distances; including it complicates the calculation without changing the answer much.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Constant-speed bike on Beach Road.* Forward thrust from pedalling balances air drag and rolling friction. Gravity down balances normal force up. Net force zero, so velocity is constant.
2. *Yarra speed humps.* They force drivers to slow before crossing, requiring braking (deceleration) and acceleration after. Pedestrian safety improves because vehicles are at lower speeds at crossings.
3. *Physio limp assessment.* Force diagrams reveal where the patient is loading or unloading limbs. They guide exercise plans that rebalance forces over a stride and reduce stress on injured joints.
4. *Phone case drop test.* Issues: damage to property, risk of broken glass. Safer setup: use a controlled drop tower with foam catch, low height, and supervised conditions; record forces with a meter app.
5. *Wurundjeri throwing tools.* Discuss with permission and credit. Explain the lever and projectile physics: applied force at the throwing arm gains greater range from the tool's leverage. Use only published, community-approved material.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Sport: AFL.* Moments: kicking the ball (impact force); tackling (impulse and friction); marking (deceleration in the air). Free-body diagram: a player landing from a mark, showing weight, ground reaction force, and friction. Small change: shifting weight forward by 5 cm could change ankle moment and reduce injury risk.
