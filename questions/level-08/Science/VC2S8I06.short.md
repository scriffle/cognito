# VC2S8I06 — Science Inquiry Skills (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Evaluate scientific methods and conclusions by identifying assumptions, sources of error, conflicting evidence and unanswered questions.
- **Year / Area:** Year 8, Science.
- **Big ideas:** Evaluate as you go. Name assumptions. Conflicts and gaps point the way forward.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate** methods and conclusions by **identifying** assumptions, errors, conflicts and unanswered questions.
- You should be able to read a study, including your own, with a fair but tough eye.

## Core content

An **assumption** is a belief carried into a study without testing it. **Random errors** are reduced by repeating; **systematic errors** are reduced by calibration and design. **Conflicting evidence** between studies usually means different methods, samples or chance. Every study leaves **unanswered questions**, and listing them is a sign of honesty.

Use hedged language: "suggests", "appears to", "within the conditions tested". Avoid "proves" unless your study is designed to test causation rigorously, which most school studies are not.

**Worked example.** A school cafeteria food-waste study weighs leftovers across five lunchtimes. Assumptions: similar menus, similar attendance, working scales. Errors: random (some students hide food), systematic (uncalibrated scale). Conflicting evidence: a student survey says waste is mostly bread, but weights say fruit. Unanswered question: are students underreporting one item?

**Cultural context.** Country Needs People and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish program evaluations that list cultural protocols and limitations. Western evaluation frameworks can sit alongside community-led review. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Evaluate as you go, not only at the end.
- Name assumptions to make them testable.
- Random repeats. Systematic calibrates.
- "Suggests" beats "proves".

## Watch out for these traps
- "Evaluation is just self-criticism." No. It is what makes a finding trustworthy.
- "Assumptions are weaknesses." No. Naming them is a strength. Hidden ones are weaknesses.
- "If two studies disagree, one is wrong." No. They may be measuring different things.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *What is an assumption?* A belief carried into a study without testing it.
- *One assumption when using a phone temperature app.* That the sensor is accurate, calibrated and not affected by case heat or charging.
- *Why is "this proves exercise improves grades" too strong?* The data are correlational and small. Better: "In this sample, students who exercised regularly tended to score higher, suggesting a link worth further study."
- *Why finished reports still end with questions.* No study answers everything. Naming gaps directs the next study and shows what has not been shown.
- *Short summary of evaluating the food-waste study.* List assumptions, name random and systematic errors, compare survey and weight data and explain the conflict, then propose a follow-up that resolves the underreporting issue.
