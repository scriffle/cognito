# VC2S8H03 — Science as a Human Endeavour

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8H03. Scientific responses to real-world issues can involve ethical, environmental, social and economic trade-offs.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Science as a Human Endeavour).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Real-world science choices rarely have a perfect option.
  - Trade-offs balance ethics, environment, society and economy.
  - Whose interests count is part of the science decision.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Sustainability; Ethical Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that scientific responses to real-world issues can **involve** ethical, environmental, social and economic **trade-offs**.
- You are learning to weigh and explain those trade-offs in concrete cases.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **trade-off**, **stakeholder** and **opportunity cost** in your own words.
- Identify ethical, environmental, social and economic effects of a real decision.
- Explain why a "best" answer can change if you give different effects different weight.
- Describe one way to make a trade-off decision more transparent.

## Core content

**1. What a trade-off is.** A **trade-off** is a choice where gaining one thing costs you another. Choosing a faster but more expensive train upgrade is a trade-off between time and money. *Quick check:* Name a small daily trade-off you make.

**2. Four lenses to apply.** Useful science decisions are usually examined through four lenses: **ethical** (right and wrong, fairness), **environmental** (effects on ecosystems and climate), **social** (effects on people and communities) and **economic** (costs, jobs, value). *Quick check:* Why use all four rather than just one?

**3. Stakeholders matter.** A **stakeholder** is anyone affected by a decision. Listing stakeholders forces you to ask whose costs and benefits you are counting. Hidden stakeholders, like future generations or non-human species, are easy to miss. *Quick check:* Who is a stakeholder in a new wind farm near Ararat?

**4. Worked example: offshore wind near Gippsland.** Australia is planning offshore wind zones, including off Gippsland on Gunaikurnai Country. Ethical: how to share benefits with Traditional Owners and locals. Environmental: turbines reduce emissions but can affect seabirds and whales. Social: construction and operation jobs in the Latrobe Valley. Economic: high upfront cost, lower fuel bills over time. *Follow the thinking:* identify each lens → list one positive and one negative under each → ask who decides → propose a condition that improves the deal (such as community ownership shares).

**5. Opportunity cost.** **Opportunity cost** is what you give up when you pick one option. Money spent on one project cannot be spent on another. A government that funds desalination has less to spend on stormwater capture. *Quick check:* What is the opportunity cost of your school spending on iPads instead of a science lab upgrade?

**6. Weighting changes the answer.** Two reasonable people can study the same evidence and reach different conclusions because they weight effects differently. Naming your weights makes a debate honest. *Quick check:* Why is "honest disagreement" different from "wrong"?

**7. Tools that make trade-offs visible.** Cost-benefit analysis, multi-criteria scoring and stakeholder mapping force the decision into the open. They are not perfect, but they beat hidden judgement. *Quick check:* Why is showing the working better than just announcing the answer?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Western Treatment Plant at Werribee on Bunurong Country protects Port Phillip Bay while creating wetlands used by migratory birds. Decisions there balance treatment cost, energy use and conservation outcomes.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** On Gunaikurnai Country, the Gunaikurnai Land and Waters Aboriginal Corporation has signed agreements with the Victorian Government over joint management of parks. Trade-offs include cultural protection, tourism income and ecological restoration. Authors such as the late Aunty Doris Paton and ongoing GLaWAC reports describe the complexity. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** In Melbourne's north and west, Indian, Sudanese and Italian-Australian growers run market gardens that depend on stable water allocations. Drought policy decisions affect their livelihoods and food prices. In coastal communities such as Cairns and Karratha, Filipino and Anglo-Australian fisheries workers feel marine-park trade-offs sharply.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your family is choosing between an electric and a hybrid car. Map the four lenses for each option and recommend one, naming which lens you weighted highest and why.
2. **Civic.** A Geelong council debates a bypass that cuts traffic but crosses a wetland. Write a 70-word brief listing one positive and one negative under each lens. End with a question the council should answer before deciding.
3. **Vocational.** You are an environmental consultant advising a Latrobe Valley solar farm. Suggest two design changes that improve social and ecological outcomes without making the project unviable.
4. **Ethical.** A vaccine works but is expensive. The state can buy it for high-risk groups only or fewer doses for everyone. Argue for one option using the four lenses, then state what would change your mind.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A new mine on Eastern Maar Country promises jobs and royalties but threatens a culturally significant site. Suggest a process that makes the trade-offs explicit and identify one outcome that could be a deal-breaker.

## Memorable takeaways
- Every real decision costs something somewhere.
- Use all four lenses, not just the loudest.
- List stakeholders before listing answers.
- Name your weights to keep debate honest.
- Showing the working beats announcing the answer.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Science gives us the right answer." No. Science narrows the options. Humans choose between trade-offs.
- "If it is good for the environment, the cost does not matter." No. Cost can decide whether a project is built at all.
- "Trade-offs mean someone is being unfair." No. Trade-offs are unavoidable. Fairness is in how they are decided and shared.
- "Whoever pays the most decides." No. Many decisions require legal, ethical and community consent regardless of cost.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *A small daily trade-off.* Sleeping in versus a healthier breakfast. You gain rest, you lose set-up time.
2. *Why use all four lenses?* A single lens hides costs and benefits. Using all four makes hidden effects visible and reduces nasty surprises later.
3. *Wind-farm stakeholders near Ararat.* Local farmers and households, Eastern Maar Traditional Owners, the energy retailer, council, birdlife, future grid users.
4. *Opportunity cost of iPads over a lab upgrade.* Lab equipment that students cannot use for chemistry and biology in years to come. Useful learning trades for different useful learning.
5. *Honest disagreement vs wrong.* Honest disagreement names different weights on shared facts. "Wrong" usually means the facts are mistaken, not the priorities.
6. *Why show the working?* Others can challenge it, improve it and trust it. Hidden decisions invite suspicion.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *EV vs hybrid family car.* EV: low local emissions and fuel cost, higher upfront cost and reliance on grid mix. Hybrid: cheaper now, ongoing fuel costs and emissions. If you weight environment highest in a household with home solar, recommend EV. State that financial pressure could shift the answer.
2. *Geelong wetland bypass.* Ethical: community time saved versus species harm. Environmental: less congestion emissions, but wetland loss. Social: faster commutes, but split neighbourhoods. Economic: jobs in build, ongoing maintenance. Question for council: can a tunnel or alternate route deliver most of the time saving without the wetland loss?
3. *Latrobe solar farm.* Add native ground cover and sheep grazing under panels to keep soil and farm income. Run a community ownership share so locals see ongoing benefit, not only construction jobs.
4. *Expensive vaccine.* Argue for high-risk groups first because it likely saves the most lives per dose, with planned wider rollout as price falls. Change your mind if evidence shows herd immunity needs broad coverage to work.
5. *Mine on Eastern Maar Country.* Process: free, prior and informed consent led by Eastern Maar Aboriginal Corporation, with independent cultural and environmental assessments, public reporting and a community benefit agreement. Deal-breaker: any plan that would damage the culturally significant site irreversibly.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Decision: managed retreat from a Bass Coast erosion zone.* Options: rock revetment vs staged retreat with land swap. Stakeholders: residents, council, insurers, Bunurong Land Council, future buyers. Four-lens table: rock wall scores high on short-term economic and social comfort, low on long-term environment and ethics; staged retreat scores high on environment and ethics, lower on short-term social acceptance. Recommendation: staged retreat with strong support package, weighting long-term environmental and ethical effects.
