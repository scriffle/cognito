# VC2S8I04 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S8I04. Organise and process data by constructing appropriate representations such as tables, graphs, keys and models.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 8, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The right representation reveals the pattern.
  - Tables, graphs, keys and models each show different things.
  - Processing data turns numbers into information.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **organise** and **process** data by **constructing** appropriate representations such as tables, graphs, keys and models.
- You are learning to choose the right representation for the question you are asking.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **variable**, **categorical** and **continuous** in your own words.
- Choose between a bar chart, a line graph and a scatter plot for a given dataset.
- Build a simple dichotomous key.
- Describe what a model adds beyond a table or graph.

## Core content

**1. Tables tidy raw data.** A table is your first stop. Rows are usually trials or samples, columns are variables, and headings include units. A tidy table makes errors visible. *Quick check:* Why should the first column usually be the independent variable?

**2. Bar charts vs line graphs vs scatter plots.** Use a **bar chart** for **categorical** data, like surface type. Use a **line graph** for a **continuous** independent variable measured at set intervals, like temperature over time. Use a **scatter plot** when both variables are continuous and you want to see a relationship. *Quick check:* Which graph fits "mass added vs spring extension"?

**3. Axes, units and scales matter.** Always label both axes with the variable name and unit. Choose a scale that fills the plot without cutting off data. Avoid broken scales that exaggerate small differences. *Quick check:* Why can a graph with a non-zero y-axis mislead?

**4. Worked example: plant growth across light levels.** Independent: light intensity (lux). Dependent: average height (cm) after 14 days. Three trials per condition. Step 1: tidy table with means and ranges. Step 2: scatter plot with a line of best fit. Step 3: a simple model: "Height increases with light up to about 8000 lux, then plateaus". *Follow the thinking:* table → graph → pattern → model.

**5. Keys for living things.** A **dichotomous key** is a series of yes-or-no questions that lead to an identification. Used widely in field biology, including by Parks Victoria rangers. Build keys with clear, observable features only. *Quick check:* Why is "looks like a typical leaf" a poor key step?

**6. Models simplify and predict.** A **model** is a simplified version of a system used to explain or predict. A water-cycle diagram, a particle model of matter and a population graph are all models. They make trade-offs between detail and clarity. *Quick check:* Name one thing a particle model of solids, liquids and gases simplifies.

**7. Process before you plot.** Common processing steps include calculating means, ranges, percentages and rates. Do these in the table before graphing. Highlight any cell that looks like an outlier. *Quick check:* What is the rate when 60 seeds germinate from 80 over 7 days?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Bureau of Meteorology Australia and EPA Victoria publish open datasets that students can graph in spreadsheets. Examples include Melbourne air-quality and rainfall records back to the late 1800s.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal seasonal calendars are themselves a model. The Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung calendar describes seven seasons defined by indicators on Country, such as the flowering of silver wattle. The Bureau of Meteorology has published Indigenous Weather Knowledge calendars in partnership with communities. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant home gardeners across Melbourne keep informal but powerful records of crop yields by month. Vietnamese growers in Springvale, Maltese growers in Sunshine and Sudanese growers in Footscray hold yearly logs that work as small datasets, useful for community planning.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You time how long ice cubes take to melt at five room temperatures. Choose a graph type and sketch the axes you would use, with units.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Hobsons Bay records monthly bay water temperatures. They want a graph that shows the seasonal pattern. Recommend a chart type and one feature you would highlight.
3. **Vocational.** A nurse logs patient pulse rates at admission, 30 minutes and 60 minutes for ten patients. Recommend the table layout and which graph would best show changes per patient over time.
4. **Ethical.** A class survey asks about household income to graph against grades. Identify the issue and suggest a fairer alternative.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung rangers and your class want to share findings on flowering dates with the local primary school. Choose a representation that suits a Year 3 audience and explain why.

## Memorable takeaways
- Table first, then graph.
- Bar for categories. Line for time. Scatter for relationships.
- Label everything, units and all.
- Keys use observable features.
- Models simplify on purpose, not by accident.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "The independent variable is ___, which is [categorical/continuous]. The best chart type is ___ because ___."
- **Words to keep close.** variable, categorical, continuous, axis, scale, model, key, outlier.
- **Try this picture.** Sketch three small chart types (bar, line, scatter) on one page and label one example you would use for each from your last experiment.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Photograph your tidy table and annotate the columns and units with arrows, instead of writing a paragraph.

## Stretch yourself
Take a public dataset from the Bureau of Meteorology Australia or EPA Victoria and build one good chart and one bad chart of the same data. Annotate why the bad one misleads and why the good one is honest. 200 words or one annotated double figure.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More chart types means more impressive." No. The best representation is the one that fits the data.
- "Bar charts work for everything." No. They hide trends in continuous data.
- "Models are guesses." No. Models are deliberate simplifications used to predict and explain.
- "Outliers should be deleted." No. Investigate them. They may reveal a fault or a real signal.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why is the independent variable usually first column?* It is what you set, so reading left to right matches cause to effect, and graphs are easier to plot.
2. *Mass added vs spring extension.* Scatter plot with a line of best fit, since both variables are continuous and the relationship matters.
3. *Why a non-zero y-axis can mislead.* Small differences look huge when the bottom of the scale is cropped, exaggerating change that is not really there.
4. *Why "looks like a typical leaf" is a poor key step.* It is not observable in a clear yes-or-no way and depends on opinion.
5. *One thing a particle model simplifies.* It treats particles as identical spheres with simple motion, ignoring real shapes, charges and forces.
6. *Germination rate.* 60 of 80 over 7 days = 75% over 7 days, or roughly 10.7% per day, or 8.6 seeds per day.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Ice melt at room temperatures.* Scatter plot. X axis: room temperature in °C. Y axis: melt time in seconds. Five points with a trend line.
2. *Bay water temperature.* Line graph of monthly mean temperature, X axis months in order, Y axis temperature in °C. Highlight the warmest month and add a 5-year average for context.
3. *Patient pulse log.* Table with rows per patient and columns for admission, 30 min and 60 min. Best graph: small line graphs for each patient (sparklines or paired-line chart) so individual changes are visible.
4. *Income and grades survey.* Issue: privacy and stigma. Alternative: collect optional postcode and use it only at aggregate level, or replace with a school-resource survey.
5. *Flowering dates for Year 3.* A simple month-by-month picture calendar with one drawing per month and a colour band for the season. Suits the audience and tells the story without numbers.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Dataset: Melbourne mean monthly maximum temperatures, 2014–2024.* Good chart: line graph with months on the X axis, temperature on the Y axis starting at 0, two lines for the first and last year. Annotation explains the warming visible in summer months. Bad chart: 3D bar chart with cropped axis from 22°C to 28°C, exaggerating the difference. Caption explains what is hidden by the crop.
