## What you are learning
- You are learning to **draw logical conclusions** based on **data and information** from **verified sources**.
- You are learning to make conclusions that earn belief.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **conclusion**, **logic**, **inference** and **assumption** in your own words.
- Trace a conclusion from data to claim with clear reasoning.
- Identify two assumptions behind a conclusion.
- State limits and what would change your view.

## Core content

**1. What a conclusion is.** A **conclusion** is the claim your evidence and reasoning support. It is the answer to your research question, stated clearly. *Quick check:* Why is "and then I had some thoughts" not a conclusion?

**2. From data to claim: logic.** Logic is the chain of reasoning that connects data to claim. Strong conclusions use **inference** (drawing the most plausible explanation), name **assumptions** behind the chain, and acknowledge **alternative explanations**. *Quick check:* What is one assumption behind "wages have risen, so workers are better off"?

**3. Verified sources.** Verified sources have passed evaluation on authority, currency, method, independence and verifiability (VC2HE10S06). Conclusions built on unverified sources are fragile. *Quick check:* Why is "I saw it on a forum" a poor base for a conclusion?

**4. Worked example: drawing a conclusion on youth wage growth.** Data: ABS wage-price index for 15–24 age group, 2019–2024; ABS CPI for the same period. Trend: wages grew, but slower than CPI in 2022–23. Conclusion: real youth wages fell over part of the period, with limits (sector variation, sample size). Reasoning: nominal wage growth − inflation = real change; if real change is negative, purchasing power fell. Alternatives: industry mix change, hours worked. *Follow the thinking:* data → reasoning → conclusion → limits → alternatives.

**5. Avoiding common errors.** Overclaiming (the data prove); cherry-picking (selecting only supporting evidence); missing limits; ignoring alternatives. Honest conclusions name what they do not show. *Quick check:* Why is "and that is why X" too quick a phrase in many cases?

**6. What would change your view.** Strong analysis names the evidence that would change your conclusion. This makes you credible and helps others test. *Quick check:* Why does naming "what would change my view" earn trust?

**7. Communicating conclusions.** State the conclusion in plain English with one or two key numbers and a cited source. Limit jargon. *Quick check:* What is one common mistake in writing conclusions?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABS, RBA, the Productivity Commission and the Auditor-General publish conclusions in published reports. Reading their structures models good practice.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Conclusions about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander outcomes should respect community context and avoid deficit framing. AIATSIS ethics guidelines apply. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Community-level conclusions should engage with first-hand voices from multilingual community media and councils rather than relying solely on mainstream sources.

## Memorable takeaways
- Conclusion = supported claim.
- Logic bridges data and claim.
- Verified sources before conclusions.
- Name limits and alternatives.
- "What would change my view" earns trust.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one current dataset. Draw a conclusion. State the reasoning, limits, two alternative explanations, and the evidence that would change your view. Cite sources. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "The data prove." Data support; conclusions need reasoning.
- "If most studies agree, it is settled." Often, but check methodology.
- "Naming limits weakens conclusions." It actually strengthens trust.
- "Alternative explanations are distractions." They are part of honest reasoning.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"And then I had some thoughts" not a conclusion.* It does not state a claim or attach evidence and reasoning.
2. *Assumption in "wages rose, workers better off".* Assumes wages outpaced inflation and that distribution did not worsen.
3. *Why "saw it on a forum" is poor.* Unverified authorship, method and currency; cannot be checked.
4. *Why "and that is why X" is too quick.* It implies causation without checking mechanism or alternatives.
5. *Why "what would change my view" earns trust.* Shows openness to evidence and self-awareness.
6. *Common mistake in conclusions.* Restating the data without making a claim.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Rents have stabilised.* Check the source; check the time window; compare with rent vs income; ask which suburbs.
2. *Wyndham improvement claim.* Period covered; comparison group; underemployment vs unemployment; participation rate.
3. *Junior policy adviser draft.* Headline claim; key numbers cited; limits stated; one recommendation.
4. *Programme "fixed" the issue.* Issues: confounding and selection effects. Use comparison groups; transparent methodology.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian counselling pilot.* Outcome data before and after; participant interviews; comparison with non-participant peers.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Dataset: ABS youth wage-price index and CPI, 2019–2024.* Conclusion: real youth wages fell for part of 2022–23. Reasoning: nominal growth minus inflation. Limits: sector variation, sample size. Alternatives: industry mix change, hours worked.
