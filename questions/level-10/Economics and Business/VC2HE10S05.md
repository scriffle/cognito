# VC2HE10S05 — Economics and Business

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HE10S05. Develop and evaluate a response to an economic and business issue, using cost-benefit analysis or criteria.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Economics and Business (Evaluating, concluding and decision-making).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A response is a plan, not a wish.
  - Cost-benefit and criteria make trade-offs explicit.
  - Evaluation reveals what works for whom.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Numeracy; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop and evaluate a response** to an **economic and business issue**, using **cost-benefit analysis or criteria**.
- You are learning to design and judge real responses.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **cost-benefit analysis**, **multi-criteria analysis**, **stakeholder** and **net effect** in your own words.
- Build a simple cost-benefit table for one response.
- Use three criteria to compare two responses.
- Justify a recommended response with limits stated.

## Core content

**1. Developing a response.** A **response** is a specific plan addressing an issue. Components: objective, target group, actions, resources, timeline, measures. Vague responses fail; specific responses get tested. *Quick check:* Why is "raise awareness" rarely enough?

**2. Cost-benefit analysis (CBA).** **CBA** lists the costs and benefits of a response, often across time and stakeholders. Quantify where you can; describe where you cannot. State assumptions. *Quick check:* Why are non-money costs and benefits part of CBA?

**3. Multi-criteria analysis.** When values matter more than dollars, **multi-criteria analysis** scores options on factors like impact, feasibility, equity, sustainability, risk. Choose criteria, agree weights, score, summarise. *Quick check:* When is multi-criteria better than CBA?

**4. Worked example: a small-business support package in Greater Geelong.** Issue: rising small-business closures. Two responses: (A) a one-off grant; (B) ongoing mentoring with a small grant. CBA: A has higher immediate cost; B has lower one-off cost but ongoing administrative cost. Criteria: impact (B may have longer-lasting effect); feasibility (A is faster); equity (B reaches more diverse businesses); sustainability (B builds capability). Recommendation: B, with monitoring and a sunset clause. *Follow the thinking:* issue → options → CBA → criteria → recommendation → limits.

**5. Stakeholders.** Different stakeholders see different costs and benefits. Name them and consult where possible. A response that ignores a stakeholder group often fails in implementation. *Quick check:* Who is one easily-forgotten stakeholder?

**6. Evaluating responses.** After implementation, evaluate against the measures set at design. Did the response meet its goals? At what cost? Who benefited? Honest evaluation feeds the next cycle. *Quick check:* Why is "measures set at design" the key?

**7. Limits and ethics.** Honest analysis names what the response cannot do, who pays and how distribution works. Ethical responses respect community context and avoid unintended harms. *Quick check:* Why is "no unintended effects" almost never true?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Department of Treasury and Finance Victoria publishes cost-benefit analysis frameworks. The Auditor-General reviews delivered programs. Local councils run CBA for community projects.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Responses affecting Aboriginal communities should consider cultural and community impacts. Closing the Gap targets and the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria provide context. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual community organisations and ethnic community councils help evaluate community-specific responses.

3. **Vocational.** A junior policy analyst drafts a small-business response. Outline three criteria for assessment.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates a "cash-for-attendance" school policy. Identify two arguments on each side.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Sudanese-Australian community proposes a financial-counselling pilot. Outline three culturally responsive evaluation criteria.

## Memorable takeaways
- Response = specific plan.
- CBA quantifies; criteria broadens.
- Stakeholders see different impacts.
- Measure at design, evaluate after.
- Honest analysis names limits.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Add it up and the answer is obvious." Weights matter.
- "If most benefit, it is justified." Distribution still matters.
- "Unintended effects can be ignored." They often dominate over time.
- "Specific responses lack ambition." Specific responses get tested and improved.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "raise awareness" rarely enough.* Without a specific action, audience and measure, awareness does not translate to outcomes.
2. *Non-money costs/benefits in CBA.* Wellbeing, time, equity and environment shape real outcomes; ignoring them misleads.
3. *When multi-criteria beats CBA.* When values matter more than dollar comparison; when CBA cannot capture key effects.
4. *Easily forgotten stakeholder.* Frontline workers who deliver the response; affected community members not in formal channels.
5. *Why measures-at-design are key.* Allow honest evaluation; reduce post-hoc rationalisation.
6. *Why "no unintended effects" is almost never true.* Complex systems respond in ways not all predicted.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Heat pump vs insulation.* Cost: upfront. Benefit: comfort, bills, emissions. Time horizon: 10+ years. Net: depends on household energy use and climate.
2. *Hume youth-business grant CBA.* Cost: grants and administration. Benefit: new businesses, jobs, learning, community benefit. Time: 1–5 years.
3. *Small-business response criteria.* Impact, feasibility, equity.
4. *Cash-for-attendance debate.* For: short-term attendance lift. Against: motivation crowding-out and equity. Both sides agree on need for better support services.
5. *Sudanese-Australian pilot criteria.* Cultural appropriateness; community ownership; measured outcomes.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: Victorian small-business closures.* Option A: one-off cash grant (CBA). Option B: mentoring plus grant (multi-criteria). Recommendation: B, with monitoring, sunset clause, and equity-targeted communication. Sources cited.
