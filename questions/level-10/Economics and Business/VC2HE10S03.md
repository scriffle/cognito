## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate and use** data and information to **address economic, business, work or financial issues** through **trends and cause-and-effect relationships**.
- You are learning to do real analysis, not just describe charts.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **trend**, **correlation**, **causation** and **confounder** in your own words.
- Describe a trend with direction, magnitude, timeframe and uncertainty.
- Distinguish correlation from causation in an economic example.
- Identify one confounding variable in a real case.

## Core content

**1. Reading a trend properly.** State direction (up, down, flat), magnitude (how much), timeframe (over what period) and uncertainty (range, confidence). Always cite the source. *Quick check:* Why is "the line went up" weak?

**2. Correlation.** **Correlation** is the degree to which two variables move together. Positive: same direction. Negative: opposite directions. Zero: no relationship. Strength varies. *Quick check:* What does a strong negative correlation between interest rates and consumer spending suggest?

**3. Causation.** **Causation** requires a mechanism plus evidence that change in one variable drives change in the other. Many economic correlations have causes; some do not. *Quick check:* Why is "if A then B" not enough for causation?

**4. Confounding.** A **confounder** is a third variable that affects both A and B, creating apparent correlation without direct causation. Spotting confounders is part of evaluation. *Quick check:* What is a likely confounder in an apparent correlation between ice-cream sales and drownings?

**5. Worked example: house prices and interest rates.** Data: Australian capital-city house prices and the cash rate, 2010–2024. Correlation: often negative (lower rates, higher prices) but with significant variation. Causation: lower borrowing costs increase borrowing power; investor sentiment also matters. Confounders: government policy, supply, demographics, foreign capital. Conclusion: interest rates influence house prices, but they are not the only driver. *Follow the thinking:* observe trend → check correlation → propose cause → name confounders → state conclusion with limits.

**6. Using evidence to address an issue.** Strong analysis: states the issue; presents the data; describes the trend; proposes the most plausible cause; names alternatives and limits; offers a recommendation. *Quick check:* Why does naming alternatives strengthen recommendations?

**7. Communicating analysis.** Use clear language; cite sources; pair charts with narrative; avoid overclaiming. *Quick check:* Why is "the data prove" usually overclaiming?

## Memorable takeaways
- Direction, magnitude, timeframe, uncertainty.
- Correlation hints; cause needs mechanism.
- Confounders mislead the unwary.
- Name alternatives and limits.
- Cite sources by name and date.

## Stretch yourself

## Watch out for these traps
- "Correlation = cause." Not without mechanism.
- "If it worked once, it works always." Replication and context matter.
- "Models give the answer." They give one answer; sensitivity matters.
- "Recent change = trend." Short-term moves are often noise.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "line went up" is weak.* Missing direction context, magnitude, timeframe and uncertainty.
2. *Strong negative correlation, rates and spending.* When rates rise, spending falls; consistent with rate-effect on disposable income and confidence.
3. *Why "if A then B" is not causation.* Many other factors could explain B; A might not be the cause.
4. *Ice-cream and drownings confounder.* Hot weather drives both.
5. *Why naming alternatives strengthens recommendations.* It shows you have considered other explanations and supports your final choice.
6. *Why "the data prove" overclaims.* Data support; claims need reasoning and limits.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Youth unemployment chart.* Check source and method; describe the trend properly; identify possible causes and confounders.
2. *Frankston youth-employment programme.* Pre-and-post outcome data; comparison group data; cost-effectiveness analysis.
3. *Inflation and wages confounders.* Energy prices; exchange rate; labour-supply changes.
4. *"It worked" claim.* Issues: cherry-picking and confounding. Use comparison group; transparent methodology.
5. *Community initiative claim.* Outcome data before and after; comparison with similar communities; participant interviews.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Dataset: Victorian wage-price index and CPI, 2019–2024.* Trend: wages grew slower than CPI for most of 2022–23. Correlation: yes. Cause hypothesis: post-pandemic supply shocks. Confounders: energy prices and labour-market settings. Recommendation: monitor real wages going forward and target policy at low-income households. Sources cited.
