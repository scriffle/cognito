# VC2HE10S06 — Economics and Business

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HE10S06. Evaluate sources of data and information to determine authenticity and validity.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Economics and Business (Evaluating, concluding and decision-making).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Authenticity is "is it really from this author".
  - Validity is "does it measure what it claims".
  - Both matter for trust.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate sources** of data and information to determine **authenticity** and **validity**.
- You are learning to grade sources before using them.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **authenticity**, **validity**, **methodology** and **provenance** in your own words.
- Apply five criteria to evaluate a source.
- Distinguish authenticity issues from validity issues.
- Decide which sources to trust for a specific claim.

## Core content

**1. Authenticity.** **Authenticity** is whether the source is really what it claims to be: a real author, a real organisation, a real date. Scams and impersonation break authenticity. *Quick check:* Why is checking the URL part of authenticity?

**2. Validity.** **Validity** is whether the source measures what it claims to measure. A genuine survey is authentic; a poorly designed survey may still be invalid for the question. *Quick check:* What is one example of an authentic but invalid source?

**3. Five criteria revisited.** Authority, currency, method, independence, verifiability. Score each and decide. Even strong sources have limits; weaker sources can be useful with caveats. *Quick check:* Which criterion targets validity most directly?

**4. Worked example: assessing a "cost-of-living" claim.** Claim: "Australians can't afford rent". Source A: a real-estate-agent blog with anecdotes. Source B: ABS rental-data release. Source C: a peer-reviewed paper on housing affordability. Source D: an anonymous social-media post with a chart. Authenticity: A, B, C pass; D fails (no author, no source). Validity: A is weak (small sample, conflict of interest); B is strong for general trends; C is strong for analysis; D unknown. *Follow the thinking:* check authenticity → check validity → match to claim → use with limits.

**5. Spotting fakes and AI-generated content.** Fakes mimic authenticity through design and tone. Check: domain spelling; author bios; cross-references in other reliable sources; reverse-image search. AI-generated content can look polished but may not be authentic or valid. *Quick check:* Why is "looks professional" not enough?

**6. Communicating evaluation.** When you use a source, briefly note its authenticity and validity in your analysis. Readers should not need to redo your verification. *Quick check:* What is one short sentence form for documenting evaluation?

**7. Limits of evaluation.** No evaluation is perfect. Time, language and access limit what you can check. Honest analysis names what you could and could not verify. *Quick check:* Why is "I verified everything" usually overstating?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABS, RBA, the Productivity Commission and the Auditor-General publish documented methodology. ABC Fact Check evaluates contested claims. Universities publish peer-reviewed work.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Authentic First Nations voices come through published, community-approved channels (NITV, IndigenousX, the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria, AIATSIS). Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual media (SBS Radio in 60+ languages) and community councils provide authentic community voices; validity checks still apply.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Real-looking = authentic." Fakes can look real.
- "Authentic = valid." A real survey can still be poorly designed.
- "All AI content is fake." Some AI-assisted content has authentic provenance; check.
- "Anonymous = unbiased." Anonymity removes accountability.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *URL and authenticity.* Domain spelling and presence of https; impersonation often shows up in URL.
2. *Authentic but invalid example.* A genuine survey with a tiny sample, leading questions, or misleading wording.
3. *Criterion targeting validity.* Methodology.
4. *Why "looks professional" is not enough.* Design is cheap; method and provenance are what matter.
5. *Documentation sentence.* "ABS Retail Trade, June 2024, accessed [date]: authentic; valid for monthly turnover; limit: not seasonally adjusted in raw series."
6. *Why "I verified everything" overstates.* Time, language and access limit what is possible.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Viral economic claim.* Authenticity: who, where, when, URL. Validity: method, sample, conflict of interest, peer support.
2. *Wyndham community submission.* Author and organisation; method behind any data; representative of community.
3. *Junior researcher and stakeholder report.* Authentic source? Conflict of interest? Method and data quality?
4. *AI-generated content.* Issues: hallucination and unverifiable claims. Use only as an aid; verify all factual claims through real sources.
5. *Multilingual community survey.* Author/community organisation; consent and sampling; cross-check with ABS data.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Claim: "Australian wages have outpaced inflation".* Sources: ABS wage-price index (authentic, valid for measure of nominal wages); ABS CPI (authentic, valid for inflation measure); a finance-blog post (authentic but with conflict); a viral social-media chart (unverified). Final use: ABS data with stated time window and method. Sources cited.
