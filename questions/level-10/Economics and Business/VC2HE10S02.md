# VC2HE10S02 — Economics and Business

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HE10S02. Locate, select, organise and analyse relevant information and data from a range of sources.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Economics and Business (Investigating).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Sources are graded, not equal.
  - Organisation makes analysis possible.
  - Analysis combines numbers and narrative.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Digital Literacy; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **locate, select, organise and analyse** relevant information and data from a **range of sources**.
- You are learning to build a working evidence base for your investigation.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **primary source**, **secondary source**, **data**, **information** and **analysis** in your own words.
- Use five criteria to evaluate a source.
- Organise data in a spreadsheet ready for analysis.
- Combine quantitative and qualitative evidence in a short analysis.

## Core content

**1. Locating sources.** Economic and business sources include ABS, RBA, ATO, Productivity Commission, Treasury, Auditor-General, ACCC, AEMO, industry bodies, NGOs, peer-reviewed articles, news and community-language media. Start with primary sources where possible. *Quick check:* Why is starting with primary sources usually better than starting with news?

**2. Selecting sources.** Apply five criteria: authority, currency, method, independence, verifiability. Discard sources that fail badly. Mark sources you keep with notes on strength and limits. *Quick check:* Which criterion is most often forgotten by Year 10 students?

**3. Organising data.** Use a spreadsheet with consistent columns: date, value, units, source, notes. One observation per row. Save raw and cleaned versions. Add a "source-list" tab with full citations. *Quick check:* Why save raw and cleaned versions separately?

**4. Worked example: investigating Victorian retail trade.** Question: How has Victorian retail trade changed between 2019 and 2024? Sources: ABS Retail Trade release (primary); RBA Statement on Monetary Policy (secondary commentary); a peer-reviewed article on consumer behaviour during COVID-19 (secondary); a Victorian industry report (secondary). Organise data by month; calculate year-on-year changes; visualise as a line graph. Combine quantitative (graph) with qualitative (narrative on behaviour change). *Follow the thinking:* identify the question → locate sources → score → organise → visualise → narrate.

**5. Triangulation.** **Triangulation** uses multiple independent sources on the same point. Agreement strengthens confidence; disagreement reveals contested ground. Disagreement is often a clue, not a problem. *Quick check:* What does it usually mean if a news article and ABS data conflict?

**6. Quantitative plus qualitative.** Numbers tell part of the story; narrative explains what happened and why. Strong analysis uses both. *Quick check:* What is one thing a chart cannot show alone?

**7. Citation and ethics.** Cite by author, year and where accessed. Use a consistent style. Quote sparingly; paraphrase carefully; respect copyright. *Quick check:* Why is "I found it online" not a citation?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** ABS provides Victorian splits in many series. The Victorian Department of Treasury and Finance and the Auditor-General publish accessible primary sources.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS ethics guidelines apply to research with First Nations communities. Data from IBA, Supply Nation and the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria provide primary evidence. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** ABS Census data by birthplace and language complements multilingual community media reports in tracking community economic experience.

## Memorable takeaways
- Start with primary sources.
- Score on five criteria.
- One row, one observation.
- Triangulate to verify.
- Quant plus qual together.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Wikipedia is unreliable." Useful as a starting point; check its references.
- "Numbers do not lie." They can mislead through framing, sampling and scale.
- "Recent is always best." Older primary data still matters for trends.
- "Anonymous = reliable." Anonymity removes accountability.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Primary before news.* Primary data is closer to the source and more reliable for the underlying claim.
2. *Most-forgotten criterion.* Methodology: students often take headline numbers without checking how they were produced.
3. *Raw vs cleaned versions.* Errors in cleaning can be reversed; lost raw data cannot.
4. *News and ABS conflict.* The news may interpret a different definition or time window; the ABS release is usually closer to the source.
5. *What charts cannot show.* Reasons people behave the way they do; qualitative context.
6. *"I found it online" not a citation.* Citations name author, year and access details so others can check.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Housing affordability.* REIV primary rent data; ABS Census housing data; Tenants Victoria report. Most authoritative depends on the specific claim.
2. *Whittlesea small business.* Council planning documents; ABS small-business statistics; ABR/ASIC business registers.
3. *Spreadsheet conventions.* Consistent headers with units; one source per row; save raw and cleaned files separately.
4. *Community-language survey ethics.* Consent of participants; community ownership of data; clarity on use.
5. *Sudanese-Australian small business.* ABS data by birthplace; council business-register; community-organisation reports.
