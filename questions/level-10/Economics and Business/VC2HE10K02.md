## What you are learning
- You are learning the **ways government and the Reserve Bank of Australia** intervene in the economy to **improve economic performance and living standards**.
- You are learning to see intervention as a toolkit with trade-offs.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **intervention**, **living standards** and **public good** in your own words.
- List five tools the Australian Government uses in the economy.
- Describe one RBA intervention and its purpose.
- Discuss one trade-off in intervention.

## Core content

**1. Why governments intervene.** Reasons include market failure (externalities, public goods), inequality, stability and supporting living standards. Pure markets do not deliver everything well. *Quick check:* Why is "leave it to the market" not enough for clean air?

**2. Government tools.** Fiscal policy (tax and spending); regulation (rules on competition, safety, environment); public services (health, education); transfer payments (welfare, pensions); ownership (publicly owned utilities); social insurance (Medicare, NDIS). *Quick check:* Which tool is Medicare?

**3. RBA interventions.** Sets the cash rate; supplies bank reserves; can provide emergency liquidity to banks; participates in foreign-exchange markets when needed. Independent from the government on monetary-policy decisions. *Quick check:* Why does RBA independence matter?

**4. Living standards.** **Living standards** include income but also housing, health, education, environment, safety, social connection. GDP measures output; the OECD Better Life Index, ABS Measures of Australia's Progress and HILDA Survey provide richer measures. *Quick check:* Why can GDP rise while living standards fall?

**5. Worked example: managing high inflation in 2022–24.** RBA: raised the cash rate to slow demand and inflation. Government: targeted cost-of-living relief; energy-bill assistance; competition policy. Different effects on mortgagors, renters and savers. Living standards measured across multiple dimensions, not only headline inflation. *Follow the thinking:* identify the problem → identify tools → trace effects → measure across dimensions.

**6. Trade-offs.** Lower inflation may come with slower employment growth; tax cuts may stimulate but increase deficits; new regulation may protect consumers but raise compliance costs. Honest analysis names these. *Quick check:* Why is "no trade-off" rare?

**7. Limits.** Some problems cannot be solved by intervention alone: structural change, global shocks, demographic shifts. Interventions reshape, rather than replace, markets. *Quick check:* What is one Australian global-shock example from the last decade?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian Government intervention includes school funding, public transport investment, hospital operation, planning rules and energy-transition programs. The Auditor-General reviews outcomes.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Specific intervention frameworks include Closing the Gap, the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria, IBA financing and procurement targets (Supply Nation). Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Settlement services, multilingual public-health and consumer information, and small-business grants for migrant entrepreneurs are part of inclusive intervention.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Government should stay out." Markets fail in many ways.
- "More intervention is better." More is not always better; design matters.
- "GDP captures wellbeing." It captures one dimension.
- "RBA controls the economy." It controls one major lever; other forces matter.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why clean air is not market-led.* Markets struggle to price what everyone shares; government rules and public funding step in.
2. *Medicare as tool.* Social insurance; pooled funding for universal health access.
3. *RBA independence.* Reduces political pressure on rate-setting; supports stable, longer-term policy.
4. *GDP up, living standards down.* Possible if growth is concentrated, inequality rises, or environmental harm grows.
5. *Trade-off example.* Tighter monetary policy may slow inflation but also slow employment growth.
6. *Australian global-shock example.* COVID-19; 2008 GFC; oil-price spikes in 2022.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *RBA decisions and households.* Mortgage repayments; rents; small-business borrowing costs; spending behaviour.
2. *Casey health investment.* Local jobs; better access for vulnerable groups; preventive-care savings.
3. *Cost-of-living relief options.* Targeted rebates; tax adjustments; regulated price caps in specific sectors; concession upgrades.
4. *Public utilities ownership debate.* For: long-term planning, accountability. Against: efficiency, innovation. Both sides can be addressed through hybrid models.
5. *Sudanese-Australian community grant pathways.* Local council grants; state community-grants programmes; federal multicultural and settlement funds.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Intervention: Victorian Government's Solar Homes programme.* Tool: subsidy plus regulation. Purpose: reduce emissions and household bills. Trade-offs: budget cost, possible market distortion. Alternative: feed-in-tariff reform. Sources cited.
