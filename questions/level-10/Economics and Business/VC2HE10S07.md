# VC2HE10S07 — Economics and Business

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HE10S07. Explain and present arguments about economics and business concepts and issues using subject-specific terminology, with reference to sources.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Economics and Business (Communicating).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Strong arguments link claim, evidence and reasoning.
  - Terminology used precisely earns trust.
  - Format follows audience.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Literacy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **explain and present arguments** about **economics and business concepts and issues** using **subject-specific terminology**, with **reference to sources**.
- You are learning to communicate as economists do.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **argument**, **claim**, **evidence**, **reasoning** and **counter-argument** in your own words.
- Build a four-part argument with citations.
- Choose format and tone for an audience.
- Defend an argument against one likely objection.

## Core content

**1. Four-part argument.** A solid argument has a **claim** (specific and testable), **evidence** (named sources, dated), **reasoning** (the mechanism linking them) and a **counter-argument** (the strongest opposing view fairly stated). *Quick check:* Why is engaging the counter-argument a strength, not a weakness?

**2. Subject-specific terminology.** Use precise terms: scarcity, opportunity cost, supply, demand, equilibrium, GDP, CPI, RBA, monetary policy, fiscal policy, competitive advantage, superannuation. Define on first use for non-expert audiences. *Quick check:* Why does precise vocabulary build credibility?

**3. References and citations.** Cite sources by author, year and where accessed. Use a consistent style. Cite every significant claim. *Quick check:* Why is "everyone knows" not a citation?

**4. Worked example: an argument on raising the minimum wage.** Claim: A targeted minimum-wage rise above CPI would improve real living standards for low-income workers without triggering significant unemployment. Evidence: Fair Work Commission annual review; ABS wage and CPI data; peer-reviewed Australian studies. Reasoning: low-income workers have a higher marginal propensity to consume; small rises align with productivity growth. Counter-argument: small-business employment may shrink in low-margin sectors. Response: targeted rise with sector-specific transition support. *Follow the thinking:* claim → evidence → reasoning → counter → response → conclusion with limits.

**5. Audience and format.** Choose by audience: parliamentary submission for policymakers; op-ed for general public; explainer video for peers; report for assessors. Plain English for general audiences; technical for experts. *Quick check:* What changes between writing for Year 8 peers and a Treasury audience?

**6. Visual support.** Charts, tables and infographics help. Label axes; cite sources on chart; pair visuals with narrative. *Quick check:* Why is "a chart without a caption" risky?

**7. Integrity and limits.** Cite sources; quote sparingly; paraphrase carefully; acknowledge limits; avoid overclaiming. Integrity beats persuasion in the long run. *Quick check:* What is one overclaiming phrase to avoid?

## Memorable takeaways
- Four-part argument: claim, evidence, reasoning, counter.
- Use precise terms.
- Cite by author, year, source.
- Audience and format together.
- Integrity beats persuasion.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Sounding neutral" means no view. Neutral framing avoids loaded words; you can still argue strongly.
- "Counter-arguments weaken you." Engaging strengthens you.
- "Long quotes prove the point." Use sparingly; reasoning is yours.
- "If it is on a polished slide deck, it must be right." Polish is not provenance.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Engaging counter-arguments.* It shows you have considered the strongest opposing view; strengthens credibility.
2. *Precise vocabulary builds credibility.* Signals careful thinking; lets reader follow exactly.
3. *Why "everyone knows" is not a citation.* It hides the source and is not checkable.
4. *Year 8 peers vs Treasury.* Peers need accessibility; Treasury needs precise terms, full citations, technical analysis.
5. *Chart without caption risky.* Reader does not know what to take away; risk of misreading.
6. *Overclaiming phrase.* "Always", "everyone", "the data prove", "guaranteed".

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Renters are doing better than owners".* Claim: depends on time horizon and asset position. Evidence: ABS housing data; HILDA wealth data. Reasoning: renters avoid maintenance but miss capital growth. Counter: owners can borrow against equity. Response: depends on individual situation.
2. *Yarra op-ed.* Intro with stakes; three evidenced claims; counter-argument considered; recommendation; sources cited.
3. *Wages-decision media kit.* Media release; fact sheet; brief explainer video. Each audience needs different tone and length.
4. *Strong opinion.* Issues: factual accuracy and respect for those affected. Cite sources; allow right of reply; avoid stereotypes.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian policy submission.* Translate key terms; use community-language case studies; co-design with community leaders.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: housing affordability.* Argument: targeted state investment in social housing improves living standards more than universal first-home grants for the same dollar. Evidence: ABS rental data; Productivity Commission analysis; an academic study. Counter: market-based stimulus boosts construction. Response: complementary policies; specific design matters. Formats: 600-word op-ed for general audiences; 200-word brief for the state Minister. Sources cited.
