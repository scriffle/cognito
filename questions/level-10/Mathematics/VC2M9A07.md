## Core content

**1. What are parameters?** A parameter is a constant in an equation that can be varied to produce a family of related functions. In y = a(x − h)² + k, the parameters are a, h and k. Changing each produces a different but related parabola. *Quick check:* In y = 2(x − 3)² + 1, identify the values of a, h and k.

**2. Vertical stretch and reflection (parameter a).** In y = af(x): |a| > 1 stretches the graph vertically (taller/narrower). 0 < |a| < 1 compresses vertically (flatter/wider). a < 0 reflects the graph in the x-axis (flips upside down). Example: y = 2x² is narrower than y = x²; y = −x² is reflected. *Quick check:* Describe the effect on y = x² when a changes from 1 to 3 to −1.

**3. Horizontal translation (parameter h).** In y = f(x − h): positive h shifts the graph RIGHT; negative h shifts LEFT. The sign is opposite to what you might expect — (x − 3) shifts right 3 because x = 3 makes the bracket zero. *Quick check:* y = (x − 4)² is shifted ___ units ___ from y = x².

**4. Vertical translation (parameter k).** In y = f(x) + k: positive k shifts UP; negative k shifts DOWN. The graph moves k units vertically. Example: y = x² + 5 is the standard parabola shifted up 5. *Quick check:* Compare y = 2ˣ with y = 2ˣ − 3.

**5. Combining transformations.** y = a(x − h)² + k combines stretch/reflection, horizontal shift and vertical shift. Order of reading: stretch by a, shift right h, shift up k. The vertex of a parabola in this form is at (h, k). Similar patterns apply to other functions: y = a√(x − h) + k, y = a/((x − h)) + k, y = a × 2^(x−h) + k. *Quick check:* What is the vertex of y = −2(x + 1)² + 5?

**6. Worked example — exploring with Desmos.**
Enter y = a(x − h)² + k in Desmos with sliders for a, h and k.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Set a = 1, h = 0, k = 0: standard parabola, vertex at origin.
- Slide a from 1 to 4: parabola narrows (stretches vertically).
- Slide a to −1: parabola flips (reflects in x-axis).
- Slide h from 0 to 3: parabola moves right 3 units.
- Slide k from 0 to −2: parabola moves down 2 units.
- Generalisation: a controls shape/direction, h controls horizontal position, k controls vertical position.
- Try with y = a × 2^(x−h) + k — same parameters have analogous effects on the exponential.

**7. Generalising patterns.** After experimenting, state generalisations: "For any function y = af(x − h) + k: a stretches/reflects vertically, h translates horizontally (opposite sign), k translates vertically (same sign)." These patterns hold for parabolas, exponentials, hyperbolas, square roots — the specific function shape changes but the transformation rules are universal. *Quick check:* Without graphing, predict where y = 3(x − 2)² − 4 has its vertex and whether it opens up or down.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Animation studios (like Melbourne-based studios working on films and games) transform functions to create motion paths — stretching, shifting and reflecting curves to animate characters. Architectural software parametrically adjusts building designs using exactly these transformation principles. VCE Mathematical Methods is built on understanding transformed functions. Desmos and GeoGebra are used in Victorian classrooms as standard tools.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Parametric variation — adjusting one element to observe its effect on the whole — mirrors experimental approaches in traditional land management (e.g. adjusting fire timing/intensity and observing effects on regrowth). The concept of systematically varying one factor while observing outcomes is a universal approach to building knowledge. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Digital tools for graphing are accessible to all students regardless of language background — visual learning through parameter exploration transcends language barriers. Students from all backgrounds can explore and discover mathematical patterns through dynamic interaction.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Open Desmos and enter y = a × sin(x). Use a slider for a (1 to 5). Describe what happens. Then add y = sin(x − h) with a slider for h. Describe the horizontal shift. Take screenshots showing your discoveries.
2. **Civic.** A council models a fountain's water arc as y = −a(x − h)² + k. If they want the water to go higher (increase maximum height), which parameter do they change? If they want to move the fountain's position, which parameter?
3. **Vocational.** A sound engineer adjusts an audio waveform y = a × sin(bx). What does changing a do to the volume? What does changing b do to the pitch (frequency)? Experiment in Desmos.
4. **Ethical.** A student says "I explored on Desmos and discovered that a always makes things bigger." Identify why this generalisation is incomplete (what about 0 < a < 1? What about negative a?).
5. **Cross-cultural.** Use GeoGebra to create a family of parabolas that form an arch pattern (like those in Islamic geometric architecture). Vary h to create multiple parabolas side by side. Screenshot your design and describe the parameter relationships.

## Memorable takeaways
- a: stretches (|a| > 1), compresses (|a| < 1), reflects (a < 0).
- h: shifts horizontally (OPPOSITE sign — (x−3) shifts RIGHT).
- k: shifts vertically (SAME sign — +5 shifts UP).
- These rules apply to ALL function families.
- Digital tools let you explore dynamically — slide and observe.

## Watch out for these traps
- **(x − 3) shifts RIGHT, not left.** The sign inside is opposite to the direction. (x + 2) = (x − (−2)) shifts LEFT 2.
- **Confusing stretch with shift.** The parameter a stretches/compresses; h and k shift position.
- **Assuming a > 1 always makes things "bigger."** For 0 < a < 1, the graph compresses. For a < 0, it reflects.
- **Forgetting that patterns are universal.** The same transformation rules apply to all function types — don't learn separate rules for each.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Sine exploration.* Increasing a increases the wave height (amplitude). Shifting h moves the wave left/right (phase shift). a = 3 makes peaks at 3 and troughs at −3.
2. *Fountain.* Higher: increase k (maximum height) or increase a (steeper, but also higher for same width). Move position: change h. Specifically, k directly controls maximum height.
3. *Sound engineer.* a = amplitude = volume (louder/quieter). b = frequency = pitch (higher b = higher pitch, more oscillations per unit). These are fundamental audio parameters.
4. *Incomplete generalisation.* When 0 < a < 1, the graph compresses (gets flatter/wider). When a < 0, it reflects. "Makes bigger" only applies to |a| > 1 with positive a.
5. *Islamic arches.* Vary h to place parabolas at x = 0, 3, 6, 9... with same a and k. Creates repeating arch pattern. Each arch: y = −a(x − nh)² + k for n = 0, 1, 2, 3...

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Hyperbola y = a/(x−h) + k: h shifts the vertical asymptote to x = h; k shifts the horizontal asymptote to y = k; a stretches the branches (larger |a| = branches further from asymptotes); negative a reflects branches to opposite quadrants. Square root y = a√(x−h) + k: domain restricted to x ≥ h (h controls the starting point); k shifts vertically; a stretches vertically and negative a reflects below x-axis. Universal generalisation: "For any function y = af(x−h) + k, the parameter a controls vertical stretch/reflection, h translates the graph horizontally (opposite sign direction), and k translates vertically. The asymptotes, vertices, or starting points all shift by (h, k). These rules are the same regardless of the function type."
