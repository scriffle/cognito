## Core content

**1. What is a network?** A **network** (or graph) is a collection of **vertices** (nodes/points) connected by **edges** (arcs/lines). Networks model connections between things — cities connected by roads, people connected by friendships, computers connected by cables. The shape and position of the drawing don't matter — only which vertices are connected to which. *Quick check:* A network has 4 vertices and 5 edges. Draw one possible version of this network.

**2. Degree of a vertex.** The **degree** of a vertex is the number of edges meeting it. In a friendship network, a person's degree is the number of their connections. The sum of all degrees in a network equals twice the number of edges (each edge contributes to two vertices). *Quick check:* A vertex has degree 3. What does this mean? If a network has vertices with degrees 2, 3, 3, 4, how many edges does it have?

**3. Connectedness.** A network is **connected** if there is a path between every pair of vertices — you can get from any vertex to any other by following edges. A **disconnected** network has at least two vertices with no path between them (it breaks into separate **components**). *Quick check:* A school has 5 classrooms. Doors connect rooms 1–2, 2–3, 3–4, and 4–5. Is the network connected? What if door 3–4 is locked?

**4. Paths, circuits and trees.** A **path** is a sequence of edges connecting two vertices without repeating a vertex. A **circuit** (cycle) is a path that starts and ends at the same vertex. A **tree** is a connected network with no circuits — it has exactly n−1 edges for n vertices. *Quick check:* Can a tree with 6 vertices have 6 edges?

**5. Practical network representations.** Transport networks: vertices = stations, edges = routes. Social networks: vertices = people, edges = relationships. Communication networks: vertices = devices, edges = connections. Supply chains: vertices = suppliers/factories/shops, edges = delivery routes. *Quick check:* Draw a network for Melbourne's train lines with 5 stations. What would "connected" mean for commuters?

**6. Worked example — analysing a delivery network.**
A courier delivers to 6 suburbs. The road connections are:
- A–B, A–C, B–C, B–D, C–E, D–E, D–F.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Draw the network: 6 vertices (A to F), 7 edges.
- Degrees: A = 2, B = 3, C = 3, D = 3, E = 2, F = 1. Sum = 14 = 2 × 7 ✓.
- Is it connected? Check: can we reach F from A? A→B→D→F ✓. All vertices reachable. Connected.
- Is F vulnerable? If edge D–F is removed, F is disconnected (degree 1 means only one connection).
- A vertex with degree 1 is called a **leaf** — removing its single edge disconnects it.

**7. Weighted and directed networks.** Some networks have **weights** on edges (distances, costs, times) or **directions** (one-way streets, data flow). A weighted network allows questions like "what is the shortest path?" A directed network (digraph) shows flow direction. *Quick check:* Give an example of a real-world directed network.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne's public transport network (train, tram, bus) is a classic network with stations as vertices and routes as edges — PTV uses network analysis to identify critical connections. The NBN rollout across Victoria was planned using network diagrams to optimise coverage. Electricity distribution from the Latrobe Valley uses network models to ensure all regions are connected. VicRoads models traffic flow on directed, weighted networks.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Songlines and trading routes form networks connecting communities across Country — vertices are significant places (water sources, meeting points, sacred sites) and edges are the paths between them. These networks enabled trade of goods (ochre, shells, tools) and knowledge across thousands of kilometres. The network's connectedness was maintained through obligations of reciprocity and shared ceremony. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Every migration story involves networks — family connections, community networks, trade routes. Students can map their own family's network of connections across countries. Social media networks are familiar to all students and demonstrate concepts of degree, connectedness and influential nodes.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Draw a network of your friendship group (you and 4 friends). Add an edge if two people know each other. What is your degree? Is the network connected? Who is most connected?
2. **Civic.** A regional council has 7 towns connected by roads. Draw a possible network. If a flood cuts one road, could some towns become disconnected? Which roads are critical (their removal disconnects the network)?
3. **Vocational.** An electrician wires 8 rooms. They need every room connected to the power source using minimum cable. Draw the network as a tree. How many cables are needed? Why is a tree the most efficient connected network?
4. **Ethical.** A social media company reveals that its network is "connected" — every user can reach every other through friend links. Discuss: does connectedness mean everyone is equally accessible? What does degree tell us about influence and reach?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research the historic Silk Road trade routes. Draw a simplified network with 6 cities as vertices. Is it connected? Identify which cities have the highest degree (most trade connections) and explain their historical importance.

## Memorable takeaways
- Network = vertices + edges.
- Degree of a vertex = number of edges meeting it.
- Sum of degrees = 2 × number of edges.
- Connected = path exists between every pair of vertices.
- Tree = connected network with no circuits (n−1 edges for n vertices).

## Stretch yourself
A disaster recovery team must connect 8 emergency shelters with communication cables. The possible connections and their costs (in $000s) are given as a weighted network. Find the **minimum spanning tree** — the cheapest way to connect all shelters. Use a systematic approach: start with the cheapest edge, add the next cheapest that doesn't create a circuit, repeat until all vertices are connected (Kruskal's algorithm). How many edges does your tree have? What is the total cost? Then: if one cable fails, which failure(s) would disconnect the network? Suggest where backup cables should be placed. Discuss why redundancy (extra edges beyond the tree) matters for critical infrastructure. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Confusing the drawing with the network.** Two different-looking diagrams can represent the same network — what matters is which vertices are connected, not the physical layout.
- **Miscounting degree.** A loop (edge from a vertex to itself) counts 2 towards that vertex's degree.
- **Assuming all networks are connected.** Always check — a network can have isolated vertices or separate components.
- **Confusing path and circuit.** A path visits each vertex at most once. A circuit returns to the start.

A note on certainty. The properties of networks (sum of degrees = 2 × edges, a tree with n vertices has n−1 edges, etc.) are mathematical theorems — provably true for all networks. Whether a real-world situation is accurately modelled by a particular network is a modelling choice — the mathematics is exact once the model is defined; the question is whether the model captures the relevant features of reality.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *4 vertices, 5 edges.* Many possibilities. One example: vertices A, B, C, D with edges AB, AC, AD, BC, BD (degrees: A=3, B=3, C=2, D=2).
2. *Degree 3 means 3 edges meet at that vertex.* Degrees 2+3+3+4 = 12 = 2 × edges, so 6 edges.
3. *5 classrooms.* Connected (path from any room to any other: e.g. 1→2→3→4→5). If door 3–4 locked: disconnected — rooms {1,2,3} cannot reach {4,5}.
4. *Tree with 6 vertices.* No — a tree has n−1 = 5 edges. Six edges would create a circuit.
5. *Train network.* Vertices = stations, edges = direct rail connections. "Connected" means commuters can travel between any two stations (possibly with changes). If disconnected, some stations are unreachable.
6. *Delivery network.* Worked through in example — connected, F is vulnerable (leaf node).
7. *Directed network.* Twitter follows (one-way), one-way streets, email (sender → receiver), food chains (predator → prey).

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Friendship network.* Answers vary. Degree = number of friends in the group who know each other. Connected if everyone can be linked through mutual friends. Most connected = highest degree.
2. *Regional council.* With 7 towns, minimum 6 edges needed for connectedness (tree). Critical roads are "bridges" — edges whose removal disconnects the network. Bridges can be identified by checking if removal creates two components.
3. *Electrician.* 8 rooms need 7 cables (tree: n−1 = 7). A tree is most efficient because any additional edge creates a redundant circuit — wasted cable without improving connectedness.
4. *Social media ethics.* Connectedness means a theoretical path exists, but high-degree nodes (influencers) are far more reachable than low-degree nodes. Network structure creates inequality of visibility even in a "connected" system.
5. *Silk Road.* Key cities: Constantinople, Baghdad, Samarkand, Kashgar, Xi'an, Delhi. Samarkand and Baghdad likely highest degree (central trading hubs). Their historical importance correlates with their network centrality — more connections meant more trade, wealth and cultural exchange.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** 8 shelters need 7 edges (tree). Kruskal's: sort edges by cost, add cheapest without creating circuits. Example costs might give total $45,000. Every edge in a tree is a bridge — its removal disconnects. To identify critical failures: every cable is critical (that's the nature of trees — no redundancy). Backup cables should be placed to create alternative paths for the most critical connections (those where disconnection would isolate the most shelters). Adding just 2–3 backup edges provides significant resilience. Real infrastructure (power grids, internet) uses redundancy beyond the minimum tree precisely because single-point failures are unacceptable for critical services.
