# VC2M9A01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9A01. Apply the exponent laws to numerical expressions with integer exponents and the zero exponent, and extend to variables.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Exponent laws are rules that simplify expressions involving powers — they apply to both numerical and algebraic expressions.
  - The zero exponent rule (a⁰ = 1) and negative exponent rule (a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ) follow logically from the other laws.
  - Mastering exponent laws is essential for working with algebra, exponential equations, and scientific notation.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **apply exponent laws** to simplify numerical and algebraic expressions involving integer exponents.
- You are learning why the zero exponent equals 1 and how negative exponents create fractions.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- State and apply the multiplication law (aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ).
- State and apply the division law (aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ).
- State and apply the power of a power law ((aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ).
- Apply the power of a product law ((ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ).
- Explain and use a⁰ = 1 and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.
- Extend all laws to algebraic expressions.

## Core content

**1. Multiplication law.** When multiplying powers with the same base, ADD the exponents: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ. Example: 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷ = 128. With variables: x⁵ × x³ = x⁸. The bases must be the same — you cannot simplify 2³ × 3⁴ using this law. *Quick check:* Simplify 5² × 5⁶.

**2. Division law.** When dividing powers with the same base, SUBTRACT the exponents: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ. Example: 3⁷ ÷ 3⁴ = 3³ = 27. With variables: y⁹ ÷ y² = y⁷. *Quick check:* Simplify 10⁸ ÷ 10³.

**3. Power of a power.** When raising a power to another power, MULTIPLY the exponents: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ. Example: (2³)⁴ = 2¹² = 4096. With variables: (x⁴)³ = x¹². *Quick check:* Simplify (7²)⁵.

**4. Power of a product and quotient.** (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ. Example: (3x)² = 9x². (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ. Example: (2/5)³ = 8/125. *Quick check:* Expand (2y)⁴.

**5. Zero exponent.** a⁰ = 1 (for any a ≠ 0). Why? Using the division law: a³ ÷ a³ = a⁰. But a³ ÷ a³ = 1. Therefore a⁰ = 1. Examples: 5⁰ = 1, x⁰ = 1, (3xy)⁰ = 1. *Quick check:* Evaluate 7⁰ + 3⁰ + 100⁰.

**6. Worked example — combining laws with variables.**
Simplify: (2x³y²)⁴ ÷ (4x⁵y).

*Follow the thinking:*
- Numerator: (2x³y²)⁴ = 2⁴ × x¹² × y⁸ = 16x¹²y⁸.
- Denominator: 4x⁵y = 4x⁵y¹.
- Divide: 16x¹²y⁸ ÷ 4x⁵y = 4x⁷y⁷.
- Check each component: 16÷4 = 4, x¹²⁻⁵ = x⁷, y⁸⁻¹ = y⁷. ✓

**7. Negative exponents.** a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (the reciprocal). Why? a³ × a⁻³ = a⁰ = 1, so a⁻³ = 1/a³. Examples: 2⁻³ = 1/8, x⁻² = 1/x². Conversely: 1/aⁿ = a⁻ⁿ. To simplify expressions with negative exponents, move factors between numerator and denominator, changing the sign of the exponent. *Quick check:* Write 3⁻⁴ as a fraction. Write 1/x⁵ with a negative exponent.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Scientific notation uses exponent laws extensively — CSIRO scientists express measurements from atomic scales (10⁻¹⁰ m) to astronomical distances (10²⁶ m). The Australian Synchrotron works with energies measured in powers of 10. Victorian VCE science requires fluent use of exponent laws for chemistry (molar calculations), physics (SI prefixes), and biology (cell sizes). Computer science (binary: powers of 2) is taught across Victorian schools.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The concept of repeated doubling (exponential growth) connects to ecological observations — population growth of species after fire, spread of plants after rain. Understanding orders of magnitude supports discussions about deep time — the 65,000+ year history of First Nations people involves quantities expressed in scientific notation when discussing archaeological evidence. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Every culture uses powers and large numbers — from ancient Indian mathematics (which named numbers up to 10⁵³) to modern computing. Scientific notation and exponent laws are universal tools of modern science and technology that students encounter regardless of background.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your phone storage is 128 GB = 2⁷ GB. Each photo takes 2³ MB. How many photos fit? Express your answer using exponent laws (noting 1 GB = 2¹⁰ MB).
2. **Civic.** Australia's national debt is approximately $9 × 10¹¹. The population is approximately 2.6 × 10⁷. Calculate the debt per person using exponent laws and division.
3. **Vocational.** A microbiologist counts bacteria doubling every 20 minutes. Starting with 2³ bacteria, how many after 2 hours (6 doublings)? Express as a power of 2.
4. **Ethical.** A student writes x⁰ = 0 because "anything times zero is zero." Explain why this is wrong and prove that x⁰ = 1 using the division law.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Archimedes estimated the number of grains of sand to fill the universe as approximately 10⁶³. Express this as a power. If each grain is 10⁻³ m, and you lined them up, what would the total length be? (Use exponent laws.)

## Memorable takeaways
- Multiply: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ (add exponents).
- Divide: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (subtract exponents).
- Power of power: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ (multiply exponents).
- Zero: a⁰ = 1 (any base, provided a ≠ 0).
- Negative: a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (flip to denominator).

## Watch out for these traps
- **Adding exponents when multiplying different bases.** 2³ × 3² ≠ 6⁵. The bases must be the same.
- **Confusing multiplication and power-of-power.** (x³)⁴ = x¹² (multiply), but x³ × x⁴ = x⁷ (add).
- **Thinking a⁰ = 0.** Remember: a⁰ = 1 for all a ≠ 0.
- **Wrong sign with negative exponents.** 2⁻³ = 1/8, not −8. Negative exponent means reciprocal, not negative number.
- **Forgetting to apply power to ALL factors.** (2xy)³ = 2³x³y³ = 8x³y³, not 2x³y³.

A note on certainty. The exponent laws are logical consequences of the definition of powers as repeated multiplication. They are not arbitrary rules to memorise — each can be derived from first principles. Once the definition is established (aⁿ means a multiplied n times), every law follows necessarily. The extensions to zero and negative exponents are definitions chosen to make the laws continue to work consistently.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Phone storage.* 128 GB = 2⁷ GB = 2⁷ × 2¹⁰ MB = 2¹⁷ MB. Each photo = 2³ MB. Photos = 2¹⁷ ÷ 2³ = 2¹⁴ = 16,384 photos.
2. *National debt per person.* (9 × 10¹¹) ÷ (2.6 × 10⁷) = (9/2.6) × 10⁴ ≈ 3.46 × 10⁴ = $34,600 per person.
3. *Bacteria.* Start: 2³. After 6 doublings: 2³ × 2⁶ = 2⁹ = 512 bacteria.
4. *Zero exponent proof.* x⁰ = xⁿ ÷ xⁿ = xⁿ⁻ⁿ = x⁰. But xⁿ ÷ xⁿ = 1. Therefore x⁰ = 1. The confusion arises from "zero power" sounding like "multiply by zero," but exponents mean repeated multiplication, not multiplication by the exponent.
5. *Archimedes.* 10⁶³ grains, each 10⁻³ m. Total length = 10⁶³ × 10⁻³ = 10⁶⁰ m.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** [(3x²y⁻³)² × (9x⁻¹y⁴)] ÷ (27x³y⁻²)⁰. Step 1: (27x³y⁻²)⁰ = 1 (zero exponent). Step 2: (3x²y⁻³)² = 9x⁴y⁻⁶ (power of product). Step 3: 9x⁴y⁻⁶ × 9x⁻¹y⁴ = 81x³y⁻² (multiplication law). Final: 81x³y⁻² ÷ 1 = 81x³y⁻² = 81x³/y². On 0⁰: undefined in most contexts (indeterminate form), though sometimes defined as 1 in combinatorics by convention. Proof: (a/b)⁻ⁿ = (a⁻ⁿ)/(b⁻ⁿ) = (1/aⁿ)/(1/bⁿ) = bⁿ/aⁿ = (b/a)ⁿ. 0.000064 = 6.4 × 10⁻⁵ = 64 × 10⁻⁶ = 2⁶ × 10⁻⁶. Check: 2⁶ = 64, so 64 × 0.000001 = 0.000064 ✓.
