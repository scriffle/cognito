## Core content

**1. Direct proportion and rates.** When y = kx, y is directly proportional to x. The constant k is often a **rate**: speed (km/h), wage ($/ hour), price ($/kg), density (g/cm³). If fuel costs $1.85/L, then cost = 1.85 × litres. The rate k = 1.85 links the two quantities. *Quick check:* Water flows at 12 litres per minute. How much flows in 45 minutes?

**2. Ratio.** A ratio compares quantities of the same kind. If concrete is mixed in the ratio 1:2:4 (cement:sand:gravel) and you need 2.1 m³ total, each part = 2.1/7 = 0.3 m³. Cement: 0.3, sand: 0.6, gravel: 1.2 m³. Ratios can be scaled up or down proportionally. *Quick check:* Share $450 in the ratio 2:3:4.

**3. Scale.** A scale links model/map measurements to real dimensions. Scale 1:25 000 means 1 cm on the map = 25 000 cm = 250 m in reality. For areas: square the scale factor. For volumes: cube it. A 1:100 architectural model has areas 1/10 000 of reality and volumes 1/1 000 000. *Quick check:* On a 1:50 000 map, two towns are 3.8 cm apart. What is the real distance?

**4. Financial modelling — unit pricing and best buys.** Compare prices by finding the cost per unit. Brand A: 750 g for $4.20 → $5.60/kg. Brand B: 1.2 kg for $6.00 → $5.00/kg. Brand B is the better buy per kg. Modelling assumes all else is equal (quality, waste). *Quick check:* Orange juice: 2 L for $5.40 or 3 L for $7.50. Which is better value?

**5. Financial modelling — currency exchange.** Exchange rates are direct proportion. If AUD 1 = USD 0.66, then converting $500 AUD gives 500 × 0.66 = USD 330. Going the other way: USD 200 ÷ 0.66 = AUD 303.03. Assumptions: no fees, rate stays constant. In reality, fees and spread apply. *Quick check:* AUD 1 = EUR 0.58. Convert AUD 1 200 to euros.

**6. Worked example: planning a community barbecue.**

A school plans a barbecue for 320 people. Experience says the ratio of sausages to burgers to vegetarian options is 5:3:2.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Total parts: 5 + 3 + 2 = 10.
- Each part: 320/10 = 32 servings.
- Sausages: 5 × 32 = 160. Burgers: 3 × 32 = 96. Vegetarian: 2 × 32 = 64.
- Sausages cost $1.20 each: 160 × $1.20 = $192. Burgers $2.50: 96 × $2.50 = $240. Veg options $1.80: 64 × $1.80 = $115.20.
- Total food cost: $547.20.
- Assumptions: everyone eats exactly one serving; the ratio from past events holds; no waste.
- Evaluation: in practice, order 10–15% extra for seconds and waste. Revised total ≈ $630.

**7. The modelling process.** (1) Formulate: identify the proportion/rate/ratio/scale relationship. (2) Solve: calculate using y = kx, ratio division, or scale conversion. (3) Interpret: translate the answer back to the context. (4) Evaluate: are the assumptions valid? Is the answer sensible? (5) Report: state methods, assumptions, findings and limitations. *Quick check:* You model a journey time as distance/speed. Name two assumptions this makes.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne households regularly compare unit prices at supermarkets — Coles and Woolworths are now required to display per-unit pricing. VicRoads uses scale drawings for road designs. Melbourne's public transport timetabling uses speed–time rates. The housing market reports price-per-square-metre as a comparison rate.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations trade networks across south-eastern Australia involved exchange ratios — quantities of resources like possum skins, stone axes and ochre were traded in understood proportional relationships. The scaling of community gathering sizes to food preparation demonstrates ratio reasoning. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Currency exchange is a daily reality for families sending remittances overseas. Recipe scaling for community events (Eid, Lunar New Year, Diwali gatherings) involves ratio and proportion. Market traders in Melbourne's Queen Victoria Market work with rates ($/kg) and best-buy comparisons constantly.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You earn $23.50 per hour at a weekend job. Calculate your pay for 6.5 hours. If you want to save $800 for a phone, how many hours must you work? Model assumes no tax — estimate the impact of 15% tax.
2. **Civic.** A council road is built at a rate of 120 m per day. The road is 3.2 km long. How many working days will construction take? If each day costs $14 500, what is the total cost? Discuss one assumption in this model.
3. **Vocational.** A tiler needs to cover a floor 4.2 m × 3.8 m using tiles that are 30 cm × 30 cm. Calculate the number of tiles needed. If tiles come in boxes of 12 at $38 per box, find the cost. Allow 10% extra for cuts and breakage.
4. **Ethical.** A phone plan costs AUD 49/month with 50 GB data. Excess data is $10/GB. You model monthly cost as C = 49 + 10(d − 50) for d > 50. A rival plan offers unlimited data for $79. At what usage do they cost the same? Discuss whether "unlimited" plans are always the ethical choice for consumers.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A family sends AUD 500 to the Philippines every month. Exchange rate: AUD 1 = PHP 37.20. The transfer service charges a flat $8 fee. How many pesos does the family receive? Over a year, how much goes to fees? Model the total pesos received over 12 months.

## Memorable takeaways
- Direct proportion: y = kx. The rate k connects the two quantities.
- Ratios divide totals into parts. Total parts = sum of ratio numbers.
- Scale: 1:n means each map/model unit = n real units. Area scales as n². Volume as n³.
- Financial: unit pricing finds the best buy. Exchange rates are proportion.
- Model, interpret, evaluate, report. State your assumptions.

## Stretch yourself
A school excursion takes 45 students from Melbourne to Ballarat (112 km). Option A: hire a bus at $580 flat fee. Option B: use 9 cars (5 students each) with fuel cost modelled as $0.15/km/car. (a) Calculate the cost per student for each option. (b) The bus averages 70 km/h; cars average 90 km/h. Calculate travel time for each. (c) Model total cost including driver time valued at $35/hour (1 bus driver vs 9 car drivers, for the return trip). (d) The bus emits 0.8 kg CO₂/km; each car emits 0.15 kg CO₂/km. Calculate total emissions for each option. (e) Evaluate both options considering cost, time, emissions and safety. Report your recommendation and assumptions. Show all working. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I used the wrong rate direction." Speed is km/h (distance per time). If you want time, divide distance by speed, don't multiply.
- "I forgot to convert units." Scale problems often need cm → m → km. Ensure all quantities are in compatible units before calculating.
- "I divided the ratio incorrectly." The total number of parts is the SUM of the ratio components. Each share = total ÷ total parts × that component's number.
- "I assumed the model works everywhere." Most models have a valid range. A fuel-cost model breaks down at extreme distances (fatigue stops), and a unit-price model ignores waste (buying 5 kg of perishable food you can't use).

A note on certainty. Proportion, ratio and scale calculations are exact mathematics. Their application to real-world problems depends on assumptions (constant rates, no hidden costs, things remaining proportional at different scales). Models are useful within their valid range — always check whether the assumptions hold in your specific context.
