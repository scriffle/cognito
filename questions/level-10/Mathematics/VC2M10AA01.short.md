# VC2M10AA01 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Polynomials and the factor and remainder theorems.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** The remainder theorem gives the remainder when P(x) is divided by (x − a) as P(a). The factor theorem says (x − a) is a factor if and only if P(a) = 0. Together they enable efficient factorisation and solving of polynomial equations.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **apply the factor and remainder theorems** to factorise polynomials and solve polynomial equations.
- You should be able to identify polynomial features, test for factors, and factorise cubics completely.

## Core content

**Polynomials** are expressions of the form aₙxⁿ + … + a₁x + a₀. The degree is the highest power; the leading coefficient is the coefficient of that term.

**Remainder theorem:** The remainder when P(x) is divided by (x − a) is P(a). For P(x) = x³ − 2x + 1 divided by (x − 3): P(3) = 27 − 6 + 1 = 22.

**Factor theorem:** If P(a) = 0, then (x − a) is a factor. For P(x) = x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6: P(1) = 0, so (x − 1) is a factor.

**Factorising cubics:** Find one root by testing, divide to get a quadratic, then factorise the quadratic. P(x) = x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = (x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3).

**Non-monic example.** P(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6. P(3) = 0, so (x − 3) is a factor. Dividing: (x − 3)(2x² + 3x − 2) = (x − 3)(2x − 1)(x + 2). Roots: x = 3, ½, −2.

**End behaviour:** Odd degree, positive leading coefficient → rises bottom-left to top-right. Even degree, positive → opens upward.

**Victorian context.** Engineers at CSIRO use polynomial models for signal processing. Polynomial theory spans civilisations — from Babylonian quadratics to Qin Jiushao's 13th-century Chinese methods and Omar Khayyam's algebraic contributions.

## Memorable takeaways
- P(a) gives the remainder when dividing P(x) by (x − a) — no long division needed.
- P(a) = 0 means (x − a) is a factor.
- Factorise cubics: find one root → divide → factorise the quadratic.
- A degree-n polynomial has at most n real roots; a cubic has at least one.

## Watch out for these traps
- Testing only positive integers — roots can be negative or fractional (use the rational root theorem).
- Sign errors when evaluating P(a) for negative values of a.
- Assuming three distinct real roots for every cubic — repeated or complex roots are possible.
