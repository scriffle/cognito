# VC2M10A01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10A01. Factorise algebraic expressions by taking out a common algebraic factor.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Factorising is the reverse of expanding — it rewrites an expression as a product.
  - The first step in any factorisation is to look for a common factor across all terms.
  - A common factor can be a number, a variable, or a combination of both.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **factorise algebraic expressions** by identifying and extracting a **common algebraic factor**.
- You are learning to recognise when an expression can be simplified by factoring, and to verify your answer by expanding.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify the highest common factor (HCF) of two or more algebraic terms.
- Factorise expressions with numerical, single-variable and multi-variable common factors.
- Factorise expressions where the common factor is a bracketed expression.
- Check your factorisation by expanding.

## Core content

**1. What is factorising?** Factorising rewrites an expression as a **product of factors**. It is the reverse of expanding. Expanding: 3x(2x + 5) = 6x² + 15x. Factorising: 6x² + 15x = 3x(2x + 5). A factorised expression is often simpler to work with — especially for solving equations or simplifying fractions. *Quick check:* What does "factorise" mean in plain language?

**2. Finding the numerical HCF.** Look at the coefficients (the numbers in front of the variables). Find the largest number that divides into all of them. For 12x + 18: the HCF of 12 and 18 is 6. So 12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3). *Quick check:* What is the HCF of 8 and 20?

**3. Finding the variable HCF.** Look at the variables in every term. The HCF includes each variable raised to its **lowest power** across all terms. For 6x³ + 9x²: numerical HCF = 3, variable HCF = x² (lowest power of x). So 6x³ + 9x² = 3x²(2x + 3). *Quick check:* What is the HCF of x⁴ and x²?

**4. Combined numerical and variable HCF.** Take both the numerical and variable parts together. For 15a²b − 10ab²: numerical HCF = 5, variable HCF = ab (lowest powers). So 15a²b − 10ab² = 5ab(3a − 2b). Always check by expanding: 5ab × 3a = 15a²b ✓; 5ab × (−2b) = −10ab² ✓. *Quick check:* Factorise 8m³n + 12m²n².

**5. Expressions with more than two terms.** The same method works for three or more terms. For 4x³ − 6x² + 10x: HCF = 2x. Result: 2x(2x² − 3x + 5). Check each term. *Quick check:* Factorise 3y³ + 9y² − 6y.

**6. Worked example — factorising a perimeter expression.**
An L-shaped garden has perimeter P = 6x² + 12x + 18.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Identify the terms: 6x², 12x, 18.
- Numerical HCF: 6 divides 6, 12 and 18.
- Variable HCF: the constant term 18 has no x, so x cannot be part of the common factor.
- HCF = 6.
- Factorise: 6x² + 12x + 18 = 6(x² + 2x + 3).
- Check: 6 × x² = 6x² ✓; 6 × 2x = 12x ✓; 6 × 3 = 18 ✓.

**7. Factorising with a bracketed common factor.** Sometimes the common factor is an entire bracket. For x(a + b) + 3(a + b): the common factor is (a + b). Result: (a + b)(x + 3). This technique is called **grouping** and is the gateway to more advanced factorisation. *Quick check:* Factorise 2m(x − 4) + 5(x − 4).

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Engineers at AMSI and in Victorian manufacturing use factorisation to simplify formulas before computing — reducing calculation errors and computation time. Architects simplify area and volume expressions when designing structures. The Australian Synchrotron's physicists factorise algebraic models of particle trajectories.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Algebraic structure mirrors relational thinking found in many knowledge systems. First Nations pattern-based reasoning — visible in kinship systems and seasonal calendars — demonstrates the same principle of identifying what is shared across multiple instances. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Factorisation techniques have roots in Islamic mathematical traditions — al-Khwārizmī's work on algebra (9th century CE) developed systematic approaches to simplifying expressions. Indian mathematicians like Brahmagupta also contributed foundational algebraic methods. Students in Victorian classrooms bring these diverse mathematical heritages.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You buy x T-shirts at $15 each and x pairs of socks at $5 each. The total cost is 15x + 5x. Factorise this expression and explain what the factorised form tells you.
2. **Civic.** A council's budget for two park projects is 8x² + 12x (in thousands of dollars). Factorise to show the common investment factor and the project-specific multipliers.
3. **Vocational.** An electrician calculates the total resistance of two circuit sections: 6R² + 9R. Factorise and explain what the common factor R represents physically.
4. **Ethical.** A student writes: 6x² + 9x = 3(2x² + 3x). Is this fully factorised? If not, complete the factorisation and explain why stopping too early is a common mistake.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research al-Khwārizmī's contribution to algebra. Then factorise completely: 14a³b² − 21a²b³ + 7a²b².

## Memorable takeaways
- Always look for a common factor first — it is step one of any factorisation.
- HCF = highest common factor of the numbers × lowest power of each shared variable.
- Check by expanding: if you get back the original, your factorisation is correct.
- A constant term (no variable) limits the variable part of the HCF.
- Factorising simplifies expressions and prepares them for solving.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Not finding the highest common factor.** 12x + 18 = 2(6x + 9) is partly factorised but not fully — the HCF is 6, giving 6(2x + 3).
- **Forgetting variables in the HCF.** In 6x³ + 9x², the HCF is 3x² not just 3. Always check the variable powers.
- **Including a variable when a term has none.** In 4x² + 8x + 12, the constant 12 has no x, so x is not part of the HCF.
- **Not checking by expanding.** Always multiply out your answer to verify it matches the original.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *T-shirts and socks.* 15x + 5x = 5x(3 + 1) = 5x × 4 = 20x. Or more usefully: 15x + 5x = x(15 + 5) = x(20) = 20x. The factorised form shows x items at a combined price of $20.
2. *Council budget.* 8x² + 12x = 4x(2x + 3). Common factor 4x represents the base investment; (2x + 3) represents the project-specific multiplier.
3. *Resistance.* 6R² + 9R = 3R(2R + 3). The common factor R represents the shared resistance component.
4. *Incomplete factorisation.* 3(2x² + 3x) is not fully factorised — inside the bracket, x is still common. Fully: 3x(2x + 3).
5. *al-Khwārizmī + factorisation.* 14a³b² − 21a²b³ + 7a²b² = 7a²b²(2a − 3b + 1).

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** A = 4x² + 20x + 24. HCF = 4. A = 4(x² + 5x + 6). The bracket factorises: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3). So A = 4(x + 2)(x + 3). Check: 4(x + 2)(x + 3) = 4(x² + 5x + 6) = 4x² + 20x + 24 ✓. When x = 3: A = 4(5)(6) = 120 m². Pool area: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3). When x = 3: pool = 5 × 6 = 30 m². Surround = 120 − 30 = 90 m². Alternatively: surround = 4(x + 2)(x + 3) − (x + 2)(x + 3) = 3(x + 2)(x + 3) = 3 × 30 = 90 m².
