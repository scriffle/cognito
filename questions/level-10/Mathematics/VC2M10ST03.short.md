# VC2M10ST03 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Construct two-way tables and discuss possible relationship between categorical variables.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Two-way tables display counts for two categorical variables simultaneously. Comparing row/column percentages reveals association. If proportions are similar across categories, no association exists. If they differ, variables are associated. Association ≠ causation.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct two-way tables** and investigate whether categorical variables are associated.
- You should calculate proportions, compare across groups, and describe associations in context.

## Core content

**Two-way table structure.** Rows = one categorical variable, columns = another. Cells = frequency counts. Margins = row/column totals.

**Row percentages for comparison.** Each cell ÷ row total × 100. Compare percentages across rows. Similar rates → no association. Different rates → association.

**Example.** 15 males walk, 8 females walk (out of 15 males, 15 females). Males: 8/15 = 53% walk. Females: 10/15 = 67% walk. Association: females more likely to walk.

**Worked example — transport and year level.** Year 9: Bus 30%, Car 40%, Walk 30%. Year 10: Bus 20%, Car 30%, Walk 50%. Year 10 more likely to walk — association exists between year level and transport mode.

**Caution.** Association ≠ causation. Confounding variables may explain the link. Small samples may show apparent associations due to chance.

**Victorian context.** ABS Census cross-tabulates employment, education and health for Victorian regions. VicHealth investigates lifestyle-health associations. Research about Aboriginal communities frequently uses two-way tables — critical literacy helps communities evaluate whether causal claims from associations are justified.

## Memorable takeaways
- Two-way table: rows × columns of frequency counts.
- Compare row percentages for fair comparison.
- Similar proportions = no association; different = association.
- Association does not prove causation.
- Small samples can show apparent associations by chance.

## Watch out for these traps
- Comparing raw counts when group sizes differ — always use percentages.
- Concluding causation from a two-way table.
- Drawing strong conclusions from tiny samples (need 30+ for reliability).
- Forgetting context — percentages need explanation of what they represent.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *8 of 15 males walk.* Row percentage = 53.3%.
- *60% vs 62% pizza preference across year levels.* Very similar — no meaningful association.
- *Online 69% satisfied vs in-store 71%.* Minimal difference — no association.
- *80% pet-owners exercise vs 50% non-owners.* Association exists, but not necessarily causal (active people may choose dogs).
- *Council: 70% north support vs 60% south.* Moderate association between area and support for park.
