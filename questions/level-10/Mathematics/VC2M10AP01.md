# VC2M10AP01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AP01. Counting principles and factorial notation for efficient counting in probability and combinatorics.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The multiplication principle states that if one event can occur in m ways and a second in n ways, the two events together can occur in m × n ways.
  - Factorial notation n! = n × (n−1) × … × 1 counts the number of ways to arrange n distinct objects.
  - Permutations count ordered arrangements; combinations count unordered selections.

## Core content

**1. The multiplication principle.** If task A can be done in m ways and task B in n ways, then A followed by B can be done in m × n ways. For 5 shirts and 3 pants: 5 × 3 = 15 outfits. This extends to more stages: 5 shirts × 3 pants × 2 shoes = 30 combinations. *Quick check:* A menu offers 4 mains and 3 desserts. How many different two-course meals are possible?

**2. Factorial notation.** n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × … × 2 × 1. 5! = 120. 3! = 6. By convention, 0! = 1. Factorials count the number of ways to arrange n distinct objects in a line: 5 books on a shelf can be arranged in 5! = 120 ways. *Quick check:* Evaluate 6! and 4!/2!.

**3. Permutations — order matters.** The number of ways to arrange r objects from n distinct objects is P(n,r) = n!/(n−r)!. Choosing a president, secretary and treasurer from 10 people: P(10,3) = 10!/7! = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720. Order matters because the roles are different. *Quick check:* How many three-digit numbers can be formed from the digits 1–7 without repetition?

**4. Combinations — order does not matter.** The number of ways to choose r objects from n where order is irrelevant is C(n,r) = n!/[r!(n−r)!]. Choosing a committee of 3 from 10 people: C(10,3) = 10!/(3!·7!) = 120. Order does not matter because committee members are equal. *Quick check:* How many ways can 4 students be chosen from 9 for a team?

**5. Distinguishing permutations from combinations.** Key question: does the order of selection matter? If selecting positions (1st, 2nd, 3rd) → permutation. If selecting a group → combination. "Choose 3 from 10 for a committee" → C(10,3) = 120. "Rank 3 from 10 in a race" → P(10,3) = 720. *Quick check:* A lock requires a 4-digit code from digits 0–9 without repetition. Is this a permutation or combination problem? Find the answer.

**6. Worked example — probability using counting.**
A committee of 3 is chosen at random from 5 women and 4 men. Find the probability that the committee has at least 2 women.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Total committees: C(9,3) = 84.
- Exactly 2 women: C(5,2) × C(4,1) = 10 × 4 = 40.
- Exactly 3 women: C(5,3) = 10.
- At least 2 women: 40 + 10 = 50.
- P(at least 2 women) = 50/84 = 25/42.

**7. The addition principle.** If events A and B are mutually exclusive, the total number of outcomes is m + n. Choosing from 5 fiction books OR 3 non-fiction books: 5 + 3 = 8 choices. The addition principle often works alongside the multiplication principle. *Quick check:* A student may choose 1 elective from 6 arts subjects or 4 science subjects. How many choices are there?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Combinatorics underpins cryptography (password strength calculations), genetics (DNA combinations), and statistical sampling — all active research areas in Victorian universities.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Combinatorial thinking appears in the structure of First Nations kinship systems, where complex rules determine permissible relationships. The Aranda (Arrernte) eight-section system is a sophisticated combinatorial structure studied by anthropologists and mathematicians alike.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Pascal's triangle, which encodes combination values, was studied by Chinese mathematician Yang Hui (13th century), Indian mathematician Pingala (2nd century BCE), and Persian mathematician al-Karajī (10th century) before Pascal.

2. **Civic.** A council selects a 5-person committee from 12 applicants. How many possible committees are there?

## Memorable takeaways
- Multiplication principle: m × n for sequential choices.
- n! counts arrangements of n distinct objects.
- Permutations (order matters): P(n,r) = n!/(n−r)!.
- Combinations (order irrelevant): C(n,r) = n!/[r!(n−r)!].
- Ask: "Does order matter?" to distinguish permutation from combination.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Using permutations when combinations are needed.** If the order of selection does not matter, divide by r!.
- **Forgetting 0! = 1.** This is a definition, not a calculation error.
- **Repeating elements.** P(n,r) assumes no repetition. If repetition is allowed, use nʳ instead.
- **Mixing up the formula.** C(n,r) = n!/[r!(n−r)!]. Forgetting the r! in the denominator gives the permutation instead.

## Try drawing this
Draw a tree diagram for choosing 2 items from {A, B, C} where order matters. List all 6 permutations. Then circle the 3 combinations (pairs that differ only in order). This shows why C(3,2) = P(3,2)/2! = 3.

A note on certainty. Counting formulas give exact results — the number of permutations or combinations is a definite integer. Probability calculations based on these counts are equally exact (for equally likely outcomes).
