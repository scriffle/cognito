# VC2M10ST02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10ST02. Construct scatterplots and consider a line of good fit; comment on the association between the 2 numerical variables in terms of strength, direction and linearity.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A **scatterplot** displays the relationship between two numerical variables — each point represents one data pair.
  - Association is described by **direction** (positive/negative), **strength** (strong/moderate/weak) and **form** (linear/non-linear).
  - A **line of good fit** approximates the trend and can be used for prediction, but correlation does not imply causation.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct scatterplots**, fit a line to the data, and describe the association between two variables.
- You are learning to distinguish correlation from causation and to make cautious predictions.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Construct a scatterplot from paired data.
- Describe association in terms of direction, strength and linearity.
- Draw a line of good fit (by eye or using digital tools).
- Use the line to make predictions and discuss limitations.
- Identify outliers and non-linear patterns.

## Core content

**1. Constructing scatterplots.** Plot each data pair as a point: horizontal axis = explanatory variable (independent), vertical axis = response variable (dependent). Example: plot height (x) against weight (y) for 20 students. Each student is one point. *Quick check:* You investigate whether study hours affect test scores. Which variable goes on the x-axis?

**2. Direction of association.** **Positive association:** as x increases, y tends to increase (points slope up-left to right). **Negative association:** as x increases, y tends to decrease (points slope down). **No association:** no clear pattern — points scattered randomly. *Quick check:* Temperature vs ice cream sales — positive or negative? Age vs reaction time?

**3. Strength of association.** **Strong:** points clustered closely around a line/curve. **Moderate:** general trend visible but with scatter. **Weak:** vague trend, wide scatter. Strength is judged by how tightly points cluster around the trend — the less scatter, the stronger the association. *Quick check:* Points form a tight upward band. Describe the association.

**4. Linearity.** **Linear:** points follow a straight-line trend. **Non-linear:** points follow a curve (quadratic, exponential, etc.). A line of good fit is only appropriate for linear associations. If the pattern curves, a straight line will misrepresent the relationship. *Quick check:* Height vs age for children aged 0–18 — likely linear or non-linear? Why?

**5. Line of good fit.** A line drawn to represent the overall trend of data with a linear association. It should: pass through the middle of the data, have roughly equal numbers of points above and below, and minimise overall distance from points to line. Can be drawn by eye or using technology (least-squares line). *Quick check:* A line of good fit for height (cm) vs weight (kg) passes through (160, 55) and (180, 75). What is its gradient? What does the gradient mean in context?

**6. Worked example — study time and marks.**
Data: (1, 42), (2, 48), (3, 55), (4, 58), (5, 63), (6, 70), (7, 72), (8, 78), (9, 82), (10, 88).

*Follow the thinking:*
- Plot points: study hours (x) vs mark (y).
- Pattern: clear positive trend, approximately linear.
- Direction: positive (more study → higher marks).
- Strength: strong (points close to a line).
- Line of good fit: passes through approximately (1, 42) and (10, 88). Gradient = (88−42)/(10−1) = 46/9 ≈ 5.1. y-intercept ≈ 37.
- Equation: y ≈ 5.1x + 37.
- Prediction: 12 hours → y ≈ 5.1(12) + 37 = 98.2. But caution — this extrapolates beyond the data range. Scores can't exceed 100, and the trend may not continue.
- **Correlation ≠ causation.** The data shows an association. Other factors (ability, prior knowledge) also affect marks.

**7. Correlation ≠ causation.** Just because two variables are associated does not mean one causes the other. There may be: a **confounding variable** (third factor affecting both), **reverse causation** (y causes x), or **coincidence**. Example: ice cream sales and drowning rates are positively correlated — but ice cream doesn't cause drowning. Hot weather (confounding variable) increases both. *Quick check:* A study finds that countries with more TV sets have higher life expectancy. Does TV cause longer life? What might explain this?

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Collect data: hours of sleep (x) and energy level out of 10 (y) for 10 days. Plot a scatterplot. Describe the association. Draw a line of good fit and predict your energy level for 9 hours of sleep.
2. **Civic.** A council plots suburb distance from CBD (km) against median house price ($). The scatterplot shows a strong negative linear association. What does this mean? Use a line of good fit to predict the price at 25 km from CBD.
3. **Vocational.** A fitness trainer records weeks of training (x) and 5km run time in minutes (y). The association is negative and moderate. What does this mean? If the line of good fit is y = −0.5x + 30, predict run time after 12 weeks. Is this prediction reliable?
4. **Ethical.** A media article claims "students who eat breakfast score higher on tests" based on a scatterplot showing positive association. Identify two possible confounding variables. Explain why this does not prove breakfast causes better scores.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Using World Bank data, plot GDP per capita (x) against life expectancy (y) for 20 countries. Describe the association (likely positive, non-linear — diminishing returns at high GDP). Why might a straight line be inappropriate here?

## Memorable takeaways
- Scatterplot: one point per data pair, explanatory on x, response on y.
- Direction: positive (up), negative (down), or none.
- Strength: strong (tight), moderate (visible trend), weak (scattered).
- Form: linear (straight trend) or non-linear (curved).
- Correlation ≠ causation — always consider confounding variables.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Claiming causation from correlation.** A scatterplot shows association, never proof of causation. Always consider confounders.
- **Drawing a line through non-linear data.** If the pattern curves, a straight line is inappropriate. Check by looking at residuals.
- **Extrapolating too far.** Predictions beyond the data range are unreliable — the trend may not continue.
- **Ignoring outliers.** A single unusual point can pull the line of good fit. Identify and investigate outliers before fitting the line.
- **Putting variables on wrong axes.** The explanatory (cause/input) goes on x; the response (effect/output) on y.

A note on certainty. The mathematical line of best fit (least-squares regression) is exact — it minimises the sum of squared residuals. But its meaning depends on context: a strong correlation is a mathematical fact about the data; a causal interpretation is a scientific claim that requires additional evidence (controlled experiments, elimination of confounders). Statistics reveals patterns; science and critical thinking determine their meaning.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Sleep and energy.* Answers vary. Likely positive linear association (more sleep → more energy). Line prediction for 9 hours should be checked against whether 9 hours is within the data range (interpolation = reliable).
2. *Distance and house price.* Strong negative linear = as distance from CBD increases, prices tend to decrease consistently. Prediction at 25 km uses the line equation — reliable only if 25 km is within the data range.
3. *Fitness.* Negative moderate = more training tends to reduce run time, but with variability. At 12 weeks: y = −0.5(12) + 30 = 24 min. Reliable if 12 weeks is within range; may not hold indefinitely (can't run negative time).

5. *GDP vs life expectancy.* Positive but non-linear — big gains at low GDP, diminishing returns at high GDP. A straight line would overestimate life expectancy at low GDP and underestimate the "plateau" effect at high GDP. A logarithmic or square-root transformation might linearise the relationship.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Example data: 20 pairs, exercise hours vs resting heart rate. Scatterplot shows moderate-to-strong negative linear association. Line: y = −2.5x + 80 (each extra hour of exercise reduces resting HR by ~2.5 bpm). For 10 hours: y = −2.5(10) + 80 = 55 bpm. This is interpolation if data ranges from 0–15 hours. Outlier: one person exercises 8 hours but HR = 85 — possibly a medical condition or measurement error. Causation is plausible (exercise strengthens heart) but confounders exist (genetics, diet, stress). At 30+ hours/week, the linear model predicts HR = 5 bpm — impossible. The relationship plateaus. Residuals: point (4, 73), predicted 70, residual +3; point (7, 60), predicted 62.5, residual −2.5. Small residuals suggest good fit.
