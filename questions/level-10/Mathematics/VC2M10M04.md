## Core content

**1. Direct proportion.** Two quantities are in **direct proportion** if one is a constant multiple of the other: y = kx. When x doubles, y doubles. The graph is a straight line through the origin. Example: cost of fuel at a fixed price per litre. If petrol is $1.85/L, then cost = 1.85 × litres. The constant k = 1.85 is the **constant of proportionality**. *Quick check:* A recipe uses 3 cups of flour for 12 scones. How many cups for 20 scones?

**2. Inverse proportion.** Two quantities are in **inverse proportion** if their product is constant: y = k/x, or xy = k. When x doubles, y halves. The graph is a hyperbola. Example: if 4 workers take 6 hours to paint a fence, then workers × hours = 24. Two workers take 12 hours; 8 workers take 3 hours. *Quick check:* A journey takes 3 hours at 80 km/h. How long at 60 km/h?

**3. Identifying the type.** Ask: if I double one quantity, does the other double (direct) or halve (inverse)? Distance at constant speed: direct (double time → double distance). Time for a trip at varying speed: inverse (double speed → half time). Not every relationship is proportional — check whether the data fits y = kx or y = k/x before assuming. *Quick check:* Is the relationship between the number of identical tiles and the area they cover direct or inverse?

**4. Scale factors — length, area and volume.** When an object is scaled by a factor of k: lengths multiply by k, areas multiply by k², volumes multiply by k³. A model car at 1:24 scale has lengths 1/24 of the real car, surface area (1/24)² = 1/576 of the real car, and volume (1/24)³ = 1/13 824 of the real car. *Quick check:* A cube has side 2 cm. A similar cube has side 6 cm. What is the ratio of their volumes?

**5. Measurement errors and accuracy.** Every measurement has uncertainty. If you measure a room as 5.0 m ± 0.1 m by 4.0 m ± 0.1 m, the area could range from 4.9 × 3.9 = 19.11 m² to 5.1 × 4.1 = 20.91 m². That is a spread of nearly 2 m² from a measurement error of just 0.1 m in each dimension. Errors compound when measurements are multiplied (for area and volume). Reporting results to an appropriate number of significant figures reflects this uncertainty. *Quick check:* A cylinder has r = 3.0 ± 0.1 cm, h = 10.0 ± 0.2 cm. Calculate the maximum and minimum volume.

**6. Worked example: scaling a water tank.**

A council designs a water tank as a cylinder. A 1:10 scale model holds 5 litres.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Volume scales as k³. The real tank is 10× larger in each dimension.
- Volume ratio = 10³ = 1 000.
- Real tank capacity = 5 × 1 000 = 5 000 litres = 5 m³.
- Surface area ratio = 10² = 100. If the model has SA = 0.12 m², real SA = 12 m².
- Assumptions: exact geometric similarity; no thickness changes in walls at full scale.
- If the model length was measured ± 1 mm at 1:10 scale, this translates to ± 10 mm (1 cm) at full size — errors also scale.

**7. The modelling cycle.** Mathematical modelling follows a cycle: (1) identify the real-world problem, (2) make simplifying assumptions, (3) build a mathematical model, (4) solve and interpret, (5) compare with reality, (6) refine if needed, (7) report assumptions and findings. Reporting should state what you assumed (e.g. "we assumed constant speed"), what your model predicts, how accurate results are (given measurement error), and what the model does not capture. *Quick check:* You model paint needed for a room using SA = 2(lw + lh + wh). Name two assumptions this model makes and one thing it ignores.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** VicRoads uses proportional modelling to estimate road-surfacing material: surface area is proportional to road length at a given width. Melbourne Water uses scale models to test dam spillway designs — volume scales as the cube of the scale factor, a critical consideration. Architects for the Melbourne Metro Tunnel project used scaled cross-section models where measurement error at model scale translated to construction tolerances at full scale.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations design and construction embeds proportional reasoning — the scaling of fish traps at Budj Bim to different stream widths uses direct proportion between trap size and waterway dimensions. Boomerang design involves proportional relationships between arm length, width and flight characteristics. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Recipe scaling in multicultural cuisine is direct proportion — doubling ingredients for larger gatherings. Textile and garment design across cultures involves scaling patterns, where area of fabric scales as the square of the linear scale factor. Import businesses across Melbourne use inverse proportion (exchange rate × quantity at fixed budget).

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A recipe for 4 people uses 200 g of rice, 150 g of lentils and 400 mL of water. Scale it for 7 people. If your kitchen scale has an accuracy of ± 5 g, what is the range of rice you might actually use?
2. **Civic.** A council plans a new park fountain. The architect's 1:20 scale model uses 3.5 litres of water. How much water will the full-size fountain hold? If the model's measurements are accurate to ± 2 mm, what is the error range in the real fountain's key dimensions?
3. **Vocational.** A solar panel installer knows that 10 panels produce 4.5 kW. A client wants 12 kW. Assuming direct proportion, how many panels are needed? Discuss one assumption this model makes and when it might break down.
4. **Ethical.** A pharmaceutical company scales up drug production from a lab batch (500 mL) to industrial scale (5 000 L). The scale factor is 10 000. Discuss why simply multiplying all quantities by 10 000 might not produce the same result, and why measurement accuracy becomes critical at each stage.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A Turkish ceramic artist in Melbourne creates tiles. A large decorative tile is a scaled-up version of a small tile (scale factor 2.5). If the small tile uses 80 g of glaze, estimate the glaze needed for the large tile. Explain why the answer uses k² not k.

## Memorable takeaways
- Direct proportion: y = kx. Double one → double the other.
- Inverse proportion: y = k/x. Double one → halve the other.
- Scaling: lengths × k, areas × k², volumes × k³.
- Models require assumptions — state them.
- Measurement errors compound, especially in area and volume calculations.
- Report what the model assumes and what it cannot capture.

## Stretch yourself
A landscape architect designs a pond. The 1:25 scale model is an irregular shape, but by filling it with water and measuring, she determines the model holds 2.4 litres and has a wetted surface area of 0.18 m². (a) Calculate the full-size pond's volume in litres and m³. (b) Calculate the full-size pond's wetted surface area. (c) The model's volume measurement is accurate to ± 0.1 L. What is the error range for the real pond's volume? (d) A liner costs $45 per m² of surface area. Calculate the liner cost and its error range if the model's surface area measurement is ± 0.01 m². (e) Discuss two assumptions in this modelling approach and how they affect confidence in the final cost estimate. Show all working. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I scaled area by k instead of k²." Areas scale as the square of the linear scale factor. If lengths triple, area goes up by 9, not 3.
- "I assumed every relationship is proportional." Check the data. Not everything follows y = kx or y = k/x. Plot the data or check ratios before assuming.
- "I ignored measurement error." Small errors in measured dimensions become large errors in calculated areas and volumes. Always report the impact.
- "I forgot to state my assumptions." A model without stated assumptions cannot be evaluated or improved. Always report what you assumed.

A note on certainty. Proportional relationships and scaling laws are established mathematics. Mathematical models are simplifications — their usefulness depends on whether the assumptions hold in the real situation. Measurement error is inherent in all physical data; reporting its impact honestly is part of good mathematical practice.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Flour for 20 scones.* 3 cups for 12. k = 3/12 = 0.25 cups per scone. For 20: 0.25 × 20 = 5 cups.
2. *Journey at 60 km/h.* Speed × time = distance = 80 × 3 = 240 km. At 60: time = 240/60 = 4 hours.
3. *Tiles and area.* Direct proportion — double the tiles, double the area covered.
4. *Volume ratio of cubes.* Scale factor = 6/2 = 3. Volume ratio = 3³ = 27.
5. *Cylinder volume range.* Max: π(3.1²)(10.2) = π(9.61)(10.2) ≈ 308.0 cm³. Min: π(2.9²)(9.8) = π(8.41)(9.8) ≈ 258.8 cm³. Nominal: π(9)(10) ≈ 282.7 cm³. The range spans about ± 25 cm³ from nominal.
6. *Paint model assumptions.* Assumes rectangular walls (no alcoves or irregular shapes); assumes flat ceiling not counted (or that ceiling is a separate calculation); ignores doors and windows (which reduce the area to be painted).

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Recipe scaling.* Scale factor = 7/4 = 1.75. Rice = 200 × 1.75 = 350 g. Lentils = 150 × 1.75 = 262.5 g. Water = 400 × 1.75 = 700 mL. With ± 5 g scale accuracy, rice range: 345–355 g.
2. *Fountain.* Volume ratio = 20³ = 8 000. Real volume = 3.5 × 8 000 = 28 000 L = 28 m³. Model ± 2 mm at 1:20 scale → real error = ± 40 mm = ± 4 cm per dimension.
3. *Solar panels.* 10 panels → 4.5 kW, so 1 panel → 0.45 kW. For 12 kW: 12/0.45 = 26.7 → need 27 panels. Assumption: each panel produces equal output. Breaks down if panels are shaded differently, face different directions, or efficiency varies with temperature.
4. *Pharmaceutical scaling.* Mixing, heating and reaction times do not scale linearly. A reaction in 500 mL may behave differently in a 5 000 L vessel (heat distribution, mixing uniformity). Measurement errors at each stage compound — a 1% error in a key ingredient at industrial scale means 50 L too much or too little.
5. *Ceramic tiles.* Glaze covers surface area. Scale factor = 2.5. Area ratio = 2.5² = 6.25. Glaze = 80 × 6.25 = 500 g. We use k² because glaze covers a 2D surface — area scales with the square of the linear dimension, not linearly.

(c) Model ± 0.1 L. Range: (2.3 to 2.5) × 15 625 = 35 938 to 39 063 L. Error range ≈ ± 1 563 L. (d) Model SA range: (0.17 to 0.19) × 625 = 106.25 to 118.75 m². Cost at $45/m²: 106.25 × 45 = $4 781 to 118.75 × 45 = $5 344. Nominal = 112.5 × 45 = $5 063. Range ≈ ± $280. (e) Assumptions: (1) exact geometric similarity between model and real pond — in practice, construction tolerances mean the real shape may differ; (2) the liner lies flat against the surface with no overlaps or waste — in practice, extra material is needed for seams and edges. Both assumptions mean the real cost is likely higher than calculated.
