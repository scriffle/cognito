# VC2M9M04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9M04. Calculate and interpret absolute, relative and percentage errors in measurements.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Measurement).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Every measurement has uncertainty — no measurement is perfectly exact.
  - Absolute error is the difference between measured and actual values (or half the smallest unit).
  - Relative error expresses the absolute error as a fraction of the measurement.
  - Percentage error expresses relative error as a percentage.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Science (cross-curricular); Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **calculate and interpret** absolute, relative and percentage errors in measurements.
- You are learning that all physical measurements are approximations, and that quantifying the error tells you how trustworthy a measurement is.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define and calculate absolute error.
- Define and calculate relative error.
- Convert relative error to percentage error.
- Determine the maximum possible error from a measuring instrument.
- Interpret what error values mean in practical contexts.
- Compare the precision of different measurements using relative/percentage error.

## Core content

**1. Absolute error.** The **absolute error** is the magnitude of the difference between the measured value and the true (or accepted) value: absolute error = |measured − true|. When the true value is unknown, the maximum absolute error is half the smallest graduation on the measuring instrument. A ruler marked in mm has maximum absolute error of ± 0.5 mm. *Quick check:* A tape measure marked in cm measures a table as 142 cm. What is the maximum absolute error?

**2. Relative error.** The **relative error** expresses how large the error is compared to the measurement itself: relative error = absolute error / measured value. This is a dimensionless number (no units). A 0.5 mm error on a 200 mm measurement gives relative error = 0.5/200 = 0.0025. *Quick check:* A measurement of 5.0 m has an absolute error of ± 0.05 m. What is the relative error?

**3. Percentage error.** **Percentage error** = relative error × 100%. This is the most intuitive way to communicate measurement quality. A relative error of 0.0025 = 0.25%. Small percentage errors mean high precision. *Quick check:* A student measures the length of a football field as 98 m. The actual length is 100 m. What is the percentage error?

**4. Precision vs accuracy.** **Precision** relates to how small the error is (how closely repeated measurements agree). **Accuracy** is how close the measurement is to the true value. You can be precise but inaccurate (consistently wrong) or imprecise but accurate on average. Relative and percentage error measure accuracy; the instrument's smallest division relates to precision. *Quick check:* Two scales measure a 500 g mass. Scale A reads 498 g. Scale B reads 503 g. Which has the smaller percentage error?

**5. Error in calculated quantities.** When measurements are used in calculations, errors propagate. For addition/subtraction: absolute errors add. If length = 5.2 ± 0.1 m and width = 3.4 ± 0.1 m, perimeter has absolute error up to 4 × 0.1 = 0.4 m. For multiplication: percentage errors add approximately. Area percentage error ≈ 1.9% + 2.9% ≈ 4.8%. *Quick check:* Two lengths 8.0 ± 0.1 m and 6.0 ± 0.1 m are multiplied for area. What is the approximate percentage error in the area?

**6. Worked example: surveying a block of land.**

A surveyor measures a rectangular block as 24.5 m × 18.3 m using equipment accurate to ± 0.05 m.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Absolute error on each measurement: ± 0.05 m.
- Relative error (length): 0.05/24.5 = 0.00204 = 0.204%.
- Relative error (width): 0.05/18.3 = 0.00273 = 0.273%.
- Area = 24.5 × 18.3 = 448.35 m².
- Percentage error in area ≈ 0.204% + 0.273% = 0.477%.
- Absolute error in area ≈ 448.35 × 0.00477 ≈ 2.1 m².
- Report: Area = 448 ± 2 m² (to appropriate precision).
- Interpretation: for a land sale at $1 200/m², this 2 m² uncertainty represents about $2 500.

**7. Choosing appropriate precision.** Report results to a level of precision that matches your measurement accuracy. If your measurements have ± 1% error, don't report results to 6 significant figures. Round to reflect genuine precision. The number of significant figures in your answer should match the least precise measurement used. *Quick check:* Your measurements give a volume of 2 347.892 cm³ but your measurement error is ± 3%. How should you report this?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Surveyors across Victoria must meet precision standards for land titles — measurement errors translate directly to property boundary disputes. The Melbourne Metro Tunnel project required construction tolerances of millimetres over kilometres, making error analysis critical. VicRoads speed cameras have a stated error margin (typically ± 2 km/h) built into infringement thresholds.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Traditional measurement in First Nations practice uses comparative and relational approaches — the accuracy of a fish trap at Budj Bim doesn't require millimetre precision, but does require proportional accuracy (gaps must be consistent relative to fish size). This represents an intuitive understanding of relative error. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Precision in measurement is critical in trades across Melbourne — cabinetmakers, tilers and garment makers all work to specific tolerances. Cultural food preparation (fermentation timing, spice quantities) involves understanding acceptable error ranges.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You measure your height as 168 cm using a tape measure marked in cm. Your friend measures their height as 12 cm using the same tape (they measured their hand span by mistake). Both have absolute error ± 0.5 cm. Calculate the percentage error for each. Which measurement is more reliable? Why?
2. **Civic.** A council speed camera has ± 3% error. It records a car at 64 km/h in a 60 km/h zone. Can the council be certain the car was speeding? What speed must the camera record to guarantee the car was exceeding 60 km/h?
3. **Vocational.** A carpenter cuts timber to length 2 400 mm ± 2 mm. The client's specification requires accuracy within 0.1%. Does the carpenter meet the specification? If 10 such pieces are placed end-to-end, what is the maximum cumulative error?
4. **Ethical.** A pharmaceutical company measures drug doses with 0.5% percentage error. For a dose of 200 mg, what is the absolute error range? Discuss why tighter tolerances (smaller errors) are critical in medicine but might be unnecessarily expensive in other contexts.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A textile designer measures fabric width as 1.52 m ± 0.01 m. She needs 45 m of this fabric for a project. Calculate the percentage error in width and the potential area variation over the full 45 m length. How might this affect pattern matching?

## Memorable takeaways
- Absolute error = |measured − true| or ± half the smallest unit.
- Relative error = absolute error / measured value (no units).
- Percentage error = relative error × 100%.
- Small measurements with the same absolute error have LARGER percentage errors.
- For multiplied quantities: percentage errors add approximately.
- Report results to a precision that matches your measurement accuracy.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I said the absolute error is the same as the percentage error." They are different: absolute error has units (mm, cm, m); percentage error is dimensionless (a %).
- "I thought a small absolute error always means a precise measurement." A 1 mm error is excellent for a 10 m measurement (0.01%) but terrible for a 2 mm measurement (50%). Context matters — use relative or percentage error to compare.
- "I reported my answer to 8 decimal places." If your measurements have 1% error, your answer should reflect that precision. Extra decimal places are false precision.
- "I forgot to halve the smallest division." The maximum error from a graduated instrument is ± half the smallest marked interval, not the full interval.

A note on certainty. The mathematics of error calculation is exact. However, real measurement errors may be systematic (consistently high or low) or random (varying each time). The methods here primarily address maximum possible error from instrument precision. In scientific work, statistical methods handle random errors more rigorously.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Table measured to nearest cm.* Maximum absolute error = ± 0.5 cm.
2. *Relative error.* 0.05/5.0 = 0.01.
3. *Football field percentage error.* Absolute error = |98 − 100| = 2 m. Percentage error = 2/100 × 100% = 2%.
4. *Scales.* Scale A: |498 − 500|/500 = 2/500 = 0.4%. Scale B: |503 − 500|/500 = 3/500 = 0.6%. Scale A has smaller percentage error.
5. *Area percentage error.* Length %error = 0.1/8.0 × 100 = 1.25%. Width %error = 0.1/6.0 × 100 = 1.67%. Area %error ≈ 1.25 + 1.67 = 2.92%.
6. *Volume reporting.* 3% of 2 348 ≈ 70. Report as 2 350 ± 70 cm³ (or 2.35 × 10³ cm³ to 3 significant figures).

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Height measurements.* Your height: %error = 0.5/168 × 100 = 0.30%. Hand span: %error = 0.5/12 × 100 = 4.17%. Your height measurement is far more reliable — the same absolute error is a much smaller fraction of a larger measurement.
2. *Speed camera.* At 64 recorded with ± 3%: actual speed could be 64 × 0.97 = 62.1 to 64 × 1.03 = 65.9 km/h. Since 62.1 > 60, the car was likely speeding, but the lower bound (62.1) still exceeds 60. Camera must record above 60/0.97 ≈ 61.9 km/h to guarantee speeding. Actually, if camera over-reads by 3%, then 64 × 0.97 = 62.1 is the minimum true speed. Since 62.1 > 60, the car was definitively speeding. In practice, authorities add a buffer (e.g. won't fine below 64 km/h).
3. *Carpenter.* Percentage error = 2/2400 × 100 = 0.083%. Spec requires ≤ 0.1%. Yes, meets spec. Ten pieces end-to-end: maximum cumulative error = 10 × 2 = ± 20 mm.
4. *Pharmaceutical.* 0.5% of 200 mg = ± 1 mg. Dose range: 199–201 mg. In medicine, even small percentage errors can affect patient safety. In construction, 0.5% on a 10 m beam (± 5 cm) may be acceptable — the consequence of error determines the required precision.
5. *Textile.* Width %error = 0.01/1.52 × 100 = 0.66%. Over 45 m: area = 1.52 × 45 = 68.4 m². Area variation ≈ 68.4 × 0.0066 = ± 0.45 m². For pattern matching, 1 cm variation over 1.52 m means patterns could be offset by up to 6.6 mm per width — noticeable on repeated motifs.
