# VC2M9SP01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9SP01. Recognise the constancy of the sine, cosine and tangent ratios for a given angle in right-angled triangles using properties of similarity.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Space).
- **Big ideas:**
  - All right-angled triangles with the same angle are similar (AA similarity).
  - Because they are similar, the ratios of corresponding sides are constant.
  - This constancy is WHY sin, cos and tan work — they depend only on the angle, not the size of the triangle.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **recognise why trigonometric ratios are constant** for any given angle in any right-angled triangle — because all such triangles are similar.
- You are learning that similarity guarantees the side ratios depend only on the angle, making sin, cos and tan universally applicable regardless of triangle size.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Explain why all right-angled triangles with the same acute angle are similar.
- Demonstrate that side ratios remain constant across similar triangles.
- Connect this constancy to the definitions of sin, cos and tan.
- Verify the constancy by measuring triangles of different sizes with the same angle.
- Explain why this property makes trigonometry useful for indirect measurement.

## Core content

**1. Similar triangles — same angles, same ratios.** Two triangles are similar if they have the same angles (AA criterion). Similar triangles have sides in the same ratio. All right-angled triangles containing, say, a 30° angle are similar to each other — they all share angles 30°, 60°, 90°. Therefore their side ratios are identical. *Quick check:* A right triangle has angles 90°, 40°, 50°. Another right triangle also has a 40° angle. Are they similar? Why?

**2. The constancy of ratios.** Draw a right triangle with a 35° angle and sides 5, 8.2, 7 (approximately). Now draw a bigger one with the same 35° angle and sides 10, 16.4, 14. The ratios opp/hyp, adj/hyp, opp/adj are the same in both: approximately 0.574, 0.819, 0.700. The size doesn't matter — only the angle determines the ratios. *Quick check:* In two similar right triangles with a 50° angle, triangle A has hypotenuse 10 and triangle B has hypotenuse 25. If opp in A = 7.66, what is opp in B?

**3. Defining sin, cos, tan through similarity.** Because the ratios are constant for a given angle θ, we can name them:
- sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse (same for all triangles with angle θ)
- cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse (same for all triangles with angle θ)
- tan θ = opposite / adjacent (same for all triangles with angle θ)

These are properties of the angle itself, not of any particular triangle. That is why a calculator can store a single value for sin 35° that works for every right triangle containing 35°. *Quick check:* If sin 28° = 0.469, what is the ratio opp/hyp in ANY right triangle containing a 28° angle?

**4. Verifying with measurement.** Draw three right-angled triangles all containing 45°: one with hypotenuse 5 cm, one with 10 cm, one with 15 cm. Measure opp and adj in each. Calculate opp/hyp for all three — you should get approximately 0.707 every time (sin 45° = √2/2 ≈ 0.707). The constancy is not a coincidence; it is a consequence of similarity. *Quick check:* What value should adj/hyp give for all three of those 45° triangles?

**5. Why this matters for indirect measurement.** Because sin, cos and tan are constant for a given angle regardless of size, we can use a small measured angle to find unknown lengths in very large triangles. A surveyor measuring the angle to the top of a building uses a triangle far too large to draw — but the ratio sin θ still applies because the surveyor's small drawn triangle is similar to the large real one. *Quick check:* Explain in one sentence why we can use sin 40° to find the height of a 100 m building even though our triangle diagram is only 10 cm tall.

**6. Worked example: proving constancy through similar triangles.**

Two right-angled triangles both have a 37° angle. Triangle 1 has hypotenuse 8 cm. Triangle 2 has hypotenuse 20 cm.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Both have angles 37°, 53°, 90° → they are similar (AA).
- Scale factor = 20/8 = 2.5.
- In triangle 1: opp = 8 × sin 37° = 8 × 0.6018 = 4.81 cm. adj = 8 × cos 37° = 6.39 cm.
- In triangle 2: opp = 20 × sin 37° = 20 × 0.6018 = 12.04 cm. adj = 20 × cos 37° = 15.97 cm.
- Check ratios: opp/hyp: 4.81/8 = 0.601. 12.04/20 = 0.602. ✓ Same (within rounding).
- The ratio is constant because the triangles are similar. The angle alone determines the ratio.

**7. Extending to all angles.** This principle works for every acute angle between 0° and 90°. Each angle has its own unique set of ratios (sin, cos, tan values). These values are tabulated or stored in calculators. The key insight: trigonometry works because geometry guarantees that similar triangles preserve ratios. Without similarity, trig would not generalise from one triangle to all triangles with the same angle. *Quick check:* Without looking up, which is larger: sin 60° or sin 30°? Explain using triangle shape.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Every surveyor, architect and engineer in Victoria relies on the constancy of trig ratios. When measuring the height of the Eureka Tower from street level, the surveyor's small instrument triangle is similar to the enormous building triangle — the same ratio applies regardless of scale. Builders across Melbourne check roof pitch consistency using the same principle: a small protractor triangle is similar to the full roof.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations builders and toolmakers implicitly relied on proportional constancy — scaling a boomerang design up or down maintains the critical angle relationships that determine flight. The consistent proportions in coolamon vessels across different sizes demonstrate that ratio constancy has practical applications independent of scale. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Navigational traditions across seafaring cultures (Polynesian, Greek, Arab, Chinese) all discovered that angle-to-side ratios are constant — this enabled navigation by the stars. The principle is universal because geometry is universal, independent of the cultural tradition that discovers it.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Draw three right-angled triangles with a 55° angle, using hypotenuses of 6 cm, 10 cm and 14 cm. Measure the opposite side in each. Calculate opp/hyp for all three. How close to sin 55° = 0.819 are your values?
2. **Civic.** A council surveyor needs to find the width of a river. She creates two similar right triangles — one small (measured on paper) and one large (the real river crossing). If her model has hypotenuse 15 cm and opposite 9.6 cm, and the real hypotenuse (line of sight) is 85 m, what is the river width?
3. **Vocational.** A carpenter checks that two different-sized roof trusses have the same pitch angle. Truss A: rise 2.1 m, run 5.0 m. Truss B: rise 4.2 m, run 10.0 m. Calculate tan θ for each. Are they similar triangles? Is the pitch constant?
4. **Ethical.** A student argues: "Trigonometry only works for specific triangle sizes — you can't apply a ratio from a small triangle to a massive building." Use the concept of similarity to explain clearly why this is incorrect.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Ancient Egyptian builders used a "seked" — the horizontal distance per unit of vertical rise (essentially 1/tan θ). If a pyramid has a seked of 5 palms per cubit rise (cubit = 7 palms), calculate the angle. Explain why this ratio works regardless of pyramid size.

## Memorable takeaways
- All right-angled triangles with the same acute angle are similar (AA criterion).
- Similar triangles → constant side ratios.
- This constancy IS sin, cos and tan. They depend only on the angle.
- Any size triangle with that angle gives the same ratios.
- This is why trigonometry works for indirect measurement at any scale.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I thought trig ratios depend on triangle size." They don't — similarity guarantees the ratio is constant for any given angle, regardless of how large or small the triangle is.
- "I forgot why they're constant." The answer is similarity (AA criterion). All right triangles with the same acute angle are similar, so side ratios are preserved.
- "I measured inaccurately and got different ratios." Measurement error affects results. The mathematical guarantee of constancy is exact — differences in measured values come from drawing/measuring imprecision, not from the mathematics.
- "I confused which sides correspond." In similar triangles, match the sides by their position relative to the equal angles, not by their absolute length.

A note on certainty. The constancy of trigonometric ratios for a given angle is a theorem, not an approximation. It follows logically from the properties of similar triangles, which are proven in Euclidean geometry. This result has been known since antiquity and underpins all of practical trigonometry.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Are they similar?* Yes — both have 90° and 40°, so the third angle must be 50°. Same angles → similar (AA).
2. *Opp in triangle B.* Scale factor = 25/10 = 2.5. Opp in B = 7.66 × 2.5 = 19.15.
3. *sin 28° = 0.469 → opp/hyp in any 28° right triangle?* Always 0.469, regardless of size.
4. *adj/hyp for 45° triangles.* cos 45° = √2/2 ≈ 0.707 (same as sin 45° because it's an isosceles right triangle).
5. *Why can we use sin 40° for a 100 m building?* Because the large real triangle and our small diagram are similar (same angles), so the side ratios are identical regardless of scale.
6. *sin 60° vs sin 30°.* sin 60° ≈ 0.866 is larger than sin 30° = 0.5. A steeper angle (closer to 90°) has a larger opposite relative to hypotenuse.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Measurement exercise.* With careful drawing: all three should give opp/hyp ≈ 0.819 ± measurement error (typically within 0.01–0.03 for careful work).
2. *River width.* Ratio = 9.6/15 = 0.64. River width = 85 × 0.64 = 54.4 m.
3. *Roof trusses.* A: tan θ = 2.1/5.0 = 0.42. B: tan θ = 4.2/10.0 = 0.42. Same ratio → same angle → similar triangles. Pitch is constant.
4. *Correcting the student.* By AA similarity, every right triangle with a given acute angle is similar to every other such triangle — their side ratios are mathematically guaranteed to be identical. Size is irrelevant because similarity preserves ratios by definition. The 10 cm diagram triangle is similar to the 300 m building triangle; therefore sin θ is the same in both.
5. *Egyptian seked.* Seked = 5/7 (per unit rise in the same units). tan θ = rise/run = 7/5 = 1.4. θ = tan⁻¹(1.4) ≈ 54.5°. This works at any scale because all pyramids with seked 5 form similar triangles.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** (a, b) Measured values should approximate sin 50° = 0.766, cos 50° = 0.643, tan 50° = 1.192 within ±0.02. (c) Errors come from protractor accuracy (± 1°), ruler reading (± 0.5 mm), and drawing precision. (d) If ABC ~ DEF with ratio k: DE = k×AB, EF = k×BC, DF = k×AC. sin θ from ABC = BC/AC. sin θ from DEF = EF/DF = kBC/kAC = BC/AC. The k cancels — ratio is identical. (e) sin θ/cos θ = (opp/hyp)/(adj/hyp) = opp/adj = tan θ. The hypotenuse cancels in the division.
