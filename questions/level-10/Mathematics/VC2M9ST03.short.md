# VC2M9ST03 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Represent the distribution of multiple data sets for numerical variables using comparative representations such as back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots and histograms; describe data, using terms including 'skewed', 'symmetric' and 'bi-modal'; compare data distributions using mean, median and range to describe and interpret numerical data sets with consideration of centre, spread and shape, and the effect of outliers on these measures.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Compare distributions using back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots and histograms. Shape vocabulary: symmetric, positively skewed, negatively skewed, bi-modal. Mean pulled by outliers; median resistant. Match your summary measure to the shape.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **represent and compare multiple data distributions** using back-to-back displays and summary statistics.
- You should describe shape, compare centre and spread, and explain the effect of outliers.

## Core content

**Comparative displays.** Back-to-back stem-and-leaf: shared stem, leaves spread left and right. Comparative histograms: same scale, side-by-side. Both reveal shape, centre, spread for direct comparison.

**Distribution shape.** Symmetric: mirror image, mean ≈ median. Positively skewed: tail right, mean > median (income, prices). Negatively skewed: tail left, mean < median. Bi-modal: two peaks — suggests two subgroups.

**Centre and outliers.** Mean: affected by every value, pulled toward extremes. Median: resistant to outliers. Example: $450K, $480K, $510K, $520K, $2.1M → mean = $812K, median = $510K. Median far better for "typical."

**Spread.** Range = max − min. Highly sensitive to outliers (one extreme value inflates dramatically). Compare spread alongside centre for full picture.

**Worked example.** Melbourne July temps (mean 12.3°C, symmetric, range 7) vs Brisbane July (mean 21.2°C, symmetric, range 5). Brisbane: higher centre, less variable. Both symmetric → mean and range appropriate.

**Choosing measures.** Symmetric → mean and range. Skewed → median and IQR. Bi-modal → consider separating subgroups. Always use same measures for both groups in a comparison.

**Victorian context.** BOM compares temperature distributions across Victorian regions. Melbourne's "four seasons in one day" = wide spread. First Nations data often appears bi-modal when combining urban/remote — treating as one group masks important variation. Data sovereignty means communities choosing what comparisons are meaningful.

## Memorable takeaways
- Back-to-back stem-and-leaf: leaves read outward from the stem.
- Shape: symmetric, positively skewed, negatively skewed, bi-modal.
- Symmetric → mean ≈ median. Skewed → mean pulled toward tail.
- Outliers inflate mean and range; barely affect median.
- Match your measure to the shape.

## Watch out for these traps
- Left-side leaves read outward (right to left) from the stem.
- Don't use mean for skewed data — it doesn't represent "typical."
- Two distributions with same mean can have completely different shapes.
- One outlier can double the range — always comment on this.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *House prices: mean $812K, median $510K.* Median better — one $2.1M outlier inflates mean by $300K above typical.
- *Heights: symmetric.* Mean ≈ median, either works. Adult heights are classic symmetric distribution.
- *Marathon times: positively skewed.* Most cluster 4–5 hours, tail stretches toward 6–7. Median better than mean.
- *Same mean, different spread.* Factory machines both at 50.0 mm but one wider — wider spread means more rejects. Spread matters for quality.
- *Fire station: skewed response times.* Median (6.5 min) better than mean (8 min). Skewness caused by occasional distant calls — most responses are fast.
