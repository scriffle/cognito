# VC2M9ST01 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Analyse reports of surveys in digital media and elsewhere for information on how data was obtained around everyday questions and issues involving at least one numerical and at least one categorical variable, to estimate population means and medians.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Survey reports need critical analysis: how was data collected, from whom, how many? Numerical (income, hours) and categorical (gender, region) variables are both present. Sample statistics estimate population parameters — accuracy depends on size and representativeness.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse survey reports** by examining collection methods, variables, and how sample statistics estimate population values.
- You should distinguish sample statistics from population parameters and evaluate reliability.

## Core content

**Variables.** Numerical: measured in numbers (income, time, age). Categorical: classified in groups (state, gender, preference). Most surveys have both.

**How data was obtained.** Check: who conducted? Population? Sample selection method? Size? Response rate? When?

**Sample estimates population.** Sample mean (x̄) estimates population mean (μ). Accuracy depends on sample size AND representativeness. Biased sample → biased estimate regardless of size.

**Mean vs median.** Skewed data (income, prices): mean pulled toward extremes, median stays central. Media may choose strategically. "Average" could be either.

**Worked example.** "Victorians spend $85/week on groceries" — surveyed 1200 in-store shoppers at one chain. Bias: excludes online shoppers, market shoppers, other stores. Likely overestimates true population mean.

**Evaluating reports.** Check source, methodology, sample size. Note conflicts of interest. Headlines exaggerate. Always: who, how many, how selected, what's missing?

**Victorian context.** ABS Census = gold standard. Victorian media reports surveys on housing, health, attitudes. Critical analysis of survey data about Aboriginal communities supports data sovereignty — communities should control how data is collected and reported.

## Memorable takeaways
- Sample mean estimates population mean — it's not the exact answer.
- Accuracy needs representative sample, not just large sample.
- Always ask: who, how many, how selected, what's missing?
- Mean vs median matters for skewed data.
- Biased sample → biased estimate, no matter the size.

## Watch out for these traps
- Confusing sample statistic with population parameter (estimate ≠ truth).
- Large sample doesn't fix bias (million-person online poll still biased).
- "Average" in media could be mean or median — ask which.
- Low response rate = missing voices (non-respondents may differ systematically).

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *State of residence = categorical. Income = numerical.* Correct identification.
- *Website poll (2000 responses).* Voluntary, self-selected — not representative.
- *50 students, one school → all Aus teenagers?* Not representative (one area, one SES level).
- *"Average house price $1.2M" — median?* Lower. Prices positively skewed — few expensive outliers pull mean up.
- *62% ± 4% confidence.* True value likely 58%–66%.
