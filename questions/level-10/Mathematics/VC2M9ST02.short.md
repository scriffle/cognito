# VC2M9ST02 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Analyse how different sampling methods, and different samples using the same method, can affect the results of surveys and how choice of representation can be used to support a particular point of view.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Different sampling methods (random, stratified, convenience, voluntary) produce different results. Even same method gives different estimates each time (sampling variability). Representation choices (axis scale, graph type, statistic reported) can support any point of view.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse how sampling methods affect results** and how representation choices support particular points of view.
- You should compare methods, recognise variability, and critically evaluate graphs and statistics in media.

## Core content

**Sampling methods.** Random (equal chance), stratified (proportional from subgroups), convenience (whoever's easy), voluntary (self-selected), systematic (every kth). Each produces different samples from the same population.

**Method affects results.** Voluntary attracts strong opinions. Convenience over-represents accessible groups. Random reduces bias. Example: school uniform poll — voluntary respondents (angry students) show 80% opposed; random sample shows 55% opposed.

**Sampling variability.** Same method, different sample → different estimate. Natural, not error. Larger samples have less variability. Two random samples of 50 giving means of 4.1 and 3.7 — both valid estimates.

**Representation choices.** Same data, different story: truncated y-axis (small difference looks huge), full y-axis (difference invisible), mean vs median (different "typical" values), selective time periods, 3D distortion.

**Worked example — two stories.** Teacher salaries: VIC $92K, NSW $95K. Y-axis $85K–$96K: Victoria looks far behind. Y-axis $0–$100K: almost identical. Same data, different visual conclusions.

**Misleading techniques.** Truncated axes, 3D graphs, cherry-picked periods, pictographs (doubling height quadruples area), unlabelled axes, selective reporting.

**Victorian context.** VEC election polling debates (phone vs online methods). Herald Sun vs The Age present same economic data with different impressions. First Nations data sovereignty — communities historically undersampled, results misrepresented through deficit framing. Multilingual communities undersampled by English-only phone surveys.

## Memorable takeaways
- Different methods → systematically different results.
- Same method, different sample → natural variability (not error).
- Larger samples reduce variability but don't fix method bias.
- Graphs can tell any story — check axes, scale, and what's omitted.
- Always ask: who, how, and what representation choices were made?

## Watch out for these traps
- "Random" doesn't mean "haphazard" — it requires systematic process.
- Large biased sample is still biased (million-person voluntary poll).
- Sampling variability ≠ sampling bias (variability shrinks with size; bias doesn't).
- Always check y-axis starting point before interpreting visual differences.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Radio call-in vs random phone survey.* Call-in = voluntary, attracts strong opinions. Random phone captures broader range including moderates.
- *Two samples: means 4.1 and 3.7.* Both valid. Natural variability — neither is "wrong."
- *5% profit growth — two graphs.* Truncated axis: bar nearly doubles visually. Full axis: barely visible change. Same 5%.
- *Unemployment 5.2% → 5.0%, axis from 4.8%.* Looks like unemployment halved. Axis from 0%: barely visible drop. Difference is 0.2 percentage points either way.
- *Cycling event survey about bike lanes.* Massive bias — cyclists are pro-bike. Better: stratified random of all residents.
