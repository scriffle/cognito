## Core content

**1. Bivariate data and scatterplots.** Bivariate data consists of ordered pairs (x, y) measuring two variables on the same individuals. A scatterplot displays these pairs as points on a coordinate plane. The independent variable (explanatory) is on the x-axis; the dependent variable (response) is on the y-axis. The pattern of points reveals the relationship: linear, non-linear, or no association. *Quick check:* A study measures hours of study (x) and test score (y) for 20 students. Which variable goes on which axis?

**2. Types of association.** Positive association: as x increases, y tends to increase. Negative association: as x increases, y tends to decrease. No association: no discernible pattern. Linear: points cluster around a straight line. Non-linear: points follow a curve. The strength of association refers to how tightly points cluster around the trend. *Quick check:* Describe the expected association between temperature and ice cream sales.

**3. Line of best fit.** A line of best fit (trend line) summarises the linear relationship. By eye: draw a line that passes through the "middle" of the data, with roughly equal numbers of points above and below. Using technology: the least-squares regression line minimises the sum of squared vertical distances from points to the line. The equation takes the form ŷ = a + bx, where b is the slope and a is the y-intercept. *Quick check:* A line of best fit has equation ŷ = 20 + 3x. What does the slope of 3 mean in context?

**4. Interpreting slope and intercept.** The slope b represents the predicted change in y for each one-unit increase in x. The y-intercept a is the predicted value of y when x = 0 (which may or may not be meaningful in context). For study hours vs test score: ŷ = 45 + 5x means each additional hour of study is associated with a 5-point increase in test score, and a student who studies 0 hours is predicted to score 45. *Quick check:* For ŷ = 100 − 2x (where x = age of car in years, y = value in $000s), interpret slope and intercept.

**5. The correlation coefficient r.** The correlation coefficient r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. −1 ≤ r ≤ 1. r = 1: perfect positive linear relationship. r = −1: perfect negative linear relationship. r = 0: no linear relationship. Guidelines: |r| > 0.8 strong, 0.5–0.8 moderate, 0.3–0.5 weak, < 0.3 very weak or none. r only measures linear association — a strong curved relationship may have r close to 0. *Quick check:* A data set has r = −0.85. Describe the relationship.

**6. Worked example — bivariate analysis.**
Data: height (cm) and shoe size for 8 people: (160, 7), (165, 8), (170, 9), (172, 9), (175, 10), (178, 10), (180, 11), (185, 12).

*Analysis:*
- Scatterplot shows a clear positive linear trend.
- Line of best fit (by technology): ŷ = −23.5 + 0.19x.
- Slope: each 1 cm increase in height is associated with a 0.19 increase in shoe size.
- r ≈ 0.98 — very strong positive linear relationship.
- Interpretation: height and shoe size are strongly positively correlated; taller people tend to have larger shoe sizes.

**7. Correlation vs causation.** Correlation does not imply causation. Two variables may be correlated because: (a) one causes the other, (b) both are caused by a third variable (confounding), or (c) the correlation is coincidental. Ice cream sales and drowning rates are positively correlated — both are driven by hot weather (confounding variable), not by each other. Establishing causation requires controlled experiments or rigorous study design. *Quick check:* A study finds a positive correlation between screen time and headaches. Does this prove screen time causes headaches? Explain.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Bivariate data analysis is fundamental to Victorian research in health, education and environmental science. The Bureau of Meteorology uses regression analysis to model climate trends. VCE Further Mathematics and Mathematical Methods develop these skills extensively.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations peoples have long observed relationships between paired environmental variables — rainfall and plant growth, moon phases and tidal patterns, temperature and animal behaviour. The statistical formalisation of these paired observations connects to millennia of ecological knowledge.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The correlation coefficient was developed by Karl Pearson (1896) building on Francis Galton's work on regression. The least-squares method was independently developed by Legendre (1805) and Gauss (1809).

## Memorable takeaways
- Scatterplot: plot (x, y) pairs to visualise bivariate relationships.
- Line of best fit: ŷ = a + bx. Slope b = change in y per unit change in x.
- Correlation coefficient r: −1 to 1, measures strength and direction of linear association.
- |r| > 0.8 strong, 0.5–0.8 moderate, < 0.5 weak.
- Correlation ≠ causation — always consider confounding variables.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Confusing correlation with causation.** A strong r does not mean one variable causes the other.
- **Extrapolating beyond the data.** Predictions outside the range of observed x-values are unreliable.
- **Ignoring non-linear patterns.** r only measures linear association — a perfect parabola may have r ≈ 0.
- **Misinterpreting the intercept.** The y-intercept may be meaningless if x = 0 is outside the data range.
