## Core content

**1. Choosing a model.** Ask: is the rate of change constant or changing? **Linear** (y = mx + c): constant rate of change — salary growth at a fixed annual raise, distance at constant speed, simple interest. **Quadratic** (y = ax² + bx + c): rate of change itself changes — area as dimensions grow, projectile motion, braking distance. Not sure? Plot the data and look at the shape. *Quick check:* A tree grows 30 cm per year. Is a linear or quadratic model appropriate for its height over time?

**2. Simple interest.** Simple interest is calculated on the original principal only: **I = Prt** where I = interest, P = principal (initial amount), r = annual interest rate (as a decimal), t = time in years. Total amount = P + I = P(1 + rt). This is linear in t — the graph of total amount vs time is a straight line. *Quick check:* Find the simple interest on $2 000 at 4% per year for 3 years.

**3. Simple interest applications.** Compare savings options: $5 000 at 3.5% simple interest for 4 years gives I = 5 000 × 0.035 × 4 = $700. Total = $5 700. For a loan: borrowing $8 000 at 6% for 2 years costs I = 8 000 × 0.06 × 2 = $960 in interest. *Quick check:* How long will it take $1 000 to earn $150 in simple interest at 5% per year?

**4. Linear modelling.** A taxi charges $4.50 flagfall plus $2.10 per km. Model: C = 4.50 + 2.10d. This predicts cost at any distance. To find when cost reaches $50: 50 = 4.50 + 2.10d → d = 45.50/2.10 ≈ 21.7 km. Assumptions: fixed rate per km (no surge pricing, no traffic delays). *Quick check:* A gym charges $60 joining fee plus $15 per week. Write the model and find the cost after 20 weeks.

**5. Quadratic modelling.** A ball is kicked with height h = −5t² + 20t. Maximum height: vertex at t = −20/(2×(−5)) = 2 s. h(2) = −20 + 40 = 20 m. Lands: −5t(t − 4) = 0 → t = 4 s. Assumptions: no wind, flat ground, ball treated as a point. The model is valid only for 0 ≤ t ≤ 4. *Quick check:* A rectangular area A = x(30 − x) = −x² + 30x is maximum at what value of x?

**6. Worked example: saving for a trip.**

You want to save for a $3 000 trip in 2 years. A bank offers 4.5% simple interest.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Total needed: $3 000. Time: 2 years. Rate: 4.5% = 0.045.
- Let P = principal to invest. After 2 years: P(1 + 0.045 × 2) = P(1.09) = 3 000.
- P = 3 000/1.09 ≈ $2 752.29.
- You need to deposit approximately $2 752 today to have $3 000 in 2 years.
- Interest earned: 3 000 − 2 752.29 ≈ $247.71.
- Assumption: the interest rate stays fixed for 2 years. In reality, rates can change.
- Evaluation: this is a simple model. Compound interest (not simple) is what banks typically offer — but simple interest gives a useful first approximation.

**7. The modelling cycle and reporting.** (1) Formulate: translate the problem into mathematics. (2) Solve: find the answer algebraically or graphically. (3) Interpret: translate back to the real-world context. (4) Evaluate: does the answer make sense? Are the assumptions reasonable? What does the model miss? (5) Report: state assumptions, methods, findings and limitations. A good report includes what you assumed, what you found, whether you believe the model is accurate, and what could improve it. *Quick check:* You model a car's fuel use as F = 8d (litres per 100 km × distance). Name one assumption and one limitation.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Simple interest calculations are relevant to savings accounts, term deposits and personal loans offered by Australian banks. The Victorian Curriculum explicitly includes financial mathematics for consumer awareness. Melbourne's public transport pricing (Myki) uses a linear model within zones. VicRoads models braking distance as a quadratic function of speed — critical for speed-limit decisions.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations sustainable resource management involves modelling growth and change — monitoring fish stocks at Budj Bim requires understanding whether populations grow linearly or follow more complex patterns. The planning of cultural burns uses models of how fire spread changes with conditions. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Financial literacy is important across all communities. Understanding simple interest helps families make informed borrowing and saving decisions. Small business owners across Melbourne's diverse communities model revenue as linear functions and break-even points using quadratic or piecewise models.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You deposit $1 500 in a savings account at 3.2% simple interest per year. How much will you have after 4 years? How long until you double your money at this rate?
2. **Civic.** A council models the population of a new suburb as P = 200 + 150t (where t = years after establishment). Sketch the model for 10 years. In what year will the population reach 2 000? Discuss whether a linear model is realistic long-term for population.
3. **Vocational.** A plumber charges $110 call-out plus $85 per hour. A second plumber charges $70 call-out plus $95 per hour. Write both linear models and find when they cost the same. Which is cheaper for a 3-hour job?
4. **Ethical.** A payday lender advertises "only $5 per $100 per week" (which is simple interest at 260% per year). Compare borrowing $500 for 4 weeks from this lender versus a bank personal loan at 12% per year simple interest. Discuss predatory lending.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A family is comparing two ways to save for a child's education: (a) save $200/month for 10 years (linear accumulation), (b) invest $15 000 now at 5% simple interest for 10 years. Which gives more at the end of 10 years? What assumptions does each model make?

## Watch out for these traps
- "I used the interest rate as a percentage in the formula." Convert to a decimal: 4% = 0.04, not 4.
- "I forgot to check whether my answer makes sense in context." A negative time, a distance greater than physically possible, or an interest amount larger than the principal all signal errors. Always interpret.
- "I assumed linear when the situation is quadratic." If the rate of change itself is changing (speeding up, slowing down, growing areas), a linear model will be wrong.
- "I didn't state my assumptions." Every model simplifies reality. If you don't state what you assumed, you can't evaluate whether the model is appropriate.

A note on certainty. Simple interest is defined mathematics — the formula always works as stated. However, most real-world savings accounts use compound interest (interest on interest), so simple interest is a useful approximation that slightly underestimates real growth. All models simplify reality; their value lies in being "useful enough" for the purpose.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Savings.* After 4 years: A = 1 500(1 + 0.032 × 4) = 1 500 × 1.128 = $1 692. To double: 3 000 = 1 500(1 + 0.032t) → 2 = 1.032t → t = 1/0.032 = 31.25 years.
