## What you are learning
- You are learning to **solve problems involving the surface area and volume of composite objects** using appropriate units.
- You are learning to decompose complex 3D objects into standard components and account for shared surfaces.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify the component solids within a composite object.
- Calculate volume by adding (or subtracting) component volumes.
- Calculate surface area by summing exposed faces and subtracting hidden (joined) surfaces.
- Apply formulas for prisms, cylinders, cones, spheres and hemispheres.
- Use appropriate units and convert where needed.

## Core content

**1. Composite objects.** A **composite object** is made by joining or cutting standard 3D shapes. A silo (cylinder + hemisphere), a house cross-section (rectangular prism + triangular prism), a bolt (cylinder + cone) — all are composites. *Quick check:* Name two standard solids that form an ice-cream cone with a scoop on top.

**2. Key volume formulas.** Rectangular prism: V = lwh. Cylinder: V = πr²h. Cone: V = ⅓πr²h. Sphere: V = ⁴⁄₃πr³. Hemisphere: V = ⅔πr³. Triangular prism: V = ½bh × L. *Quick check:* What is the volume of a hemisphere with radius 6 cm?

**3. Composite volume — addition.** A grain silo is a cylinder (r = 3 m, h = 8 m) topped by a hemisphere (r = 3 m). V = πr²h + ⅔πr³ = π(9)(8) + ⅔π(27) = 72π + 18π = 90π ≈ 282.7 m³. *Quick check:* A rocket is a cylinder (r = 2, h = 10) with a cone (r = 2, h = 3) on top. Find the total volume.

**4. Composite volume — subtraction.** A solid block (10 × 8 × 6 cm) with a cylindrical hole (r = 2 cm) drilled through the 6 cm depth. V = 10 × 8 × 6 − π(4)(6) = 480 − 24π ≈ 404.6 cm³. *Quick check:* A cube of side 5 cm has a hemisphere (r = 2 cm) scooped from one face. What is the remaining volume?

**5. Surface area of composite objects.** Add all exposed faces. Where two shapes join, subtract the shared surface (it is inside, not exposed). Silo example: lateral surface of cylinder + curved surface of hemisphere + circular base. The top circle of the cylinder is hidden (joined to the hemisphere), so do not count it. *Quick check:* A cylinder with a cone on top — which circular face is hidden?

**6. Worked example: surface area of a storage tank.**

A water tank is a cylinder (r = 2.5 m, height = 4 m) with a hemispherical dome on top.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Lateral area of cylinder = 2πrh = 2π(2.5)(4) = 20π m².
- Base circle = πr² = 6.25π m².
- Curved surface of hemisphere = 2πr² = 2π(6.25) = 12.5π m².
- Hidden surface (where hemisphere sits on cylinder) = πr² = 6.25π — do NOT include.
- Total SA = 20π + 6.25π + 12.5π = 38.75π ≈ 121.7 m².

**7. Choosing units and converting.** Volume in cm³, m³ or litres (1 m³ = 1 000 L). Surface area in cm² or m². For paint coverage (m²) or material costs, surface area is the key measure. For capacity or material quantity, volume. *Quick check:* A tank has volume 5.4 m³. What is its capacity in litres?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne's iconic water towers (e.g. the Collingwood water tower) are composite shapes — cylindrical tanks with conical or domed roofs. Architects at the NGV calculated surface areas for cladding the angular composite forms of Federation Square. Melbourne Water's reservoir embankments involve composite cross-sections (trapezoidal prism bases) requiring volume calculations for earthworks.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Coolamon (carrying vessels) and canoes carved by First Nations craftspeople are composite-form objects — combining curved and flat surfaces. Understanding volume and surface area helps appreciate the engineering precision involved. The Gunditjmara stone houses at Budj Bim have composite footprints whose internal volumes (living space) can be modelled using prism and hemisphere components. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Industrial design across Melbourne's manufacturing sector (packaging, tanks, machinery housings) requires composite surface-area and volume calculations. International architecture (domes, minarets, pagodas) visible in Melbourne demonstrates composite 3D forms.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A thermos flask is a cylinder (r = 4 cm, h = 20 cm) with a hemispherical base (r = 4 cm). Calculate the total internal volume (capacity in mL).
2. **Civic.** The City of Banyule commissions a public sculpture: a rectangular plinth (1.2 m × 1.2 m × 0.6 m) topped by a sphere of radius 0.5 m. Calculate the total volume and the total exposed surface area (the sphere sits on the plinth, hiding one circular contact area).
3. **Vocational.** A fabricator needs to make a steel hopper: a cylinder (r = 1.5 m, h = 2 m) on top of an inverted cone (r = 1.5 m, slant height = 2.5 m). Calculate the total surface area of steel sheet required (open top, no base).
4. **Ethical.** A company markets a "jumbo" ice-cream cone as 50% bigger than the regular. The regular is a cone (r = 3, h = 12 cm) plus a hemisphere (r = 3). The jumbo has r = 3.5, h = 14, hemisphere r = 3.5. Calculate both volumes and check whether the "50% bigger" claim is accurate.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A mosque dome in Melbourne is a hemisphere (r = 8 m) sitting on a cylindrical drum (r = 8 m, h = 3 m). Calculate the volume of internal air space and the external surface area to be tiled (hemisphere outer surface + cylinder lateral surface).

## Memorable takeaways
- Decompose: identify the standard solids within the composite.
- Volume: add components (or subtract cut-outs).
- Surface area: add exposed faces, subtract hidden (joined) surfaces.
- Know your formulas: prism, cylinder, cone, sphere, hemisphere.
- Units: volume in m³ or cm³; SA in m² or cm²; 1 m³ = 1 000 L.

## Stretch yourself
A swimming pool has a rectangular main body (25 m × 10 m × 1.8 m deep) with a semicylindrical wading area (radius 5 m, same 1.8 m depth) attached at one short end. Calculate: (a) the total volume of water when full, (b) the total internal surface area to be tiled (floor + all walls, including the curved wall of the semicylinder), (c) the cost of tiling at $95 per m². Note: the wall where the semicylinder meets the rectangle is open (no tile there). Show all working and state which surfaces are hidden. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I included the hidden face in the surface area." Where two shapes join, that circle (or rectangle) is internal — do not count it as exposed surface.
- "I forgot the base." Many composite objects still have a bottom face. Check whether it is exposed or sits on the ground (not painted/tiled).
- "I used diameter instead of radius." Many formulas use r. If given diameter, halve it first.
- "I added surface areas without thinking about which faces are exposed." Always sketch the composite and mark which faces are visible from outside.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Ice-cream cone + scoop.* Cone + hemisphere (or sphere).
2. *Hemisphere r = 6 cm.* V = ⅔π(6³) = ⅔π(216) = 144π ≈ 452.4 cm³.
3. *Rocket.* Cylinder V = π(4)(10) = 40π. Cone V = ⅓π(4)(3) = 4π. Total = 44π ≈ 138.2 cubic units.
4. *Cube with hemisphere scooped.* V = 5³ − ⅔π(2³) = 125 − ⅔π(8) = 125 − 16π/3 ≈ 125 − 16.76 ≈ 108.2 cm³.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Thermos.* Cylinder V = π(16)(20) = 320π. Hemisphere V = ⅔π(64) = 128π/3. Total = 320π + 128π/3 = (960π + 128π)/3 = 1088π/3 ≈ 1 139 cm³ = 1 139 mL.

That is 59% bigger — the "50% bigger" claim understates the actual difference slightly.
5. *Mosque dome.* Volume: hemisphere = ⅔π(512) = 341.3π ≈ 1 072 m³. Cylinder = π(64)(3) = 192π ≈ 603 m³. Total ≈ 1 675 m³. External SA: hemisphere = 2π(64) = 128π ≈ 402 m². Cylinder lateral = 2π(8)(3) = 48π ≈ 151 m². Total ≈ 553 m².
