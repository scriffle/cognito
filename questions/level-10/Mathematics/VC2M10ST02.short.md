# VC2M10ST02 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Construct scatterplots and consider a line of good fit; comment on the association between the 2 numerical variables in terms of strength, direction and linearity.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Scatterplots show relationships between two numerical variables. Association is described by direction (positive/negative), strength (strong/moderate/weak) and form (linear/non-linear). A line of good fit approximates the trend. Correlation does not imply causation.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct scatterplots**, draw lines of good fit, and describe association between two variables.
- You should distinguish correlation from causation and make cautious predictions.

## Core content

**Constructing scatterplots.** Explanatory variable on x-axis, response on y-axis. One point per data pair.

**Direction.** Positive: x up → y up. Negative: x up → y down. None: no pattern.

**Strength.** Strong: tight cluster around trend. Moderate: visible trend with scatter. Weak: vague trend.

**Linearity.** Linear: straight trend (line appropriate). Non-linear: curved trend (line inappropriate).

**Line of good fit.** Passes through middle of data, roughly equal points above and below. Gradient = rate of change in context. Use for prediction — but only within data range (interpolation, not extrapolation).

**Worked example.** Study hours vs marks: strong positive linear. Line through (1,42) and (10,88): gradient ≈ 5.1. Each extra hour ≈ 5 more marks. Predict 12 hours → 98 marks — but caution, this extrapolates beyond data.

**Correlation ≠ causation.** Ice cream sales and drowning both rise in summer — confounding variable (hot weather), not causation. Always consider third variables.

**Victorian context.** CSIRO plots environmental relationships (rainfall vs yield). Bureau of Meteorology tracks climate variable associations. Understanding scatterplots in media about Aboriginal communities helps evaluate whether causation claims are justified. Bivariate data appears in VCE biology, geography and PE.

## Memorable takeaways
- Direction: positive, negative, or none.
- Strength: strong, moderate, weak (how tightly clustered).
- Form: linear or non-linear.
- Line of good fit: for prediction within data range only.
- Correlation ≠ causation — look for confounding variables.

## Watch out for these traps
- Claiming causation from correlation — always consider confounders.
- Drawing a line through curved data — check linearity first.
- Extrapolating beyond data range — unreliable predictions.
- Wrong axis choice — explanatory (input) on x, response (output) on y.
