# VC2M10ST05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10ST05. Plan and conduct statistical investigations of situations that involve bivariate data, including where the independent variable is time; evaluate and report findings.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A statistical investigation follows a cycle: plan, collect, process, analyse, evaluate, report.
  - **Bivariate data** involves two variables measured on the same subjects — often to investigate whether a relationship exists.
  - **Time-series data** is bivariate with time as the independent variable — it reveals trends, seasonal patterns and irregular variation.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; ICT Capability; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan and conduct statistical investigations** involving bivariate data, including time-series data.
- You are learning to evaluate your findings and report them clearly with appropriate cautions.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Formulate a statistical question involving two variables.
- Plan data collection (identify variables, determine sample, choose method).
- Construct appropriate displays (scatterplots, time-series graphs).
- Analyse the data (describe association, identify trends).
- Evaluate findings (consider limitations, bias, representativeness).
- Report with conclusions linked to evidence.

## Core content

**1. Formulating a question.** A good statistical question is investigable, involves variability, and specifies the population. Example: "Is there a relationship between hours of study and test performance for Year 10 students at our school?" Bivariate = two variables; time-series adds time as one variable. *Quick check:* Turn this into a bivariate investigation question: "screen time and sleep."

**2. Planning data collection.** Decide: What variables? (Types — continuous numerical, categorical?) Who is the population? How will you sample? (Random, stratified, systematic?) What sample size is appropriate? What is the collection method (survey, observation, existing data)? Consider ethics: informed consent, anonymity, sensitivity. *Quick check:* You investigate temperature and ice cream sales. Where might you get this data? What is the independent variable?

**3. Time-series data.** When the independent variable is time, the data is a time series. Plot time on x-axis, the measured variable on y-axis. Connect points to show the trend. Features: **trend** (overall direction — increasing, decreasing, stable), **seasonal variation** (regular repeating patterns within each year/cycle), **irregular variation** (unexpected fluctuations). *Quick check:* Monthly rainfall data over 5 years. What would you expect to see as a seasonal pattern?

**4. Displaying bivariate data.** Scatterplots for two numerical variables. Time-series graphs for data over time (connected line). Add a line/curve of good fit if appropriate. Digital tools (spreadsheets, Desmos) can calculate and display regression lines. *Quick check:* Which display — scatterplot or time-series graph — for investigating relationship between altitude and temperature?

**5. Analysing and interpreting.** For scatterplots: describe direction, strength, form (linear/non-linear). For time series: identify trend direction and seasonal patterns. Always interpret in context. Make predictions cautiously — within data range (interpolation) is more reliable than beyond it (extrapolation). *Quick check:* A time series of school enrolments shows an upward trend with a regular dip in December. What is the trend? What is the seasonal pattern?

**6. Worked example — investigating temperature over time.**
Question: Has Melbourne's average temperature changed over the past 50 years?

*Follow the thinking:*
- Data source: Bureau of Meteorology (BOM) annual average temperatures for Melbourne, 1973–2023.
- Display: time-series graph (year on x-axis, temperature on y-axis).
- Analysis: upward trend visible — average temperatures have increased by approximately 1°C over 50 years. Year-to-year variation (irregular) makes individual years unpredictable, but the overall trend is clear.
- Seasonal component: if monthly data used, clear pattern (high in Jan, low in Jul).
- Evaluation: data is comprehensive (BOM covers all of Melbourne), measurement methods have been consistent, trend aligns with broader climate science.
- Conclusion: "Melbourne's average temperature shows a clear upward trend over 50 years, consistent with global warming patterns."
- Limitation: urban heat island effect may contribute locally.

**7. Evaluating and reporting.** Address: (1) Are findings consistent with expectations? (2) What are the limitations (sample size, bias, measurement error)? (3) Could other factors explain the results? (4) How confident are you in generalising? Report structure: question → method → findings (with displays) → conclusion → limitations. *Quick check:* You find a strong positive association between ice cream sales and drowning. What limitation or confounder would you report?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Bureau of Meteorology publishes Victorian climate time-series data that students can access freely. Melbourne Water tracks dam storage levels over time. VicRoads monitors traffic volume trends. The ABS provides time-series data on Victorian population, employment, and housing. VCE students conduct statistical investigations as assessment tasks — this descriptor directly supports those skills.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Time-series data about First Nations communities (health outcomes, education rates, employment over decades) tells stories of resilience, recovery and ongoing challenges. Conducting ethical research WITH communities (not about them) is essential. The principle of data sovereignty means communities should determine what questions are asked about their data. Seasonal ecological knowledge — knowing what happens in Country at different times of year — is a form of time-series understanding maintained for millennia. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Students can investigate bivariate data relevant to their own lives: family migration patterns over time, price trends for specific goods, or participation rates in cultural activities. Time-series data tells the story of change — every community has stories that can be told through data over time.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Plan an investigation: "Is there a relationship between daily exercise (minutes) and mood rating (1–10) for me over 3 weeks?" Collect data, plot a scatterplot, and describe the association.
2. **Civic.** Access monthly unemployment data for Victoria from the ABS (or use provided data). Create a time-series graph for the past 5 years. Describe the trend and any seasonal patterns. Identify the impact of any significant events (e.g. COVID-19).
3. **Vocational.** A business tracks monthly sales over 2 years. Plot the time series. Is there a seasonal pattern (e.g. peaks at Christmas)? What is the overall trend? How could this help with stock ordering?
4. **Ethical.** You investigate whether students who use social media more have lower grades. What ethical considerations apply to this investigation? How would you collect data respectfully? What limitations would you report?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Investigate time-series data on Australia's immigration numbers over the past 30 years. Plot the data. Describe the trend and any notable changes. Link changes to historical events. What does this data NOT tell you about the experiences of migrants?

## Memorable takeaways
- Investigation cycle: question → plan → collect → display → analyse → evaluate → report.
- Bivariate: two variables measured on same subjects.
- Time series: time on x-axis, identifies trends and seasonal patterns.
- Always evaluate: limitations, confounders, representativeness.
- Report conclusions linked to evidence, with appropriate caution.

## Stretch yourself
Design and conduct a full statistical investigation using bivariate data that includes a time component. Example: track your daily water intake and productivity rating over 4 weeks. Formulate a clear question, plan your method, collect data systematically, create appropriate displays (time-series graph AND scatterplot), analyse trends and associations, evaluate limitations (is 4 weeks enough? Is self-reported data reliable? What confounders exist?), and write a formal report (300+ words). Include: a clear conclusion linked to your data, at least one graph, discussion of at least two limitations, and suggestions for improving the investigation. Consider: would a longer time period change your conclusions? How would you test whether your findings apply to other people? 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting to plan before collecting.** Define variables, sample, and method BEFORE gathering data — otherwise you may collect unusable data.
- **Confusing trend with seasonal pattern.** Trend = overall long-term direction. Seasonal = regular repeated cycle within that trend.
- **Not connecting time-series points.** Time-series data should be connected with lines (showing progression), unlike scatterplots (individual points).
- **Overstating conclusions.** "My data proves..." is too strong. Use "my data suggests..." or "there appears to be..."
- **Ignoring limitations.** Every investigation has limitations — acknowledging them strengthens your work, not weakens it.

A note on certainty. The investigation process (plan, collect, analyse, report) is a well-established methodology. Individual findings depend on data quality, sample characteristics, and analytical choices — two researchers investigating the same question may reach different conclusions if they make different methodological decisions. This is why evaluation and transparency (reporting methods and limitations) are essential parts of the process.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Screen time question.* "Is there a relationship between daily screen time (hours) and hours of sleep for Year 10 students?"
2. *Ice cream data.* Source: existing data from a shop or weather bureau + sales records. Independent variable: temperature (or time, if tracking seasonally).
3. *Rainfall seasonal pattern.* Higher rainfall in winter/spring, lower in summer/autumn (for Melbourne). Regular repeating cycle each year.
4. *Altitude vs temperature.* Scatterplot — altitude is a continuous variable, not a time measurement.
5. *School enrolments.* Trend: upward (growing school). Seasonal pattern: dip in December (end of school year, some leavers counted differently, gap between years).
6. *Melbourne temperature.* Upward trend ≈ 1°C over 50 years (worked through in example).
7. *Ice cream and drowning.* Confounder: hot weather increases both. Report: "The association is likely explained by temperature as a confounding variable rather than a causal relationship between the two."

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Exercise and mood.* Plan: record minutes of exercise and mood (1–10) daily for 21 days. Display: scatterplot. Expected: moderate positive association (more exercise → higher mood). Limitations: self-reported mood is subjective; other factors affect mood; 21 days is short.
2. *Unemployment time series.* Typical pattern: seasonal variation (higher in January when contracts end), overall trend depends on period. COVID-19: sharp spike in 2020, recovery through 2021–22. Display shows both the trend and the disruption clearly.
3. *Business sales.* Seasonal: peaks December–January (holiday spending). Trend: overall growth if business is successful. Use for stock ordering: order more before seasonal peaks, reduce before troughs.
4. *Ethics of social media study.* Considerations: informed consent, anonymity (don't identify students), sensitivity (students may feel judged), voluntary participation. Collect via anonymous survey. Limitations: self-reported screen time is unreliable, grades have many causes (confounders), correlation ≠ causation.
5. *Immigration time series.* Typical: steady increase with dips during economic downturns or policy changes (e.g. COVID-19 border closure in 2020). Links to events: increases post-war, post-Vietnam-War refugee intake, temporary dip in 2020. Data doesn't tell: individual experiences, reasons for migration, settlement challenges, contributions made.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Question: "Is there a relationship between my daily water intake and self-rated productivity over 4 weeks?" Method: measured water in litres, rated productivity 1–10 at end of each day, recorded for 28 days. Time-series: both variables plotted over time show slight upward trend (improved hydration habits). Scatterplot: weak positive association (higher water → slightly higher productivity). Conclusion: "My data suggests a weak positive relationship, but this does not prove water intake causes better productivity." Limitations: (1) 4 weeks may be too short for meaningful patterns; (2) self-reported productivity is subjective and variable; (3) confounders include sleep, workload, stress. Improvements: longer period, objective productivity measure, control for other variables, replicate with more people.
