# VC2M9P01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9P01. List all outcomes for two-step chance experiments both with and without replacement, using lists, tree diagrams, tables or arrays; assign probabilities to outcomes and events.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Two-step experiments have outcomes that depend on whether items are replaced between steps.
  - Tree diagrams, tables and arrays systematically list all possible outcomes.
  - With replacement: probabilities stay the same at each step. Without: they change.
  - P(event) = number of favourable outcomes / total number of outcomes (for equally likely outcomes).
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **list all outcomes** for two-step chance experiments — both with and without replacement — and to **assign probabilities** to individual outcomes and compound events.
- You are learning to use tree diagrams, tables and systematic lists to ensure you find every possible outcome.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Distinguish between "with replacement" and "without replacement" experiments.
- Construct tree diagrams for two-step experiments.
- List all outcomes using tables or arrays.
- Calculate probabilities for individual outcomes (multiply along branches).
- Calculate probabilities for events (add probabilities of favourable outcomes).
- Recognise how "without replacement" changes the second-step probabilities.

## Core content

**1. Two-step experiments.** A two-step experiment has two stages performed in sequence. Examples: roll a die then flip a coin; draw a card then draw another card; choose a lolly then choose another. The sample space lists every possible combined outcome. *Quick check:* A coin is flipped and a die is rolled. How many outcomes are in the sample space?

**2. With replacement.** If the first item is put back before the second step, probabilities stay the same. Drawing a marble from a bag (3 red, 2 blue), replacing it, then drawing again: P(red) = 3/5 both times. The draws are **independent** — the first does not affect the second. *Quick check:* A bag has 4 green and 6 yellow marbles. A marble is drawn, replaced, then another drawn. What is P(green then green)?

**3. Without replacement.** If the first item is NOT put back, the total decreases and the composition changes. Same bag (3 red, 2 blue), no replacement: P(red first) = 3/5. If red was drawn, P(red second) = 2/4 = 1/2. The draws are **dependent** — the first affects the second. *Quick check:* Same bag (4 green, 6 yellow), no replacement. What is P(green then green)?

**4. Tree diagrams.** A tree diagram shows each step as a set of branches. Write probabilities on branches. Multiply along a path for the probability of that outcome. Add across paths for the probability of an event. The probabilities on branches from any point must sum to 1. *Quick check:* Draw a tree diagram for drawing two marbles without replacement from a bag of 3 red and 2 blue. Label all probabilities.

**5. Tables and arrays.** For two independent steps, a table (grid) shows all combinations. Rows represent one step, columns the other. Each cell is one outcome. For a die and a coin: 6 rows × 2 columns = 12 cells = 12 outcomes. Tables work best when steps are independent with few outcomes each. *Quick check:* Construct a table for choosing an entrée (soup, salad) and a main (chicken, fish, pasta). How many combinations?

**6. Worked example: raffle draw without replacement.**

A raffle has 10 tickets: 4 are prizes. Two tickets are drawn without replacement.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Step 1: P(prize) = 4/10 = 2/5. P(no prize) = 6/10 = 3/5.
- Step 2 (given prize first): P(prize) = 3/9 = 1/3. P(no prize) = 6/9 = 2/3.
- Step 2 (given no prize first): P(prize) = 4/9. P(no prize) = 5/9.
- P(both prizes) = 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15.
- P(no prizes) = 6/10 × 5/9 = 30/90 = 1/3.
- P(exactly one prize) = 4/10 × 6/9 + 6/10 × 4/9 = 24/90 + 24/90 = 48/90 = 8/15.
- Check: 2/15 + 1/3 + 8/15 = 2/15 + 5/15 + 8/15 = 15/15 = 1 ✓.

**7. Connecting to probability rules.** For independent events (with replacement): P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). For dependent events (without replacement): P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B given A). The "given A" part is why tree diagrams are so useful — each branch shows the updated probability after the previous step. *Quick check:* Two cards are drawn from a standard deck without replacement. What is P(both are hearts)?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Tattslotto (Victoria's lottery) draws numbered balls without replacement — understanding dependent probability helps interpret the actual odds. The Melbourne Cup (24 horses, barrier draws) involves without-replacement probability. Footy tipping competitions and match fixtures use two-step probability when considering sequential game outcomes.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Traditional First Nations games of chance and skill — such as marngrook (ball game) and various scoring games — involve sequential outcomes where earlier results can affect later ones. The probability of certain seasonal events (when combined, like rain followed by specific plant flowering) reflects dependent probability in ecological knowledge. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Card games played across cultures (rummy, poker, briscola, mahjong) all involve without-replacement probability — understanding these principles connects mathematics to cultural recreation. Community raffles and prize draws at cultural festivals use the same probability structure.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A sock drawer has 6 black and 4 white socks. You pull out two socks without looking (without replacement). What is the probability of getting a matching pair? Draw a tree diagram showing all outcomes.
2. **Civic.** A school council has 5 girls and 3 boys. Two members are randomly chosen to be co-captains. What is P(both girls)? P(one of each)? Does this method give fair representation?
3. **Vocational.** A quality controller tests batteries. In a box of 20, there are 3 defective ones. Two batteries are tested without replacement. What is P(both defective)? P(at least one defective)? If finding one defective triggers a full batch inspection, what is the probability of a full inspection?
4. **Ethical.** A hiring panel shortlists 8 candidates (5 men, 3 women) and randomly selects 2 for interview. What is P(both men)? P(at least one woman)? Discuss whether random selection from a gender-imbalanced shortlist produces equitable outcomes.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A deck of cards (52 cards, no jokers) is used for a two-draw game. Two cards are drawn without replacement. Calculate P(both aces), P(same suit), and P(one red and one black).

## Memorable takeaways
- With replacement: probabilities stay the same (independent). Multiply for "and."
- Without replacement: probabilities change (dependent). Update fractions after each draw.
- Tree diagrams: multiply along branches, add across favourable paths.
- Total of all outcome probabilities must equal 1.
- Tables/arrays work well for independent two-step experiments.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I didn't update the denominator." Without replacement, the total decreases by 1 after each draw. A bag of 10 becomes a bag of 9 for the second draw.
- "I multiplied when I should have added." Multiply along branches (for "and" — one specific path). Add across branches (for "or" — multiple favourable paths).
- "I forgot order matters." Drawing red then blue is a different outcome from blue then red. Both contribute to P(one red and one blue).
- "I used with-replacement probabilities in a without-replacement problem." Check the context: does the item go back or stay out? This completely changes the calculation.

A note on certainty. Probability calculations for well-defined experiments are exact mathematics. The results tell you long-run frequencies — individual outcomes are still random. Real-world applications (lotteries, quality testing) use these calculations to make informed decisions, but cannot predict specific individual outcomes.
