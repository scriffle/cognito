# VC2M10P01 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Use the language of 'if … then …', 'given', 'of' and 'knowing that' to investigate conditional statements and identify common mistakes in interpreting such language; describe and interpret conditional probability; design and conduct simulations using digital tools.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Conditional probability asks what happens given another event has occurred. P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A) — confusing them is the base rate fallacy. Language signals ('given', 'of', 'knowing that') indicate which event conditions which. Simulations model and verify conditional results.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **use conditional probability language** correctly and to identify the common mistake of confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A).
- You should calculate conditional probabilities and design simulations to model them.

## Core content

**Conditional probability.** P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B). "Given B has occurred, what fraction of the B-world also has A?" Example: 12 play sport, 8 of those also play music. P(music | sport) = 8/12 = 2/3.

**Language signals.** "If it rains, then cancelled" → P(cancelled | rain). "Given passed, probability of studied" → P(studied | passed). "Of those who exercise" → restricts sample space to exercisers.

**The base rate fallacy.** P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A). A test 95% accurate (P(positive | disease) = 0.95) does NOT mean P(disease | positive) = 95%. With rare diseases, most positives are false positives.

**Worked example — medical test.** Disease affects 1%. Test: 95% sensitivity, 90% specificity. In 10,000: 95 true positives + 990 false positives = 1085 positives. P(disease | positive) = 95/1085 ≈ 8.8%. Despite "95% accuracy."

**Simulations.** Use spreadsheets/digital tools to generate random data reflecting known probabilities, then count outcomes to estimate conditional probabilities.

**Victorian context.** Victoria Police forensic evidence requires correct conditional reasoning — the "prosecutor's fallacy" has caused wrongful convictions. COVID test interpretation during Melbourne lockdowns required this exact thinking. Aboriginal reading of Country involves conditional assessment — given certain signs, what is the probability of rain?

## Memorable takeaways
- P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B).
- P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A) — the most important distinction.
- "Given", "of", "knowing that" all restrict the sample space.
- Even accurate tests give many false positives when the condition is rare.
- Simulations build intuition and verify theory.

## Watch out for these traps
- Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A) — the base rate fallacy.
- Forgetting to restrict sample space to the "given" group only.
- Assuming independence without evidence (P(A|B) = P(A) only if independent).
- Ignoring base rates when interpreting test results.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *P(A|B) with P(A∩B) = 0.2, P(B) = 0.4.* P(A|B) = 0.2/0.4 = 0.5.
- *Drug test: 95% accurate, 2% use drugs.* In 1000: 19 true positives, 49 false positives. P(uses | positive) = 19/68 ≈ 28%, not 95%.
- *Factory: Machine A 60% (5% defects), Machine B 40% (3% defects).* P(A | defective) = 0.03/0.042 = 71%.
- *Facial recognition "98% accurate" in city of 1M, 100 wanted.* P(wanted | flagged) = 98/20,096 ≈ 0.5%. Over 99% of flags are innocent.
- *Monty Hall.* P(car door 2 | door 3 opened) = 2/3. Switching wins 2/3 of the time.
