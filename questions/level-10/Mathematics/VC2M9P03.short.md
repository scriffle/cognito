# VC2M9P03 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Design and conduct repeated chance experiments and simulations using digital tools to estimate probabilities that cannot be determined exactly.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Simulation estimates probabilities too complex to calculate. Match random device to real probability. More trials → more reliable estimates (law of large numbers).

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **design and conduct simulations** using digital tools to estimate probabilities that are impractical to calculate theoretically.
- You should match your random model to reality, run enough trials, and evaluate reliability.

## Core content

**Why simulate?** Some problems are too complex for exact calculation. Simulation runs thousands of random trials to estimate the answer experimentally.

**Designing a simulation.** Match the device to the real probability. P = 0.8 → RANDBETWEEN(1,10), 1–8 = success. P = 1/6 → use a die or RANDBETWEEN(1,6). The device must have the same probability as the real event.

**Digital tools.** Spreadsheets: =RAND() (0 to 1), =RANDBETWEEN(1,10). Count successes with =COUNTIF(). Repeat thousands of times instantly. GeoGebra and Desmos have simulation built in.

**Analysing results.** Relative frequency = successes/total trials. Example: 10 000 trials, 5 178 successes → P ≈ 0.518.

**How many trials?** 10 trials: unreliable. 100: rough estimate. 1 000+: good. 10 000+: very reliable. Law of large numbers guarantees convergence.

**Worked example.** Birthday problem (25 students, P of shared birthday): generate 25 random numbers 1–365, check for repeats. 10 000 trials → 5 689 with a repeat → P ≈ 0.569 (theoretical: 0.5687).

**Victorian and cultural context.** Bureau of Meteorology uses Monte Carlo simulation for probability forecasts. VicRoads simulates traffic flow. Hospitals simulate patient arrivals for staffing. First Nations knowledge accumulates probability estimates through generations of repeated observation — long-term data parallels the law of large numbers. Melbourne's gaming industry uses simulations to balance games.

## Memorable takeaways
- Simulate when exact calculation is too complex.
- Match your random device to the real probability.
- More trials → more reliable (law of large numbers).
- Design → run → count → estimate → evaluate.

## Watch out for these traps
- "My device doesn't match real probability." P = 0.7 but using a fair coin (0.5) gives wrong results. Match the model.
- "I only ran 10 trials." Too few for reliability. Use 1 000+.
- "I assumed independence when events are dependent." Your simulation must model dependence if the real situation has it.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Free-throw 80% — what device?* RANDBETWEEN(1,10): 1–8 = hit, 9–10 = miss. Or RAND() < 0.8.
- *500 trials, 215 successes — estimate?* P ≈ 215/500 = 0.43.
- *Why 20 trials less reliable than 2 000?* Random variation dominates in small samples; large samples converge to true probability.
- *Coin for 60% rain — what's wrong?* Coin gives 50%, not 60%. Device probability doesn't match reality.
- *Birthday problem, 10 000 trials, 5 689 matches?* P ≈ 0.569. Consistent with theoretical 0.5687.
