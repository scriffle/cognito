# VC2M10A04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10A04. Expand binomial products and factorise monic quadratic expressions using a variety of strategies.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **expand binomial products** and to **factorise monic quadratic expressions** using a variety of strategies.
- You are learning to move flexibly between expanded and factorised forms.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Expand products like (x + 3)(x + 5) using FOIL or the distributive law.
- Recognise and use perfect-square and difference-of-squares patterns.
- Factorise monic quadratics (leading coefficient 1) by finding factor pairs.
- Verify by expanding.

## Core content

**1. Binomial products — expanding.** A **binomial** is an expression with two terms (e.g. x + 3). A **binomial product** is two binomials multiplied: (x + 3)(x + 5). To expand, multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second: x·x + x·5 + 3·x + 3·5 = x² + 5x + 3x + 15 = x² + 8x + 15. This is sometimes called **FOIL** (First, Outer, Inner, Last). *Quick check:* Expand (x + 2)(x + 7).

**2. Special products — perfect squares.** (x + a)² = x² + 2ax + a². (x − a)² = x² − 2ax + a². The middle term is always twice the product of the two terms. Example: (x + 4)² = x² + 8x + 16. *Quick check:* Expand (x − 3)².

**3. Special products — difference of two squares.** (x + a)(x − a) = x² − a². The middle terms cancel. Example: (x + 6)(x − 6) = x² − 36. *Quick check:* Expand (x + 9)(x − 9).

**4. Factorising monic quadratics — the strategy.** A **monic quadratic** has the form x² + bx + c (leading coefficient = 1). To factorise, find two numbers p and q such that p + q = b and p × q = c. Then x² + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q). Example: x² + 7x + 12. Find two numbers that add to 7 and multiply to 12: 3 and 4. So x² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4). *Quick check:* Factorise x² + 9x + 20.

**5. Factorising when c is negative.** If c is negative, the two numbers have different signs. x² + 2x − 15: need p + q = 2 and p × q = −15. Numbers: 5 and −3 (5 − 3 = 2, 5 × −3 = −15). So x² + 2x − 15 = (x + 5)(x − 3). *Quick check:* Factorise x² − x − 12.

**6. Worked example — factorising a quadratic area expression.**
A rectangular garden has area x² + 10x + 21 m². Find the dimensions (as expressions in x).

*Follow the thinking:*
- This is a monic quadratic: a = 1, b = 10, c = 21.
- Find two numbers: p + q = 10 and p × q = 21.
- Factor pairs of 21: 1 × 21, 3 × 7.
- 3 + 7 = 10 ✓.
- Factorise: x² + 10x + 21 = (x + 3)(x + 7).
- The garden's dimensions are (x + 3) m by (x + 7) m.
- Check: (x + 3)(x + 7) = x² + 7x + 3x + 21 = x² + 10x + 21 ✓.

**7. Factorising difference of two squares.** Recognise the pattern x² − a² = (x + a)(x − a). Example: x² − 25 = (x + 5)(x − 5). Also works with variable squares: 4x² − 9 = (2x)² − 3² = (2x + 3)(2x − 3). *Quick check:* Factorise x² − 49.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Quadratic expressions model projectile motion (cricket balls at the MCG), profit functions in business, and structural arches (the parabolic trusses of Southern Cross Station). Engineers use factorised forms to find where a quantity equals zero — the solutions of quadratic equations. AMSI incorporates quadratic algebra across its secondary education programs.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Parabolic shapes appear in the flight paths of boomerangs and spears — trajectories that quadratic expressions describe mathematically. While the physics was understood through practical knowledge long before European algebra, the mathematical modelling of these trajectories uses the skills developed in this descriptor. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Quadratic equations were solved by Babylonian, Indian, Chinese and Islamic mathematicians centuries before European methods developed. Al-Khwārizmī's "completing the square" (9th century CE) and Brahmagupta's formula (7th century CE) are foundational contributions students can explore.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A photo is (x + 2) cm wide and (x + 5) cm tall. Write and expand an expression for its area. If x = 8, calculate the area.
2. **Civic.** A council park has a square section of side x metres, with a 4-metre-wide path added on two adjacent sides. Write an expression for the total area (as a product of binomials), expand it, and simplify.
3. **Vocational.** A builder models a room's floor area as x² + 11x + 28. Factorise to find the room's dimensions. If x = 3, what are the dimensions and area?
4. **Ethical.** A student claims x² − 16 = (x − 4)². Test this by substituting x = 5 into both sides. Is the student correct? What is the correct factorisation?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research Brahmagupta's approach to quadratics. Then factorise completely: x² − 6x + 9 (recognise the special pattern).

## Memorable takeaways
- (x + p)(x + q) = x² + (p + q)x + pq — expand by multiplying each pair.
- To factorise x² + bx + c: find p + q = b and p × q = c.
- Perfect square: (x + a)² = x² + 2ax + a².
- Difference of squares: x² − a² = (x + a)(x − a).
- Always check by expanding your factorised form.

## Stretch yourself
A farmer has a rectangular paddock with area x² + 8x + 15 m². She decides to extend the paddock by adding 2 m to the length and 3 m to the width. First, factorise the original area to find the original dimensions. Then write an expression for the new area as a binomial product. Expand and simplify the new area. Calculate the increase in area (new minus original) and factorise the increase if possible. If x = 10, calculate both areas and the increase. Show all working and verify by expanding each factorisation. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Sign errors when c is negative.** If x² + 2x − 15 = (x + 5)(x − 3), check: 5 × (−3) = −15 ✓ and 5 + (−3) = 2 ✓. Get the signs right by considering which number is larger.
- **Confusing perfect square with difference of squares.** x² + 9 is not (x + 3)(x − 3) — that gives x² − 9. And x² + 9 does not factorise over real numbers.
- **Forgetting to check by expanding.** The fastest way to catch errors is to expand your answer and see if you get back the original.
- **Assuming all quadratics factorise neatly.** Some monic quadratics (e.g. x² + 3x + 5) do not have integer factor pairs. The discriminant (b² − 4c) tells you whether integer factors exist.

## Try drawing this
Draw an area model for (x + 4)(x + 6). Make a 2×2 grid: top-left = x², top-right = 6x, bottom-left = 4x, bottom-right = 24. Show the total area = x² + 10x + 24. Then reverse the process: start with x² + 10x + 24 and show how finding 4 and 6 (add to 10, multiply to 24) recovers the grid.

A note on certainty. The techniques here are exact algebraic methods. When a monic quadratic has integer factor pairs, the factorisation is unique (up to order). When no integer pairs exist, other methods (completing the square, quadratic formula) are needed — these are extensions you will meet soon.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Expand (x + 2)(x + 7).* x² + 7x + 2x + 14 = x² + 9x + 14.
2. *(x − 3)².* x² − 6x + 9.
3. *(x + 9)(x − 9).* x² − 81.
4. *Factorise x² + 9x + 20.* Numbers: 4 + 5 = 9, 4 × 5 = 20. Answer: (x + 4)(x + 5).
5. *Factorise x² − x − 12.* Numbers: 3 + (−4) = −1, 3 × (−4) = −12. Answer: (x + 3)(x − 4).

7. *Factorise x² − 49.* (x + 7)(x − 7).

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Photo area.* (x + 2)(x + 5) = x² + 7x + 10. When x = 8: 10 × 13 = 130 cm².
2. *Park with path.* Total area = (x + 4)(x + 4) = (x + 4)² = x² + 8x + 16. (Path on two adjacent sides makes the total dimensions x + 4 by x + 4.)
3. *Room floor.* x² + 11x + 28 = (x + 4)(x + 7). When x = 3: 7 m × 10 m = 70 m².
4. *Error check.* x = 5: LHS = 25 − 16 = 9. RHS = (5 − 4)² = 1. Not equal. Correct factorisation: x² − 16 = (x + 4)(x − 4).
5. *Brahmagupta + factorisation.* x² − 6x + 9 = (x − 3)² (perfect square: numbers −3 and −3, sum = −6, product = 9).

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Original area x² + 8x + 15 = (x + 3)(x + 5). Original dimensions: (x + 3) and (x + 5). New dimensions: (x + 3 + 3) = (x + 6) and (x + 5 + 2) = (x + 7). New area = (x + 6)(x + 7) = x² + 13x + 42. Increase = (x² + 13x + 42) − (x² + 8x + 15) = 5x + 27. When x = 10: original = 13 × 15 = 195 m². New = 16 × 17 = 272 m². Increase = 77 m². Check: 5(10) + 27 = 77 ✓. The increase (5x + 27) is linear, not quadratic — adding constant amounts to dimensions produces a linear increase in area.
