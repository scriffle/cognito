## Core content

**1. What is conditional probability?** Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring **given that** another event has already occurred. It restricts the sample space. P(A|B) means "the probability of A, given B." The vertical bar | means "given." *Quick check:* In a class of 30, 12 play sport and 8 of those also play music. If a student plays sport, what is the probability they also play music?

**2. The language of conditions.** "If it rains, then the match is cancelled" — P(cancelled | rain). "Given that the student passed, what is the probability they studied?" — P(studied | passed). "The probability of failing, knowing that you didn't study" — P(fail | didn't study). "Of those who exercise, the fraction who are healthy" — P(healthy | exercise). Each phrase restricts the sample space to a subgroup. *Quick check:* Translate into P(A|B) notation: "Of those who own a dog, the proportion who also own a cat."

**3. The formula.** P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B). This says: restrict to the world where B happened, then find what fraction of that world also has A. Example: P(rain) = 0.3, P(umbrella and rain) = 0.25. P(umbrella | rain) = 0.25/0.3 = 5/6 ≈ 0.83. *Quick check:* P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.2. Find P(A|B).

**4. The common mistake — confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A).** P(disease | positive test) ≠ P(positive test | disease). A test might be 99% accurate (P(positive | disease) = 0.99) but if the disease is rare, P(disease | positive) can still be low. This is called the "base rate fallacy" or "prosecutor's fallacy." Getting this wrong has serious consequences in medicine and law. *Quick check:* A drug test is 95% accurate. 2% of athletes use drugs. If someone tests positive, is the probability they use drugs equal to 95%? Explain why or why not.

**5. Two-way tables and tree diagrams for conditional probability.** Two-way tables show joint and marginal frequencies clearly. To find P(A|B), look at the B row/column and find the proportion that is also A. Tree diagrams show conditional branches — the second branch represents conditional probabilities. *Quick check:* From a two-way table where 15 of 40 females play sport, what is P(sport | female)?

**6. Worked example — medical testing.**
A disease affects 1% of the population. A test has 95% sensitivity (P(positive | disease) = 0.95) and 90% specificity (P(negative | no disease) = 0.90).

*Follow the thinking:*
- In 10,000 people: 100 have the disease, 9900 don't.
- Diseased and test positive: 100 × 0.95 = 95.
- Healthy and test positive (false positive): 9900 × 0.10 = 990.
- Total positive tests: 95 + 990 = 1085.
- P(disease | positive) = 95/1085 ≈ 0.088 = 8.8%.
- Despite 95% accuracy, only about 9% of positive results actually have the disease.
- This is why confirmatory testing is essential.

**7. Simulations.** Use spreadsheets or digital tools to simulate conditional scenarios. Generate random data that reflects known probabilities, then count frequencies to estimate conditional probabilities. Example: simulate 1000 coin flips and 1000 die rolls. Estimate P(six | heads). If independent, P(six | heads) should equal P(six) = 1/6. Simulation confirms theoretical results and builds intuition. *Quick check:* How would you simulate the medical testing example using a spreadsheet?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria Police forensic evidence interpretation requires understanding conditional probability — confusing P(evidence | innocent) with P(innocent | evidence) is the prosecutor's fallacy, which has caused wrongful convictions. The Peter Mac Cancer Centre uses conditional probability in screening program evaluation. Melbourne's weather forecasts ("70% chance of rain given a cold front") are conditional statements. COVID-19 test interpretation during Melbourne's lockdowns required exactly this reasoning.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Reading Country involves conditional reasoning — "given these signs (clouds, animal behaviour, wind direction), what is the probability of rain?" Elders assess conditions through multiple conditional indicators built over generations of observation. This is analogous to Bayesian updating. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Conditional reasoning is universal in everyday decision-making: "given that it's Friday, what is the chance the restaurant is full?"; "knowing the team is winning, what is the chance they'll make finals?" Medical decision-making across all cultures requires understanding conditional test results.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A weather app says "80% chance of rain given cloud cover." Today is cloudy. Does this mean there's an 80% chance of rain today? What information would you need to know the actual probability of rain?
2. **Civic.** In a survey of 200 voters: 120 support a policy, 80 oppose. Of supporters, 90 are over 40. Of opposers, 20 are over 40. Calculate P(over 40 | supporter) and P(supporter | over 40). Are they the same? Why does this distinction matter for political campaigns?
3. **Vocational.** A factory produces items on two machines. Machine A makes 60% of items with 5% defect rate. Machine B makes 40% with 3% defect rate. A defective item is found. What is the probability it came from Machine A?
4. **Ethical.** A facial recognition system is "98% accurate." In a city of 1 million, 100 are wanted criminals. If the system flags someone, what is P(actually wanted | flagged)? Discuss the implications for civil liberties.
5. **Cross-cultural.** The Monty Hall problem: you pick door 1 of 3. The host opens door 3 (showing a goat). Should you switch? Calculate P(car behind door 2 | door 3 opened) using conditional probability. Explain why intuition often fails here.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A).** This is the most common and most dangerous error. "Probability of having the disease given a positive test" is NOT the same as "probability of testing positive given you have the disease."
- **Forgetting to restrict the sample space.** P(A|B) uses only the B portion of the data, not the whole population.
- **Assuming independence without checking.** P(A|B) = P(A) only if A and B are independent. Don't assume this.
- **Ignoring base rates.** Even very accurate tests give many false positives when the condition is rare.

A note on certainty. The formula P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) is a mathematical definition — it is exact. What requires careful thought is correctly identifying which event is A and which is B in a real-world context. The mathematical framework is certain; the translation from language to mathematics is where errors arise. This is why precise language ("given", "of", "knowing that") matters so much.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Sport and music.* 8 out of 12 sport players also play music. P(music | sport) = 8/12 = 2/3.
2. *Dog and cat owners.* P(cat | dog) — "of those who own a dog, the proportion who also own a cat."
3. *Formula.* P(A|B) = P(A and B)/P(B) = 0.2/0.4 = 0.5.
4. *Drug test.* No, 95% is P(positive | uses drugs). We need P(uses drugs | positive). With 2% base rate and 95% accuracy: in 1000 athletes, 20 use drugs (19 test positive), 980 don't (49 false positives). P(uses | positive) = 19/68 ≈ 28%, not 95%.
5. *Two-way table.* P(sport | female) = 15/40 = 3/8 = 0.375.
6. *Medical testing.* P(disease | positive) ≈ 8.8% (worked through in example).
7. *Simulation.* Generate random numbers 1–10000. Numbers 1–100 = disease. For disease: 95% chance of positive (random 1–100, ≤ 95 = positive). For no disease: 10% false positive. Count and calculate.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Weather app.* 80% is P(rain | cloudy). You also need P(cloudy) and P(rain) to find P(rain today). If it's definitely cloudy today, then yes, 80% applies — but only if the app's conditional is accurate.
2. *Voters.* P(over 40 | supporter) = 90/120 = 75%. P(supporter | over 40) = 90/110 = 82%. Different — campaigns targeting over-40s should note that 82% of that group already supports the policy.
3. *Factory.* P(A and defective) = 0.60 × 0.05 = 0.03. P(B and defective) = 0.40 × 0.03 = 0.012. P(defective) = 0.042. P(A | defective) = 0.03/0.042 = 5/7 ≈ 71%.
4. *Facial recognition.* True positives: 100 × 0.98 = 98. False positives: 999,900 × 0.02 = 19,998. P(wanted | flagged) = 98/20,096 ≈ 0.49%. Over 99.5% of flags are innocent people. Serious civil liberties implications.
5. *Monty Hall.* Initially P(car door 1) = 1/3. After door 3 opens: P(car door 2 | door 3 opened) = 2/3. Switching wins 2/3 of the time. The host's action gives information because the host knows where the car is and always opens a goat door.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** In 100,000 people: 100 have disease, 99,900 don't. Test 1: 100 × 0.99 = 99 true positives; 99,900 × 0.05 = 4995 false positives. P(disease | positive) = 99/5094 ≈ 1.9%. After second positive test on the 5094 positives: 99 × 0.99 = 98 true positives; 4995 × 0.05 = 250 false positives. P(disease | two positives) = 98/348 ≈ 28%. Dramatic improvement but still 72% false positive rate. For population screening: with 100,000 people, the first test produces ~5000 false positives needing expensive confirmatory testing. Psychological harm to 5000 falsely alarmed people is significant. Recommendation: don't screen entire population. Instead, screen high-risk groups (family history, symptoms) where the base rate is higher, making P(disease | positive) much more useful. Use the population test only as a first filter with mandatory confirmatory testing before any treatment decisions.
