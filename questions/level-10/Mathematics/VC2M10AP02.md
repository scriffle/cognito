# VC2M10AP02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AP02. Evaluate research studies reported in the media by examining their planning and implementation.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Probability).

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **critically evaluate research studies** reported in the media by examining how they were planned, conducted and reported.
- You are learning to identify common flaws in study design and to distinguish between correlation and causation.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Identify key features of study design: sample size, sampling method, control groups, blinding.
- Distinguish between observational studies and randomised controlled trials (RCTs).
- Identify potential confounding variables in reported findings.
- Evaluate whether conclusions are supported by the evidence.
- Recognise when media reports overstate or misrepresent research findings.

## Core content

**1. Sample size and representativeness.** A study's conclusions are only as strong as its sample. Small samples produce unreliable estimates with wide margins of error. A study of 15 participants claiming universal benefits lacks statistical power. The sample must also be representative — a study recruiting only from one demographic cannot generalise to the whole population. *Quick check:* A study of 20 university students finds that caffeine improves concentration. State two limitations of this study.

**2. Sampling methods.** Random sampling gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection — it minimises bias. Convenience sampling (e.g. surveying people at a shopping centre) introduces selection bias. Stratified sampling ensures subgroups are proportionally represented. The sampling method directly affects the validity of generalisations. *Quick check:* Why is a random sample preferred over a convenience sample?

**3. Control groups and experimental design.** A well-designed experiment compares a treatment group to a control group. In a randomised controlled trial (RCT), participants are randomly assigned to groups, and ideally the study is double-blind (neither participants nor researchers know who receives the treatment). This design minimises placebo effects and researcher bias. *Quick check:* What is the purpose of a control group in an experiment?

**4. Correlation versus causation.** A correlation between two variables means they tend to change together, but this does not prove one causes the other. Ice cream sales and drowning rates are correlated, but the confounding variable is temperature. Establishing causation requires controlled experiments, plausible mechanisms, and consistency across studies. *Quick check:* A study finds that countries with higher chocolate consumption have more Nobel Prize winners. Does this prove chocolate improves intelligence?

**5. Confounding variables.** A confounding variable is a third factor that influences both the independent and dependent variables, creating a spurious association. In a study finding that coffee drinkers exercise more, stress level might be the confounder (stressed people drink more coffee AND exercise more). Identifying confounders is essential for valid interpretation. *Quick check:* In a study showing that students who eat breakfast get higher grades, suggest a possible confounding variable.

**6. Worked example — evaluating a media report.**
A news headline reads: "New study proves vitamin D cures depression."

*Evaluation:*
- Study type? Observational (tracked people who took vitamin D, not an RCT).
- Sample size? 200 participants — moderate but not large.
- Control group? No randomised control group.
- Blinding? Not blinded.
- Confounding variables? People who take supplements may also exercise more, eat better, and have higher incomes.
- Conclusion: The study shows a correlation between vitamin D intake and lower depression scores, but cannot prove causation. The headline overstates the findings.

**7. Observational studies vs RCTs.** Observational studies observe without intervening — they can identify associations but not prove causation. RCTs randomly assign treatments and control for confounders — they can establish causation. Both have a role: RCTs are the gold standard but are sometimes impractical or unethical. *Quick check:* Why might it be unethical to use an RCT to study the effects of smoking on health?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Evidence-based policy is central to Victorian public health, education and environmental management. The Burnet Institute and Murdoch Children's Research Institute in Melbourne conduct world-leading health research with rigorous study design.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations health research has historically suffered from poor study design that failed to include Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives. Contemporary best practice requires community partnership, culturally safe methodologies, and Indigenous data sovereignty.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The development of clinical trial methodology spans cultures — from James Lind's scurvy trial (1747) to modern international multi-site trials that account for population diversity.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** An advertisement claims "9 out of 10 dentists recommend our toothpaste." What questions should be asked about this claim before accepting it?
2. **Civic.** A council cites a study showing that green spaces reduce crime. The study compared wealthy suburbs (more parks) with poorer suburbs (fewer parks). Identify the confounding variable.
3. **Vocational.** A pharmaceutical company reports that a new drug reduced symptoms in 80% of patients. What design features would make this claim more credible?
4. **Ethical.** A researcher reports only the results that support the hypothesis and omits contradictory data. What term describes this practice, and why is it harmful?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research the history of clinical trials, starting with James Lind. Then evaluate: a study finds that a traditional herbal remedy improves sleep. The study has 30 participants, no control group, and participants self-reported their sleep quality. Assess the reliability.

## Memorable takeaways
- Sample size matters — larger, representative samples give more reliable results.
- Random sampling and control groups are hallmarks of good study design.
- Correlation ≠ causation — always look for confounding variables.
- RCTs are the gold standard for establishing causation.
- Media headlines often overstate or simplify research findings.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Assuming correlation means causation.** The most common error in interpreting research. Always ask: could a third variable explain this?
- **Ignoring sample size.** A dramatic result from 10 participants is far less reliable than a modest result from 10 000.
- **Trusting self-reported data uncritically.** Participants may overestimate exercise, underreport unhealthy behaviours, or experience placebo effects.
- **Accepting media headlines at face value.** Always look for the original study — headlines are designed for impact, not accuracy.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Caffeine study limitations.* Small sample (20); university students are not representative of the general population.
2. *Random vs convenience.* Random sampling gives every member an equal chance, reducing selection bias. Convenience sampling is likely to over-represent certain demographics.
3. *Control group purpose.* Provides a baseline for comparison, allowing researchers to determine whether the treatment (rather than other factors) caused the observed effect.
4. *Chocolate and Nobel Prizes.* No. This is a correlation. Confounders include national wealth, education spending, and research funding — all of which increase both chocolate consumption and Nobel Prizes.
5. *Breakfast and grades confounder.* Socioeconomic status — wealthier families are more likely to provide breakfast AND support academic achievement.
6. *Vitamin D headline.* Observational, no control group, confounders present. The headline overstates the evidence.
7. *Smoking RCT ethics.* Randomly assigning people to smoke would expose them to a known health hazard — this is unethical. Observational studies are used instead.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Toothpaste claim.* How many dentists were surveyed? How were they selected? Were they paid? What alternatives were they comparing to? "Recommend" does not mean "exclusively endorse."
2. *Green spaces and crime.* Wealth is the confounding variable — wealthier suburbs have both more green spaces and lower crime rates, regardless of the parks.
3. *Drug claim.* Randomised controlled trial, double-blind, placebo control, large sample, pre-registered outcomes.
4. *Selective reporting.* Publication bias or cherry-picking. It is harmful because it distorts the evidence base and can lead to ineffective or dangerous treatments being adopted.
5. *Herbal remedy assessment.* Low reliability: small sample, no control group (placebo effect likely), self-reported data (subjective). An RCT with placebo control and objective sleep measurement would be needed.
