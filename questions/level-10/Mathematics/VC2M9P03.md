- You are learning that repeated random trials converge toward the true probability, and that well-designed simulations model real situations accurately.

By the end of this you should be able to:

## Core content

**1. Why simulate?** Some probability problems are too complex for exact calculation. What is the probability of getting exactly 3 heads in a row somewhere within 20 coin tosses? What is the chance a family with 4 children has at least one of each gender? These can be calculated theoretically but are much easier to estimate by simulation — running thousands of trials quickly. *Quick check:* Give an example of a probability question that would be easier to simulate than calculate exactly.

**2. Designing a simulation.** Match the random device to the real situation. If P = 1/2 (like gender at birth, simplified), use a coin or random 0/1. If P = 1/6, use a die. If P = 0.7, use a random number generator: 1–7 = success, 8–10 = failure. The key question: does your device have the same probability as the real event? *Quick check:* Design a simulation for free-throw shooting at 80% accuracy. What device models this?

**3. Conducting trials with digital tools.** Spreadsheets (Excel, Google Sheets) generate random numbers: =RAND() gives a number from 0 to 1. =RANDBETWEEN(1,10) gives an integer from 1 to 10. Repeat thousands of times instantly. Count successes using =COUNTIF(). GeoGebra and Desmos have built-in simulation tools. *Quick check:* In a spreadsheet, how would you simulate rolling a die 1 000 times?

**4. Recording and analysing results.** Record the outcome of each trial, then count favourable outcomes. Relative frequency = favourable/total. Example: simulate "at least one 6 in four die rolls" for 10 000 trials. If 5 178 trials produced at least one 6: estimated P ≈ 0.518. (Theoretical: 1 − (5/6)⁴ ≈ 0.518.) More trials → closer to theoretical probability. *Quick check:* A simulation of 500 trials produces 215 successes. Estimate the probability of success.

**5. How many trials?** More trials give more stable estimates. With 10 trials, results can vary wildly. With 1 000 trials, estimates are usually within a few percentage points of the true probability. With 10 000+ trials, estimates are very reliable. As a rule: at least 100 for a rough estimate; 1 000+ for a good estimate. *Quick check:* Why would 20 trials of a simulation give a less reliable estimate than 2 000 trials?

**6. Worked example: "birthday problem" simulation.**

What is the probability that in a class of 25 students, at least two share a birthday?

*Follow the thinking:*
- Theoretical calculation is possible but complex (uses complementary counting with 365 choices).
- Simulation: generate 25 random numbers from 1–365 (representing birthdays). Check if any repeats.
- Repeat 10 000 times using a spreadsheet.
- Results: 5 689 of 10 000 trials had at least one shared birthday.
- Estimated P ≈ 0.569 (theoretical: 0.5687 — very close).
- Conclusion: there is about a 57% chance of a shared birthday in a group of 25.

**7. Evaluating your simulation.** Does the model match reality? Check: (1) Does the random device have the correct probabilities? (2) Are the trials independent (or dependent, matching the real situation)? (3) Are enough trials run? (4) Does the simulation ignore important real-world factors? Report your design, number of trials, results and confidence in the estimate. *Quick check:* You simulate rain probability using a coin (50/50). Actual rain probability in Melbourne in July is about 60%. What is wrong with this simulation design?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne Weather Bureau uses simulation (Monte Carlo methods) to generate probability forecasts — the "70% chance of rain" comes from running atmospheric models thousands of times. VicRoads simulates traffic flow to predict congestion probabilities. Melbourne's hospitals simulate patient arrivals to plan staffing. TAC uses crash simulations to estimate road safety probabilities.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations knowledge systems accumulate probability estimates through generations of repeated observation — estimating likelihoods of seasonal events, animal movements and weather patterns through long-term data gathering. This parallels the principle that more observations give more reliable estimates. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Monte Carlo simulation (named after the casino city) is used globally in engineering, finance and medicine. Insurance companies across Melbourne's diverse business community use simulation to price risk. Game designers use random simulations to balance gameplay — a field Melbourne's gaming industry excels in.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to estimate the probability of getting at least 4 heads in 6 coin tosses. Design a simulation using a spreadsheet. Run 1 000 trials and estimate the probability. Compare to the theoretical answer (using combinations: P = (15+6+1)/64 = 22/64 ≈ 0.344).
2. **Civic.** A traffic light is red for 40 seconds out of every 100-second cycle. Simulate hitting 3 consecutive red lights on a drive. Assume lights are independent. Design and run a simulation of 500 trials. Estimate P(all 3 red).
3. **Vocational.** A call centre knows 65% of calls are answered within 30 seconds. Simulate 10 consecutive calls and estimate P(at least 8 of 10 answered in time). Design the simulation and explain your random-number assignment.
4. **Ethical.** A school uses random drug testing — 5 students selected from 200 each week. Simulate 40 weeks to estimate how many times a particular student gets tested. Discuss fairness: what is the theoretical probability, and does simulation reveal any surprising clustering?
5. **Cross-cultural.** A manufacturer claims 95% of products pass quality control. Design a simulation to test: in a batch of 50, what is the probability of finding 3 or more defective items? Run 1 000 trials and estimate the probability.

## Memorable takeaways
- Simulate when exact calculation is too complex.
- Match your random device to the real probability.
- More trials → more reliable estimate (law of large numbers).
- Design, run, count, estimate, evaluate.
- Always check: does the simulation model the real situation accurately?
- Report: design + number of trials + estimate + confidence.

## Stretch yourself
The "Monty Hall problem": a game show has 3 doors — one hides a prize. You choose a door; the host opens another door (always a losing one); you can switch or stay. (a) Design a simulation that tests the "always switch" strategy and the "always stay" strategy. Specify exactly how you model each step with random numbers. (b) Run 1 000 trials for each strategy using a spreadsheet or digital tool. (c) Estimate P(win) for each strategy. (d) Explain why the theoretical answers are P(win if switch) = 2/3 and P(win if stay) = 1/3. (e) Discuss why this result is counterintuitive and how simulation helps convince people of the correct answer. Show your simulation design and results. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "My simulation device doesn't match the real probability." If real P = 0.7 but you use a fair coin (P = 0.5), your results will be wrong. Match the device to the real probability.
- "I only ran 10 trials." Small sample sizes give unreliable estimates. Use at least 100 trials, preferably 1 000+.
- "I assumed independence when events are dependent." If the real situation has dependence (like without-replacement), your simulation must model that too.
- "I didn't check my simulation against known results." Always verify your simulation design with a case where you know the theoretical answer before using it on unknowns.

A note on certainty. Simulation estimates converge toward true probabilities but are never exact (they are estimates). The law of large numbers guarantees convergence as trials increase. For critical decisions (medicine, engineering), simulations run millions of trials and report confidence intervals. The simulation itself is only as good as its design — an inaccurate model produces inaccurate estimates regardless of trial count.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Example for simulation.* Any complex compound event, e.g.: "probability of rolling at least one double-six in 24 rolls of two dice" or "probability of being dealt a flush in poker."
2. *Free-throw 80%.* Use RANDBETWEEN(1,10): 1–8 = makes the shot, 9–10 = misses. Or RAND(): if < 0.8, success.
3. *Die rolls in spreadsheet.* =RANDBETWEEN(1,6) in 1 000 cells. Each cell is one simulated die roll.
4. *500 trials, 215 successes.* P ≈ 215/500 = 0.43.
5. *20 vs 2 000 trials.* With few trials, random variation (luck) heavily influences results. The law of large numbers means more trials produce more stable, reliable estimates closer to the true probability.
6. *Rain simulation.* The coin gives P(rain) = 0.5, but actual probability is 0.6. The simulation doesn't match reality. Use RANDBETWEEN(1,10): 1–6 = rain, 7–10 = no rain.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *4+ heads in 6 tosses.* Simulation: 6 cells with RANDBETWEEN(0,1). Count heads (=COUNTIF(range,1)). Success if count ≥ 4. Repeat 1 000 times. Expected estimate ≈ 0.34 (theoretical: 22/64 = 0.344).
2. *Traffic lights.* P(red) = 0.4 per light. Simulation: 3 cells with RAND(). Success if all three < 0.4. P(all red) = 0.4³ = 0.064 theoretical. In 500 trials expect ≈ 32 successes.
3. *Call centre.* Assign 1–65 = answered, 66–100 = not (via RANDBETWEEN(1,100)). Simulate 10 calls. Count ≥ 8 answered = success. Repeat 1 000 times. Theoretical: binomial P(X≥8 | n=10, p=0.65) ≈ 0.262.
4. *Drug testing.* P(selected in one week) = 5/200 = 0.025. Over 40 weeks, expected number of times tested = 40 × 0.025 = 1. Simulation may show some students tested 0 times, others 2–3 times — clustering by random chance, not unfairness. P(never tested in 40 weeks) = (0.975)⁴⁰ ≈ 0.364.
5. *Quality control.* P(defective) = 0.05. Simulate 50 items: RAND() < 0.05 = defective. Count defectives. Success if ≥ 3. Repeat 1 000 times. Theoretical: P(X≥3 | n=50, p=0.05) = 1 − P(X≤2) ≈ 1 − 0.541 = 0.459.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** (a) Model: RANDBETWEEN(1,3) = prize door. RANDBETWEEN(1,3) = player's choice. Host opens a remaining losing door. Switch = player takes the other unopened door. Stay = player keeps original. (b) After 1 000 trials: "always switch" won ≈ 667 times. "Always stay" won ≈ 333 times. (c) P(win|switch) ≈ 0.667. P(win|stay) ≈ 0.333. (d) When you choose initially, P(correct) = 1/3. The prize is behind another door 2/3 of the time. The host's reveal doesn't change this — it just shows you which of the remaining doors to switch to. Switching wins whenever your first choice was wrong (2/3 of the time). (e) People intuitively think it's 50/50 after the host reveals, but the host's action is not random — they always reveal a loser. Simulation demonstrates the 2/3 vs 1/3 difference convincingly because the numbers speak louder than the counter-intuitive logic.
