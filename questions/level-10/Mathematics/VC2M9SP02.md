## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9SP02. Apply the enlargement transformation to shapes and objects using dynamic geometry software as appropriate; identify and explain, using language of similarity, ratio and scale, aspects that remain the same and those that change.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Space).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Enlargement multiplies all lengths by a scale factor from a fixed centre point.
  - Angles stay the same; lengths change; areas change by the square of the scale factor; the shape remains similar.
  - Enlargement with scale factor k > 1 makes shapes bigger; 0 < k < 1 makes them smaller; k < 0 inverts.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **apply enlargement transformations** to shapes and objects and to **identify what stays the same and what changes**, using the language of similarity, ratio and scale.
- You are learning that enlargement produces similar figures — same shape, different size — with predictable relationships between lengths, angles and areas.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Perform an enlargement from a given centre with a given scale factor.
- Identify the centre and scale factor of an enlargement from a diagram.
- Explain what remains the same: angles, shape, proportions.
- Explain what changes: lengths (×k), perimeter (×k), area (×k²).
- Use dynamic geometry software to explore enlargements.
- Connect enlargement to similarity and real-world scaling.

## Core content

**1. What is an enlargement?** An enlargement (or dilation) transforms every point by multiplying its distance from a fixed **centre of enlargement** by the **scale factor** k. If k = 2, every point moves to twice its distance from the centre. The image is similar to the original. *Quick check:* A triangle is enlarged by scale factor 3 from centre O. A vertex that was 4 cm from O is now how far from O?

**2. What stays the same.** Under enlargement: **angles** are preserved (all corresponding angles are equal). **Shape** is preserved (the image looks the same, just bigger or smaller). **Ratios between corresponding sides** are preserved. The image is **similar** to the original. *Quick check:* A rectangle is enlarged by scale factor 2. What happens to its internal angles?

**3. What changes.** Under enlargement by scale factor k: **lengths** multiply by k. **Perimeter** multiplies by k. **Area** multiplies by k². If k = 3: lengths × 3, perimeter × 3, area × 9. If k = ½: lengths × ½, perimeter × ½, area × ¼. *Quick check:* A shape has area 20 cm². After enlargement by scale factor 4, what is the new area?

**4. Scale factors less than 1 and negative.** If 0 < k < 1: the image is smaller (a reduction). If k < 0: the image is on the other side of the centre (inverted) and scaled by |k|. For k = −2: the image is twice the size but flipped through the centre. *Quick check:* A figure is enlarged by factor −1. Describe the result.

**5. Finding the centre and scale factor.** Given original and image: join corresponding points with straight lines — they all meet at the centre of enlargement. Scale factor = image distance from centre / original distance from centre = image length / original length. *Quick check:* A line segment is 5 cm in the original and 12.5 cm in the image. What is the scale factor?

**6. Worked example: enlarging a logo for a banner.**

A school logo is 8 cm × 5 cm. It must be enlarged to fit a 2 m wide banner.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Required width: 200 cm. Original width: 8 cm.
- Scale factor: k = 200/8 = 25.
- New height: 5 × 25 = 125 cm = 1.25 m.
- New area: 8 × 5 = 40 cm² original. New area = 40 × 25² = 40 × 625 = 25 000 cm² = 2.5 m².
- All angles in the logo stay the same — it still looks right.
- The enlargement centre doesn't matter for the final dimensions (it affects position, not shape/size).
- Note: perimeter of original = 26 cm. New perimeter = 26 × 25 = 650 cm = 6.5 m.

**7. Using dynamic geometry software.** GeoGebra's "Dilate from Point" tool lets you explore enlargements interactively. Draw a shape, set a centre, drag the scale factor slider, and observe: angles remain constant while lengths and area change. You can verify that area scales as k² by comparing measurements before and after. Software also makes negative scale factors visually clear — the image appears on the opposite side of the centre. *Quick check:* In GeoGebra, you enlarge a triangle by factor 0.5 from its centroid. The image appears [inside/outside] the original. The area becomes [what fraction] of the original?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Architects and graphic designers across Melbourne use enlargement daily — scaling building plans from A3 drawings to full construction scale, enlarging logos for billboards, printing photographs at different sizes. The Melbourne tram network map is a reduction (scale factor < 1) of the actual routes. 3D printing in Melbourne's manufacturing sector scales digital models to physical objects.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations artists apply enlargement principles in their practice — scaling designs from small bark or skin pieces to large wall murals or sand drawings while preserving the proportional relationships that carry cultural meaning. The geometric consistency across different-sized coolamon and shield designs demonstrates understanding of similarity under scaling. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Fashion design in Melbourne scales pattern pieces from standard sizes — each size is an enlargement of the pattern template. Model-making in architecture (common in Melbourne's diverse design studios) uses reduction to create miniature representations of buildings. Photography and printing across all communities involves enlargement that preserves proportions.

## Memorable takeaways
- Enlargement = same shape, different size. From a centre, multiply distances by k.
- Stays the same: angles, shape, proportions.
- Changes: lengths × k, perimeter × k, area × k².
- k > 1: bigger. 0 < k < 1: smaller. k < 0: inverted.
- Similar figures are related by enlargement.
- Software helps visualise and verify these relationships.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I thought area scales by k (not k²)." Area always scales as the square of the linear scale factor. Double all lengths → quadruple the area.
- "I forgot angles stay the same." Enlargement only changes size, never shape. All angles are preserved — that's what makes it a similarity transformation.
- "I measured the scale factor from the wrong point." The scale factor is measured from the CENTRE of enlargement, not from the origin or a vertex.
- "I applied a scale factor to area directly." If k = 3 and area = 20, new area is NOT 60. It is 20 × 3² = 180.

A note on certainty. Enlargement (dilation) is a well-defined geometric transformation with exact properties. The preservation of angles and the k² scaling of area are theorems, not approximations. In practice, manufacturing tolerances mean real-world scaled objects have small imperfections, but the mathematical relationships hold exactly.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

3. *Area after ×4 enlargement.* 20 × 4² = 20 × 16 = 320 cm².

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Photo print.* Dimensions: 4×7 = 28 cm, 3×7 = 21 cm. Area: 12 × 49 = 588 cm² (vs original 12 cm²). Resolution: same number of pixels spread over 49× the area → pixels per cm² drops by factor 49 → appears less sharp.
2. *Map.* Real dimensions: 3.2 × 10 000 = 32 000 cm = 320 m. 2.1 × 10 000 = 210 m. Area = 320 × 210 = 67 200 m² = 6.72 hectares. If it appears square on the map, it IS square in reality (enlargement preserves shape).
3. *Engineer's drawing.* Real room: 12 × 50 = 600 cm = 6 m. 8 × 50 = 400 cm = 4 m. Floor area = 6 × 4 = 24 m². Tiling cost = 24 × $85 = $2 040.
4. *News graphic.* Enlarging a small section makes minor fluctuations look dramatic because visual impact scales with area (k²). A factor-5 enlargement makes the section appear 25× larger visually, exaggerating trends that are minor in the full context. Responsible data visualisation shows full context alongside zoomed sections.
5. *Mosaic mural.* Dimensions: 30×8 = 240 cm × 20×8 = 160 cm (2.4 m × 1.6 m). Tiles cover area, not length. Area ratio = 8² = 64. Tiles needed: 450 × 64 = 28 800 (not 3 600). Each tile covers the same area, and the mural has 64× the area.
