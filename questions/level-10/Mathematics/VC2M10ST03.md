# VC2M10ST03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10ST03. Construct two-way tables and discuss possible relationship between categorical variables.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A **two-way table** (contingency table) displays the frequency of data for two categorical variables simultaneously.
  - Comparing row or column percentages reveals whether a **relationship (association)** exists between the variables.
  - If the distribution of one variable is the same regardless of the category of the other, the variables are not associated.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct two-way tables** and to investigate whether categorical variables are associated.
- You are learning to calculate and compare proportions to identify possible relationships.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Construct a two-way frequency table from raw data.
- Calculate row percentages, column percentages, and overall percentages.
- Compare proportions to determine whether an association exists.
- Describe the nature of any association in context.
- Recognise that association does not prove causation.

## Core content

**1. What is a two-way table?** A two-way table organises data by two categorical variables (e.g. gender and preferred sport, year level and transport mode). Rows represent one variable; columns represent the other. Each cell shows the count of observations in that combination. Margins (totals) show the total for each row and column. *Quick check:* A survey records student gender (male/female) and whether they walk to school (yes/no). What are the two categorical variables?

**2. Constructing from raw data.** Given a list of data pairs, tally each combination into the appropriate cell. Example: 30 students surveyed — Male+Walk (8), Male+Don't walk (7), Female+Walk (10), Female+Don't walk (5). Fill the table with these frequencies and calculate row totals, column totals, and grand total. *Quick check:* Verify: 8 + 7 + 10 + 5 = 30.

**3. Row and column percentages.** Raw frequencies can be misleading if groups have different sizes. Convert to percentages for fair comparison. **Row percentages:** each cell ÷ row total × 100. **Column percentages:** each cell ÷ column total × 100. Choose based on your question: "Of males, what percentage walk?" → row percentage (row = males). *Quick check:* 8 out of 15 males walk = what percentage?

**4. Identifying association.** If the row percentages are similar across categories, there is **no association** (the variables are independent). If they differ substantially, there **is an association**. Example: if 53% of males walk and 67% of females walk, there appears to be an association between gender and walking to school. *Quick check:* 60% of Year 9 and 62% of Year 10 students prefer pizza. Is there likely an association between year level and pizza preference?

**5. Describing association.** Always describe in context: "Females are more likely to walk to school than males (67% vs 53%)." State the direction and magnitude of the difference. Be cautious — association does not mean one variable causes the other. There may be confounding factors (distance to school, availability of transport). *Quick check:* A two-way table shows 80% of pet owners exercise daily vs 50% of non-pet-owners. Describe the association. Is it causal?

**6. Worked example — transport and year level.**
100 students surveyed on transport mode (bus, car, walk) and year level (Year 9, Year 10).

|  | Bus | Car | Walk | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Year 9 | 15 | 20 | 15 | 50 |
| Year 10 | 10 | 15 | 25 | 50 |
| Total | 25 | 35 | 40 | 100 |

*Follow the thinking:*
- Row percentages for Year 9: Bus 30%, Car 40%, Walk 30%.
- Row percentages for Year 10: Bus 20%, Car 30%, Walk 50%.
- Comparison: Year 10 students are more likely to walk (50% vs 30%) and less likely to use car/bus.
- Association: there appears to be a relationship between year level and transport mode.
- Context: Year 10 students may have more independence, live closer, or prefer walking with friends.
- Caution: this doesn't prove year level causes transport choice — many confounders possible.

**7. Limitations and cautions.** Two-way tables show association, not causation. Small sample sizes may show apparent associations due to chance. Combining categories can mask or create associations (Simpson's paradox). Always report sample size, and be cautious with conclusions. *Quick check:* A two-way table is based on 12 responses. Should you draw strong conclusions about association?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS Census produces two-way tables for Victorian data — cross-tabulating employment status with education level, housing type with suburb, or health outcomes with demographic groups. These tables inform government policy on education, housing and health. VicHealth uses two-way tables to investigate associations between lifestyle factors and health outcomes across Victorian communities.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Two-way tables frequently appear in research about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health, education and employment. Critical literacy means understanding that association (e.g. between remoteness and health outcomes) does not automatically imply that living remotely causes poor health — systemic factors including service access, historical policy, and funding are confounders. Communities benefit from being able to read and critique these tables. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Two-way tables are used in everyday contexts: market research (age group × product preference), health (diet type × disease incidence), education (school type × achievement level). Students from diverse backgrounds can investigate associations relevant to their communities.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Survey 20 classmates on two questions: do you prefer morning or afternoon? Do you prefer sweet or savoury snacks? Construct a two-way table. Calculate row percentages. Is there an association between time preference and snack preference?
2. **Civic.** A council surveys 200 residents about a new park proposal (support/oppose) and area (north/south). North: 70 support, 30 oppose. South: 60 support, 40 oppose. Construct the two-way table. Compare support rates. Is there an association between area and support?
3. **Vocational.** A business surveys 150 customers on satisfaction (satisfied/not) and purchase method (online/in-store). Online: 55 satisfied, 25 not. In-store: 50 satisfied, 20 not. Is there an association between method and satisfaction?
4. **Ethical.** A news article claims "people who read newspapers are more politically engaged" based on a two-way table. Identify two confounding variables that might explain the association without reading causing engagement.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Use data to construct a two-way table for language spoken at home (English/other) and participation in a school program (yes/no). If participation rates differ, discuss whether this indicates a barrier or simply a preference. What additional information would you need?

## Memorable takeaways
- Two-way table: rows = one category, columns = another, cells = counts.
- Calculate row/column percentages for fair comparison.
- If percentages are similar across categories → no association.
- If percentages differ substantially → association exists.
- Association ≠ causation — always consider confounders.

## Stretch yourself
Design and conduct a statistical investigation using a two-way table. Choose two categorical variables you hypothesise might be related (e.g. year level and study location preference; device type and social media usage). Survey at least 40 people. Construct the two-way table, calculate row percentages, and determine whether an association appears to exist. Then: calculate the "expected frequencies" (what you'd expect in each cell if there were NO association — row total × column total ÷ grand total). Compare observed with expected. Discuss: is the observed difference large enough to be meaningful, or could it be due to chance in a small sample? Research the chi-squared test (you don't need to perform it, just explain its purpose). 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Comparing raw frequencies when group sizes differ.** If there are 80 males and 40 females, comparing "12 males vs 10 females" is misleading — use percentages (15% vs 25%).
- **Concluding causation from a table.** Two-way tables show association only. Always consider confounders.
- **Ignoring sample size.** An apparent association in 10 people may be due to chance. Larger samples give more reliable results.
- **Combining categories inappropriately.** Merging categories can hide associations (or create artificial ones — Simpson's paradox).
- **Forgetting context.** "70% vs 60%" is meaningless without knowing what the percentages represent.

A note on certainty. The numbers in a two-way table are exact counts from the sample. Whether those numbers indicate a genuine population-level association (rather than sampling variability) requires inferential methods — specifically, a chi-squared test of independence (studied later). At this level, you can describe apparent associations and discuss whether differences seem meaningful, while acknowledging that formal testing is needed for definitive conclusions.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Survey.* Answers vary. Calculate row percentages and compare. If "morning people" prefer sweet at 70% but "afternoon people" at 45%, there's an apparent association.
2. *Council.* North support: 70/100 = 70%. South support: 60/100 = 60%. Difference of 10 percentage points suggests a moderate association — north residents are somewhat more supportive. Possible confounders: proximity to park, demographics.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Variables: year level (9/10) and preferred study location (home/library/café). Survey 50 students. Example observed table: Year 9: home 15, library 5, café 5 (total 25). Year 10: home 8, library 10, café 7 (total 25). Row %: Year 9: 60% home. Year 10: 32% home. Expected if no association: each cell = row × column ÷ 50. Home expected for Year 9: 25 × 23/50 = 11.5. Observed 15 vs expected 11.5 — Year 9 overrepresented at home. Chi-squared test purpose: it quantifies how different observed is from expected, producing a p-value that indicates whether the difference is statistically significant or likely due to chance. With only 50 people, moderate differences might not be significant. Larger samples provide more certainty.
