# VC2M10P02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10P02. Describe the results of two- and three-step chance experiments, both with and without replacements, assign probabilities to outcomes and determine probabilities of events; investigate the concept of independence.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Multi-step experiments are analysed using tree diagrams or systematic lists.
  - "With replacement" means probabilities stay constant; "without replacement" means probabilities change at each step.
  - Two events are **independent** if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **describe and calculate probabilities** for multi-step experiments with and without replacement.
- You are learning to investigate and apply the concept of independence.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- List outcomes and assign probabilities for two- and three-step experiments.
- Distinguish between with-replacement and without-replacement scenarios.
- Use the multiplication rule for independent events.
- Test whether events are independent using P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
- Use tree diagrams to organise multi-step calculations.

## Core content

**1. Two-step experiments.** A two-step experiment involves performing two actions in sequence. Example: draw a card, then draw another. The sample space is all possible ordered pairs of outcomes. Each outcome has a probability found by multiplying along branches of a tree diagram. *Quick check:* A bag has 3 red and 2 blue marbles. You draw one marble, note its colour, then draw another. How many outcomes are possible if drawing without replacement?

**2. With replacement.** After each draw, the item is returned. Probabilities remain constant at each step. Example: bag with 3 red, 2 blue. P(red, then red) = 3/5 × 3/5 = 9/25. The second draw is not affected by the first — the events are **independent**. *Quick check:* Same bag, with replacement. P(blue, then red)?

**3. Without replacement.** The item is NOT returned. The sample space shrinks and probabilities change. Example: same bag. P(red first) = 3/5. Now 2 red, 2 blue remain. P(red second | red first) = 2/4 = 1/2. P(red, then red) = 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10. Events are **not independent** — the first outcome affects the second. *Quick check:* Same bag, without replacement. P(blue first, then red)?

**4. Three-step experiments.** Extend the tree diagram to three branches. Multiply probabilities along each path. Example: flip a coin three times. P(HHH) = 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8. P(exactly 2 heads) = 3 × (1/2)³ = 3/8 (three paths: HHT, HTH, THH). *Quick check:* A die is rolled three times. What is P(three sixes)?

**5. Independence.** Events A and B are **independent** if P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Equivalently, P(A|B) = P(A) — knowing B occurred doesn't change A's probability. With replacement → independent. Without replacement → not independent. Rolling a die and flipping a coin → independent. Drawing two cards without replacement → not independent. *Quick check:* Are these independent? Event A: first card is red. Event B: second card is red (without replacement from a standard deck).

**6. Worked example — lottery selection.**
A raffle has 100 tickets. You buy 5. Three prizes are drawn without replacement.

*Follow the thinking:*
- P(win 1st prize) = 5/100.
- Given you didn't win 1st: P(win 2nd prize) = 5/99.
- Given you didn't win 1st or 2nd: P(win 3rd prize) = 5/98.
- P(win no prizes) = 95/100 × 94/99 × 93/98 = 831,870/970,200 ≈ 0.8575.
- P(win at least one prize) = 1 − 0.8575 = 0.1425 ≈ 14.3%.
- Without replacement matters: if tickets were replaced, P(no prize) = (0.95)³ = 0.857, similar but not identical.
- The events are not independent (without replacement).

**7. Testing for independence.** To test whether observed events are independent: calculate P(A), P(B), and P(A and B) from data. If P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B), they're independent. If not, they're dependent (associated). Example: in 100 students, 60 pass maths, 50 pass English, 35 pass both. P(M) × P(E) = 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.30. P(M and E) = 0.35. Since 0.35 ≠ 0.30, the events are NOT independent — passing maths and English are associated. *Quick check:* 40% of students play sport, 30% play music, 12% play both. Are sport and music independent?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Tattersall's (now The Lott) lottery draws operate without replacement — each ball drawn changes the probabilities for remaining draws. The Melbourne Cup barrier draw determines horse positions without replacement. Cricket statistics (batting averages, conditional on conditions) use multi-step probability reasoning. Victorian gambling regulations require businesses to display accurate odds that account for replacement/non-replacement rules.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Many traditional games involve chance — possum-skin dice, throwing games, and selection processes for ceremonies. These games embed understanding of sequential outcomes and changing conditions. Resource allocation decisions (who hunts where, which area to burn) involve sequential conditional reasoning analogous to multi-step probability. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Card games (popular across many cultures) naturally involve without-replacement probability. Lotteries, raffles and games of chance are universal cultural phenomena. Understanding probability helps all students make informed decisions about gambling and risk.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You have 10 socks in a drawer: 6 black, 4 white. You grab two socks randomly without looking (without replacement). What is P(matching pair)? What is P(both black)?
2. **Civic.** A council randomly selects 3 members from a pool of 8 women and 4 men (without replacement). What is P(all three are women)? Is this different from P(at least one man)?
3. **Vocational.** A quality inspector tests 3 items from a batch of 20 (2 are defective) without replacement. What is P(no defectives found)? What is P(at least one defective)?
4. **Ethical.** A game show claims "each round is independent." Contestants draw cards from a deck without replacement. Is the show's claim accurate? What would make the rounds genuinely independent?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Design a three-step chance experiment using a standard deck of 52 cards. Calculate P(three hearts in a row) both with and without replacement. Which gives a higher probability and why?

## Memorable takeaways
- With replacement: probabilities stay constant → events are independent.
- Without replacement: probabilities change → events are not independent.
- Multiply along branches of a tree diagram.
- P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) if and only if A and B are independent.
- P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).

Then calculate P(at least one teacher). ## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting to adjust probabilities without replacement.** After removing one item, the total decreases by 1 and the count of that type may decrease.
- **Assuming independence without checking.** Without replacement automatically means events are dependent.
- **Confusing P(A or B) with P(A and B).** "Or" means add (minus overlap); "and" means multiply (along branches).
- **Forgetting the complement.** P(at least one) = 1 − P(none) is often easier than calculating all the "at least one" paths directly.
- **Order matters for counting.** In three-step experiments, count all ordered paths (HHT, HTH, THH are different paths).

## Try drawing this
Draw a tree diagram for drawing 2 marbles from a bag containing 4 red and 3 blue, WITHOUT replacement. Label all branches with probabilities. At the end of each path, multiply to get the path probability. Verify that all path probabilities sum to 1. Circle the paths for P(one of each colour) and add their probabilities.

A note on certainty. The multiplication rule and the definition of independence are mathematical certainties. Whether real-world events are truly independent is a modelling question — we assume independence when it is reasonable (e.g. separate coin flips) and account for dependence when items are physically removed or when evidence shows association. The mathematics is exact; the real-world application requires judgment about what assumptions hold.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Marble outcomes without replacement.* First draw: 5 options. Second: 4. Total ordered outcomes: 5 × 4 = 20.
2. *P(blue then red) with replacement.* P(B) × P(R) = 2/5 × 3/5 = 6/25.

4. *Three sixes.* P = (1/6)³ = 1/216.
5. *Card independence.* Not independent. P(red first) = 26/52 = 1/2. P(red second | red first) = 25/51 ≠ 1/2. First draw affects second.
6. *Raffle.* P(at least one prize) ≈ 14.3% (worked through in example).
7. *Sport and music.* P(S) × P(M) = 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.12. P(S and M) = 0.12. Since 0.12 = 0.12, they ARE independent.
