# VC2M9ST04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9ST04. Choose appropriate forms of display or visualisation for a given type of data; justify selections and interpret displays for a given context.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Different data types need different displays (categorical → bar/pie; numerical → histogram/box plot/scatter).
  - The choice of display affects how data is interpreted — some reveal patterns others hide.
  - Justifying your choice means explaining WHY it suits the data type and purpose.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Technologies (cross-curricular); Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **choose, justify and interpret** appropriate data displays for different types of data and contexts.
- You are learning that the choice of graph or visualisation matters — it can reveal or obscure patterns, and the selection should be deliberately matched to the data type and the question being asked.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Classify data as categorical (nominal or ordinal) or numerical (discrete or continuous).
- Match data type to appropriate display.
- Justify your display choice with reference to data type and purpose.
- Interpret key features of common displays (centre, spread, shape, outliers, clusters, trends).
- Critically evaluate whether a display is misleading or appropriate.

## Core content

**1. Data types determine displays.** **Categorical data** names categories (colour, suburb, transport mode). **Numerical data** measures quantities (height, temperature, time). Within these: categorical can be nominal (no order: colours) or ordinal (ordered: ratings). Numerical can be discrete (countable: goals scored) or continuous (measurable: height). The data type dictates which displays are valid. *Quick check:* Classify: (i) shoe size, (ii) favourite sport, (iii) temperature, (iv) satisfaction rating (1–5).

**2. Displays for categorical data.** **Bar chart:** compares frequencies of categories. Bars are separated (not touching). **Pie chart:** shows proportions of a whole. Best for 3–7 categories. **Stacked bar chart:** compares composition across groups. *When to use:* showing how a total is distributed across categories, comparing frequencies. *Quick check:* A survey asks 200 students their transport mode (walk, bus, car, bike, train). Which display would you choose and why?

**3. Displays for numerical data.** **Histogram:** shows distribution shape (symmetric, skewed, bimodal). Bars touch (continuous). **Box plot (box-and-whisker):** shows median, quartiles, range and outliers — ideal for comparing distributions. **Dot plot:** shows individual values for small datasets. **Stem-and-leaf plot:** preserves raw data while showing shape. *When to use:* showing distribution, spread, shape, comparing groups. *Quick check:* You have test scores for three classes and want to compare their spreads and medians. Which display?

**4. Displays for bivariate data.** **Scatter plot:** shows relationship between two numerical variables. Reveals positive/negative/no correlation, linear/non-linear patterns, clusters and outliers. **Line graph (time series):** shows change over time — one variable is time. *When to use:* investigating relationships, trends over time. *Quick check:* You want to see if study hours relate to test scores. Which display?

**5. Justifying your choice.** A good justification includes: (1) the data type (categorical/numerical), (2) what you want to show (comparison, distribution, relationship, trend), (3) why this display reveals that better than alternatives. Example: "I chose a box plot because I have numerical data from multiple groups and want to compare their medians and spreads side-by-side." *Quick check:* Why would a pie chart be a poor choice for displaying 20 categories?

**6. Worked example: choosing a display for road safety data.**

VicRoads provides data on road accidents by time of day (continuous), road type (categorical: freeway, arterial, local) and severity (ordinal: minor, serious, fatal).

*Follow the thinking:*
- Time of day: continuous numerical → histogram or time series.
- Road type: categorical → bar chart.
- Severity: ordinal → bar chart (ordered) or stacked bar (showing composition within road types).
- Combined analysis: accidents by time AND road type → grouped bar chart or multiple histograms.
- Justification: "I chose a grouped bar chart because I want to compare accident frequencies across road types for different time periods. This shows both the categorical comparison and the patterns across groups."

**7. Interpreting displays and spotting misleading graphs.** When interpreting: describe the shape (symmetric, skewed), centre (median, mode), spread (range, IQR), outliers and clusters. Misleading techniques to watch for: truncated y-axis (exaggerates differences), inappropriate scale, pie charts that don't sum to 100%, 3D effects that distort proportions, cherry-picked time ranges. *Quick check:* A bar graph starts its y-axis at 50 instead of 0. How might this mislead a reader?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS (Australian Bureau of Statistics) and Victorian government publish data using carefully chosen visualisations — census data uses maps (choropleth), demographic data uses population pyramids, and economic data uses time series. Media outlets across Melbourne make daily choices about how to display COVID, weather and sports data. The Bureau of Meteorology uses colour-mapped visualisations for weather data across Victoria.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Data sovereignty is important for First Nations communities — how data about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples is displayed affects how it is interpreted and what actions follow. Inappropriate visualisations can misrepresent community strengths. Organisations like the Mayi Kuwayu study centre Indigenous data governance, including how data visualisation choices carry responsibility. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Health data across diverse communities needs culturally appropriate visualisation — language accessibility, visual literacy and cultural sensitivity in presentation matter. Data dashboards used by multicultural community organisations must communicate clearly to audiences with varying statistical literacy.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You survey 50 classmates about daily screen time (hours per day). The data is continuous and ranges from 0.5 to 8 hours. Choose a display, justify your choice, and describe what features (shape, centre, spread) you would look for.
2. **Civic.** A council collects data on: (a) suburb of residence (categorical), (b) household recycling rate in kg/week (continuous), (c) satisfaction with waste services (ordinal, 1–5 stars). Choose an appropriate display for each dataset. Justify each choice.
3. **Vocational.** A construction company tracks project completion times. They have data for 40 projects showing planned days vs actual days. Which display would best show whether projects typically run over time? Justify. What would a pattern above the y = x line mean?
4. **Ethical.** A news article shows a pie chart of government spending with 15 slices, some less than 2%. Explain why this display is problematic. Suggest a better display and justify. Discuss how poor visualisation choices can mislead the public about budget priorities.
5. **Cross-cultural.** An international school collects data on languages spoken at home (categorical, 12+ languages) and maths test scores (continuous). A researcher wants to see if language background relates to scores. Choose a display that shows the relationship fairly without stigmatising any group. Justify.

## Memorable takeaways
- Categorical data → bar chart, pie chart. Numerical → histogram, box plot, scatter.
- Match the display to: data type + what you want to show.
- Justify: name the data type, state your purpose, explain why this display fits.
- Interpret: shape, centre, spread, outliers, trends, clusters.
- Watch for misleading graphs: truncated axes, 3D effects, wrong chart type.

- **Words to keep close.** Categorical, numerical, discrete, continuous, nominal, ordinal, histogram, box plot, scatter plot, bar chart, pie chart, distribution, correlation, justify, interpret, misleading.
- **Try this picture.** Create a decision flowchart: "Is the data categorical or numerical?" → if categorical: "Bar or pie" → if numerical: "One variable? → histogram/box plot. Two variables? → scatter plot. Over time? → line graph."
- **A different way to show what you know.** Take one dataset (e.g. class test results) and display it three different ways (histogram, box plot, dot plot). Write a paragraph comparing what each display reveals and hides.

## Stretch yourself

(a) Classify each variable by type. (b) Choose an appropriate display for each individual variable and justify. (c) Choose displays to investigate the relationship between income and education level, and between commute time and transport mode. Justify. ## Watch out for these traps
- "I used a pie chart for 15+ categories." Pie charts become unreadable with many slices. Use a bar chart instead.
- "I used a bar chart for continuous data." Continuous data needs a histogram (touching bars showing ranges), not a bar chart (separated bars for categories).
- "I chose a graph but didn't justify why." Always explain: what data type, what you want to show, why this display reveals it best.
- "I used a line graph for categorical data." Line graphs imply a continuous connection between points — only appropriate when the x-axis is ordered and continuous (usually time).

## Try drawing this
A decision tree for choosing data displays: Start: "What type of data?" → Categorical → "How many categories?" → Few (≤7): Pie chart. Many (>7): Bar chart. → Numerical → "How many variables?" → One: Histogram or box plot. → Two: Scatter plot. → Over time: Line graph.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Classify.* (i) Shoe size: numerical, discrete (ordered set of specific values). (ii) Favourite sport: categorical, nominal. (iii) Temperature: numerical, continuous. (iv) Satisfaction 1–5: categorical, ordinal.
2. *Transport mode.* Bar chart — categorical data, comparing frequencies across 5 distinct categories. Pie chart also acceptable (5 categories, showing proportion of total). Bar chart is better if you want to compare specific numbers; pie chart if you want proportional feel.
3. *Comparing three classes.* Parallel box plots — show median, IQR and range for each class side by side for direct comparison.
4. *Study hours vs test scores.* Scatter plot — two numerical variables, investigating relationship/correlation.
5. *20-category pie chart.* Too many slices → most are tiny, impossible to compare visually. Many would be < 5% and indistinguishable. A bar chart (possibly horizontal) displays 20 categories readably.
6. *Truncated y-axis.* Makes small differences look large. A bar going from 50 to 55 (a 10% increase) fills the same visual space as going from 0 to 55 — massively exaggerating the difference.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Screen time.* Histogram (continuous numerical data, showing distribution shape). Look for: symmetric vs skewed, typical value (mean/median), spread (range, IQR), any outliers (very high screen time). A box plot is also valid if comparing to other groups.
2. *Council data.* (a) Suburb: bar chart (categorical, many categories). (b) Recycling kg/week: histogram (continuous, shows distribution). (c) Satisfaction: bar chart with ordered categories 1–5 (ordinal — bars in rating order show the distribution of responses).
3. *Construction projects.* Scatter plot with planned days on x-axis, actual days on y-axis. The line y = x represents "on time." Points above this line = over time. Justification: two numerical variables, investigating relationship. Pattern above y = x means systematic underestimation of project duration.
4. *Pie chart with 15 slices.* Problematic: slices under 2% are invisible, 15 slices are too many to compare visually, hard to read percentages. Better: horizontal bar chart sorted by size — allows precise comparison of all categories. Misleading potential: tiny slices get ignored by readers, making small spending areas seem unimportant.
5. *Language and scores.* Parallel box plots grouped by language background — shows score distributions for each group without individual identification. Justification: comparing numerical distributions across categorical groups. Fair because it shows variability within each group (avoiding stereotypes about single averages) and highlights overlapping distributions.

Transport mode: categorical nominal. (b) Income: histogram (shows distribution shape, skewness). Education: histogram or dot plot. Commute: histogram. Transport: bar chart.
