# VC2M9ST01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9ST01. Analyse reports of surveys in digital media and elsewhere for information on how data was obtained around everyday questions and issues involving at least one numerical and at least one categorical variable, to estimate population means and medians.

- You are learning to **analyse survey reports** by examining data collection methods, variables involved, and how sample statistics estimate population values.
- You are learning to distinguish between sample statistics and population parameters.

By the end of this you should be able to:

## Core content

**1. Surveys and variables.** A survey collects data from a sample to learn about a population. Variables can be **numerical** (measured with numbers: age, hours, income) or **categorical** (classified into groups: gender, suburb, opinion). Most surveys involve both. Example: "How many hours do you exercise per week?" (numerical) crossed with "What is your age group?" (categorical). *Quick check:* Identify the numerical and categorical variables in: "A survey of 500 Australians asked their state of residence and annual income."

**2. How data is obtained.** Key information to look for: Who conducted the survey? Who was the population? How was the sample selected (random, voluntary, convenience)? What was the sample size? What was the response rate? When was it conducted? This information determines whether the results are trustworthy. *Quick check:* A website poll asks "Do you support the new policy?" and gets 2000 responses. What concerns arise about how data was obtained?

**3. Sample statistics as estimates.** The sample mean (x̄) estimates the population mean (μ). The sample median estimates the population median. The accuracy of these estimates depends on sample size and representativeness. Larger, more representative samples give better estimates. A biased sample gives biased estimates — no matter how large. *Quick check:* A sample of 50 students at one school has mean screen time of 4.2 hours. Is this a good estimate of all Australian teenagers' screen time?

**4. Mean vs median in reports.** Media often reports "average" without specifying mean or median. For skewed data (income, house prices), the mean is pulled toward outliers while the median stays central. Reports may strategically choose the higher or lower value to support their narrative. *Quick check:* "Average house price is $1.2 million" — would the median be higher or lower? Why does this matter?

**5. Reading survey methodology.** Look for: margin of error (±3% means the true value is likely within 3% of the reported figure), confidence level (95% means if repeated 100 times, 95 would capture the true value), and weighting (adjusting for over/under-represented groups). *Quick check:* A poll says "62% support (±4%, 95% confidence)." What range might the true support level be?

**6. Worked example — analysing a media survey report.**
Report: "Survey finds Victorians spend average of $85/week on groceries."

*Follow the thinking:*
- Who conducted it? A supermarket chain (potential conflict of interest).
- Sample: 1200 shoppers surveyed in-store at 5 locations over 1 week.
- Variables: spending (numerical), store location (categorical).
- Concerns: only surveyed people already in-store (misses online shoppers, people at markets, people who shop elsewhere). Potential bias toward the chain's customers.
- Mean or median? Not specified. If income-diverse sample, mean may be pulled up by big spenders.
- Is $85 a good population estimate? Probably biased upward — people in that specific chain may spend more than average. The sample is not representative of ALL Victorians.
- What's missing? Response rate, demographic breakdown, confidence interval.

**7. Evaluating digital media reports.** Digital media adds challenges: clickbait headlines exaggerate findings, methodology may not be linked, interactive graphics can mislead, and "surveys" may be unscientific polls. Always: check the source, look for methodology, note sample size, and consider who benefits from the reported conclusion. *Quick check:* A social media infographic says "73% of people want longer weekends." What would you need to know before trusting this?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS Census surveys every Australian — the gold standard for representative data. Victorian media regularly reports survey findings on housing affordability, health behaviours, and social attitudes. The Herald Sun, The Age, and ABC all report survey data that students can critically analyse. The Victorian Government uses survey data from the Victorian Population Health Survey to allocate health funding.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Survey data about First Nations communities requires particular scrutiny: historically, surveys have used non-representative sampling (urban only, or excluding remote communities), deficit-framing (asking what's wrong rather than what's strong), and results have been used without community consent. Data sovereignty means communities should control how data about them is collected, reported and used. Critical analysis of survey methodology is an act of self-determination. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Survey participation can be affected by language barriers, cultural attitudes to sharing personal information, and trust in institutions. Students from diverse backgrounds can evaluate whether surveys are likely to include or exclude their communities, and what impact non-participation has on reported statistics.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Find a survey result reported in today's news. Identify: population, sample, numerical variable(s), categorical variable(s), sample size, and method. Is the mean or median reported? Could the estimate be biased?
2. **Civic.** A council surveys residents about park satisfaction. They survey 200 people at the park on a Saturday morning. Identify potential bias. Who is missing from this sample? How would this affect the mean satisfaction score?
3. **Vocational.** A real estate report says "median house price in Suburb X is $950,000 (based on 15 sales last month)." Is 15 sales a reliable sample? What could make this median unrepresentative of the suburb's true median?
4. **Ethical.** A fast-food company surveys 500 of its customers and reports "Australians eat fast food 3 times per week on average." Evaluate this claim. What sampling bias exists? How might this differ from the true population mean?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Australia's Census achieves ~95% response rate through compulsory participation. Many countries have voluntary surveys with 30–60% response rates. Discuss how response rate affects the reliability of population estimates. Who is likely to not respond?

## Memorable takeaways
- Sample mean estimates population mean; sample median estimates population median.
- Accuracy depends on sample size AND representativeness.
- Always ask: who, how many, how selected, what's missing?
- Mean vs median: choose based on skewness; media may choose strategically.
- Biased sample → biased estimate, regardless of sample size.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Confusing sample statistic with population parameter.** A sample mean of 4.2 doesn't mean the population mean IS 4.2 — it's an estimate.
- **Ignoring non-response.** If only 30% respond, the 70% who didn't may differ systematically.
- **Assuming large samples are automatically good.** A million-person online poll is still biased if only certain people see it.
- **Not distinguishing mean from median.** "Average" in media could be either — and the choice matters for skewed data.
- **Trusting the headline.** The headline often overstates what the data actually shows. Read the methodology.

A note on certainty. The mathematics of estimation is well-established — formulas for margin of error and confidence intervals are exact given assumptions about sampling. The uncertainty lies in whether those assumptions hold: is the sample truly representative? Are responses honest? Is the question unbiased? Mathematical tools quantify sampling uncertainty; critical thinking evaluates whether the assumptions behind those tools are met in practice.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Variables.* State of residence = categorical. Annual income = numerical.
2. *Website poll concerns.* Voluntary response (only motivated people respond). Self-selected sample. No control over who participates. May not represent the broader population. Possible multiple responses.
3. *50 students at one school.* Unlikely representative of all Australian teenagers — one school, one socioeconomic area, one location. Better: random sample from multiple schools across states and regions.
4. *House prices.* Median would be lower (income/price data is positively skewed — a few very expensive houses pull the mean up). Matters because most people experience the median, not the mean.
5. *62% ± 4%.* True support likely between 58% and 66% (62 − 4 to 62 + 4).
6. *Grocery survey.* Analysed in worked example — biased upward due to in-store sampling.
7. *Social media "73%" claim.* Need: who was surveyed? Sample size? How selected? What exactly was asked? Who created the infographic? What's the methodology? Without this, the claim is unverifiable.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *News survey.* Answers vary. Apply the checklist: source, population, sample, method, variables, statistic, potential bias.
2. *Park survey.* Bias: only captures people who actually USE the park on Saturday mornings. Misses: non-users, weekday visitors, people who stopped coming due to dissatisfaction, people with mobility issues. Effect: mean satisfaction likely biased upward (happy users are over-represented).
3. *15 house sales.* Very small sample — one unusually expensive or cheap sale shifts the median significantly. May also be seasonal (summer sales differ from winter). If only expensive houses sold that month (e.g. due to developer release), median is unrepresentative of actual suburb values.
4. *Fast-food company survey.* Major sampling bias: surveying their OWN customers captures heavy fast-food users, not the general population. The true population mean is almost certainly lower than 3 times/week. Conflict of interest: company benefits from normalising frequent fast-food consumption.
5. *Response rate.* Non-respondents often differ systematically: harder to reach (shift workers, homeless, remote), less engaged, potentially different demographics. Low response rates → the sample represents only the "easy to reach and willing to participate" subset. Compulsory participation minimises this but has ethical trade-offs (privacy concerns).

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Two screen time reports: Report A (university study, random sample of 2000 Australians aged 15–65, mean = 6.8 hours/day). Report B (tech company survey, 5000 app users, mean = 8.2 hours/day). Differences: Report B samples from existing tech users (biased upward). Report A is more representative but still may under-sample people without phones. A's estimate likely closer to population truth. Improved design: stratified random sample across age groups, regions and socioeconomic levels; use multiple measurement methods (app tracking + diary); larger sample; include non-smartphone users; publish methodology and margin of error.
