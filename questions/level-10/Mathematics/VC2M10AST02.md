## Core content

**1. Range.** Range = maximum − minimum. The simplest measure of spread. For {3, 7, 8, 12, 15}: range = 15 − 3 = 12. Limitation: uses only two data values, so it is heavily affected by outliers and tells nothing about how data is distributed between the extremes. *Quick check:* Find the range of {20, 25, 30, 35, 100}. What is its limitation here?

**2. Interquartile range (IQR).** IQR = Q3 − Q1, where Q1 is the median of the lower half and Q3 is the median of the upper half. The IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of data. For {3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 15}: Q1 = 5, Q3 = 12, IQR = 7. The IQR is resistant to outliers because it ignores extreme values. *Quick check:* Find the IQR of {2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18}.

**3. Standard deviation (SD).** SD = √(Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/n). It measures the typical distance of data values from the mean. Unlike the IQR, SD uses every data value, making it sensitive to outliers. For symmetric distributions, SD is the most informative measure of spread. For skewed distributions or data with outliers, the IQR is more reliable. *Quick check:* Which measure of spread is most affected by outliers — range, IQR or SD?

**4. Choosing the right measure.** Symmetric data with no outliers → SD is most useful (it uses all data and describes the normal spread). Skewed data → IQR is preferred (resistant to the long tail). Data with outliers → IQR (resistant) or report SD with and without outliers. Quick summary: range gives the crudest picture, IQR the middle 50%, SD the typical deviation from the mean. *Quick check:* Data on household income is typically right-skewed. Which measure of spread is most appropriate?

**5. Effect on interpretation.** The same data can tell different stories depending on the spread measure. For {10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 60}: range = 50 (suggests huge spread), IQR = 14 − 12 = 2 (suggests tight clustering), SD ≈ 17.8. The IQR better represents the typical spread because the range and SD are inflated by the outlier 60. Reporting only the range would misrepresent the data. *Quick check:* For {5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 50}, explain why the range is misleading.

**6. Worked example — comparing distributions.**
Test scores for two classes: Class A: {55, 60, 62, 65, 68, 70, 72, 75, 78, 80}. Class B: {30, 62, 64, 66, 68, 70, 72, 74, 76, 95}.

*Analysis:*
- Class A: range = 25, Q1 = 62, Q3 = 75, IQR = 13, mean = 68.5, SD ≈ 7.6.
- Class B: range = 65, Q1 = 64, Q3 = 74, IQR = 10, mean = 67.7, SD ≈ 15.0.
- The range and SD for Class B are much larger, but the IQR is actually smaller. The extreme values (30 and 95) inflate the range and SD but do not affect the IQR. For comparing typical performance, the IQR is more informative here.

**7. Distributions and appropriate measures.** Symmetric (bell-shaped): mean and SD are the best summary. Right-skewed (long tail to the right, e.g. income, house prices): median and IQR are preferred. Left-skewed (long tail to the left, e.g. age at retirement): median and IQR. Uniform (all values equally likely): range and SD give similar information. Bimodal: neither single measure fully captures the spread — report both or analyse each mode separately. *Quick check:* Name a real-world data set that is typically right-skewed.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Understanding which spread measure to use is critical in public health, education reporting and urban planning — areas where Victorian government agencies routinely analyse data distributions.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations communities have long understood variability in natural systems — the difference between a "typical" season and an extreme one. Statistical measures of spread formalise this understanding of how much variation is usual versus exceptional.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The interquartile range was developed by Francis Galton (19th century). Modern statistical practice, applied globally, emphasises choosing appropriate measures based on data characteristics rather than applying a single measure universally.

## Memorable takeaways
- Range = max − min — simple but uses only two values.
- IQR = Q3 − Q1 — spread of the middle 50%, resistant to outliers.
- SD — typical distance from the mean, uses all data, sensitive to outliers.
- Symmetric data → SD. Skewed or outlier-affected data → IQR.
- The choice of spread measure changes the story the data tells.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Reporting only the range.** Range is the least informative measure — it tells nothing about how data is distributed.
- **Using SD for skewed data.** SD is distorted by skewness — use IQR instead.
- **Confusing IQR with range.** IQR is Q3 − Q1 (middle 50%), not max − min.
- **Forgetting context.** A "large" spread depends on the scale — always interpret in context.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Range of {20, 25, 30, 35, 100}.* 100 − 20 = 80. Limitation: the outlier 100 makes the range enormous, misrepresenting the typical spread of the other values (which span only 15).
2. *IQR of {2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18}.* Q1 = 4 + (6−4)/2 = 5 (or using n/4 method: Q1 = 6, Q3 = 14). IQR = 14 − 6 = 8. (Methods may vary slightly; accept consistent application.)
3. *Most affected by outliers.* Range (uses only extremes) and SD (uses all values including extremes). IQR is least affected.
4. *Household income.* IQR — income is right-skewed, so IQR resists the influence of very high earners.
5. *{5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 50}.* Range = 45, but five of six values are identical. The range suggests wide spread when the data is actually highly concentrated at 5 with one outlier.
6. *Class comparison.* IQR is more informative — it shows Class B's typical spread is actually tighter despite the larger range and SD caused by extreme scores.
7. *Right-skewed example.* House prices, personal income, insurance claims, social media followers.
