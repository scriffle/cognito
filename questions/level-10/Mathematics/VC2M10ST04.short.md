# VC2M10ST04 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Analyse claims, inferences and conclusions of statistical reports in the media and other places, by linking claims to displays, statistics and representative data.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Statistical claims should be linked to their data source, sample, statistic and display. Misleading statistics come from biased samples, inappropriate measures, manipulated graphs, and overgeneralised conclusions. Critical evaluation asks: is the sample representative? Is the display fair? Does the conclusion follow?

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **critically analyse statistical claims** by examining the data, methods and displays behind them.
- You should identify misleading elements and judge whether conclusions are supported by evidence.

## Core content

**Claims need evidence.** Every claim links to: data source, collection method, statistic, and display. Weak links = unreliable claim.

**Representative samples.** Must reflect the population. Biased: voluntary response, convenience sampling, undercoverage. A poll of 100 Twitter users ≠ all Australians.

**Appropriate statistics.** Mean vs median (use median for skewed data). Percentages mislead with small samples. "Doubled" from 1 to 2 is meaningless.

**Misleading displays.** Truncated y-axis, unequal intervals, 3D distortion, cherry-picked time periods. A bar chart from 95% to 100% exaggerates tiny differences.

**Overgeneralisation.** Small samples, non-representative groups, correlation claimed as causation, extrapolation beyond data. "Studies provide evidence, not proof."

**Worked example.** "Vitamin D prevents cancer" headline. Actual finding: association in 500 adults in one city. Confounders: outdoor activity, exercise, diet. Headline overgeneralises and prescribes action not supported by observational data.

**Evaluation checklist.** Who funded it? How large/representative is the sample? What statistic? Fair display? Does conclusion follow? Alternative explanations?

**Victorian context.** Victorian media reports on housing, crime, health — critical evaluation is citizenship. COVID-19 exposed Victorians to intensive statistical reporting. Aboriginal data sovereignty challenges deficit-framing and misrepresentation in media.

## Memorable takeaways
- Link every claim to: source, sample, statistic, display.
- Representative sample? Appropriate statistic? Fair display?
- Truncated axes make small differences look dramatic.
- Association ≠ causation. Studies ≠ proof.
- Who funded it? What's missing? Are there alternative explanations?

## Watch out for these traps
- Assuming large samples are automatically reliable (can still be biased).
- Taking headlines at face value (designed to attract, not to be precise).
- Ignoring what's NOT reported (confidence intervals, comparison groups).
- Assuming visual proportions match data proportions (3D, pictographs distort).

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *"9/10 dentists recommend."* Ask: how many surveyed? How selected? Company-funded? What alternatives?
- *Year 12–only uniform survey.* Not representative — one year level, about to leave, doesn't reflect whole school.
- *CEO mean salary.* Mean inflated by extreme high values (skewed). Median more representative.
- *Graph from 100 to 102.* 2% change looks dramatic. Fair graph from 0 would show near-identical bars.
- *Company funds its own "clinical proof."* Conflict of interest — funder benefits from positive results.
