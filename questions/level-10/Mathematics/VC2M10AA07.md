# VC2M10AA07 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AA07. Factorise both monic and non-monic quadratic expressions and solve a wide range of quadratic equations.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A monic quadratic has leading coefficient 1; a non-monic quadratic has a leading coefficient other than 1.
  - Three primary methods — factorisation, completing the square, and the quadratic formula — each suit different situations.
  - The discriminant Δ = b² − 4ac determines the number and nature of solutions.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **factorise monic and non-monic quadratic expressions** and to **solve quadratic equations** using multiple methods.
- You are learning to select the most efficient method, use the discriminant to predict solution types, and verify answers.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Factorise monic quadratics (x² + bx + c) by finding factors of c that sum to b.
- Factorise non-monic quadratics (ax² + bx + c where a ≠ 1) using grouping or cross-multiplication.
- Complete the square for any quadratic expression.
- Apply the quadratic formula x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a).
- Use the discriminant to determine whether solutions are real, repeated, or non-real.

## Core content

**1. Factorising monic quadratics.** For x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. For x² + 7x + 12: factors of 12 that sum to 7 are 3 and 4. So x² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4). *Quick check:* Factorise x² − 5x + 6.

**2. Factorising non-monic quadratics.** For ax² + bx + c where a ≠ 1, find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b, then split the middle term and group. For 6x² + 11x − 10: ac = −60. Numbers: 15 and −4 (15 × −4 = −60, 15 + (−4) = 11). Rewrite: 6x² + 15x − 4x − 10 = 3x(2x + 5) − 2(2x + 5) = (2x + 5)(3x − 2). *Quick check:* Factorise 2x² + 7x + 3.

**3. Completing the square.** Rewrite x² + bx + c in the form (x + p)² + q, where p = b/2 and q = c − (b/2)². For x² + 6x + 1: p = 3, q = 1 − 9 = −8. So x² + 6x + 1 = (x + 3)² − 8. This form reveals the vertex of the parabola: (−3, −8). *Quick check:* Complete the square for x² − 4x + 7.

**4. The quadratic formula.** For ax² + bx + c = 0, x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a). This works for every quadratic equation, including those that do not factorise neatly. For 2x² − 3x − 7 = 0: a = 2, b = −3, c = −7. x = (3 ± √(9 + 56))/4 = (3 ± √65)/4. *Quick check:* Use the quadratic formula to solve x² + 2x − 5 = 0.

**5. The discriminant.** Δ = b² − 4ac determines the nature of solutions. If Δ > 0: two distinct real solutions. If Δ = 0: one repeated real solution. If Δ < 0: no real solutions (two complex conjugate solutions). For 3x² − 2x + 4 = 0: Δ = 4 − 48 = −44 < 0, so no real solutions. *Quick check:* Without solving, determine the number of real solutions of x² − 6x + 9 = 0.

**6. Worked example — solving a geometric problem.**
A rectangle has length (x + 5) cm and width (x − 2) cm. Its area is 60 cm².

*Follow the thinking:*
- Area equation: (x + 5)(x − 2) = 60.
- Expand: x² + 3x − 10 = 60.
- Rearrange: x² + 3x − 70 = 0.
- Factorise: (x + 10)(x − 7) = 0.
- Solutions: x = −10 or x = 7. Since x − 2 > 0, x > 2. So x = 7.
- Dimensions: 12 cm × 5 cm. Check: 12 × 5 = 60 ✓.

**7. Choosing the best method.** Factorisation is fastest when factors are integers. Completing the square gives exact answers and reveals the vertex. The quadratic formula works universally. Strategy: try factorisation first; if the discriminant is not a perfect square, use the formula or completing the square. *Quick check:* Which method is most efficient for solving x² − 100 = 0?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Quadratic equations model projectile motion, structural arches and optimisation problems in Victorian engineering. The MCG's parabolic roof sections are designed using quadratic functions.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The parabolic trajectories inherent in spear-throwing and boomerang design demonstrate intuitive understanding of quadratic relationships in First Nations technologies.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Brahmagupta (7th century India) developed the earliest general quadratic formula. Al-Khwārizmī (9th century Persia) systematised quadratic equation solving by completing the square — giving algebra its name.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A phone is dropped from a height of 45 metres. Its height after t seconds is h = 45 − 5t². Find when the phone hits the ground.
2. **Civic.** A council needs a rectangular playground with area 200 m². If the length exceeds the width by 10 m, set up and solve a quadratic equation for the dimensions.
3. **Vocational.** A carpenter cuts a rectangular piece of wood from a square sheet of side x cm, leaving a border of 3 cm on two sides. The cut piece has area 40 cm². Find x.
4. **Ethical.** A student uses the quadratic formula and gets x = (5 ± √(−7))/4. The student writes "no solutions." Is this fully correct? Discuss.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research al-Khwārizmī's geometric method for completing the square. Then solve x² + 10x = 39 by completing the square, and compare with the formula.

## Memorable takeaways
- Monic quadratics: find factors of c that sum to b.
- Non-monic quadratics: find factors of ac that sum to b, then group.
- Completing the square: (x + b/2)² − (b/2)² + c.
- The quadratic formula works for every quadratic — it is the universal method.
- Δ > 0: two real solutions; Δ = 0: one repeated; Δ < 0: no real solutions.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Sign errors in the quadratic formula.** When b is negative, −b is positive. Handle the ± carefully.
- **Forgetting to set the equation to zero.** The formula requires ax² + bx + c = 0, not ax² + bx = c.
- **Rejecting negative solutions without context.** In pure algebra, negative solutions are valid. Only reject them when the context demands positive values (lengths, times, etc.).
- **Incomplete factorisation of non-monic quadratics.** After splitting the middle term, check that both groups share a common bracket factor.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Factorise x² − 5x + 6.* (x − 2)(x − 3).
2. *Factorise 2x² + 7x + 3.* ac = 6. Numbers: 6 and 1. 2x² + 6x + x + 3 = 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) = (x + 3)(2x + 1).
3. *Complete the square for x² − 4x + 7.* (x − 2)² − 4 + 7 = (x − 2)² + 3.
4. *Solve x² + 2x − 5 = 0.* x = (−2 ± √(4 + 20))/2 = (−2 ± √24)/2 = (−2 ± 2√6)/2 = −1 ± √6.
5. *Discriminant of x² − 6x + 9 = 0.* Δ = 36 − 36 = 0. One repeated real solution (x = 3).
6. *Rectangle worked example.* x = 7, dimensions 12 × 5, area 60.
7. *Most efficient for x² − 100 = 0.* Difference of squares: (x − 10)(x + 10) = 0. Factorisation.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Phone drop.* 45 − 5t² = 0 → t² = 9 → t = 3 (reject t = −3). Hits the ground after 3 seconds.
2. *Playground.* Width w, length w + 10. w(w + 10) = 200 → w² + 10w − 200 = 0 → (w + 20)(w − 10) = 0. w = 10 m, length = 20 m.
3. *Carpenter.* (x − 3)(x − 3) = 40 does not fit the problem. Correctly: (x − 3) and x give area, so x(x − 3) = 40 → x² − 3x − 40 = 0 → (x − 8)(x + 5) = 0. x = 8 cm.
4. *Complex solutions.* The student is correct that there are no real solutions, but it would be more complete to say "no real solutions; two complex solutions x = (5 ± i√7)/4." At this level, acknowledging complex numbers shows deeper understanding.
5. *Completing the square.* x² + 10x = 39 → (x + 5)² − 25 = 39 → (x + 5)² = 64 → x + 5 = ±8 → x = 3 or x = −13.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** (a) −5t² + 20t + 2 = 0. t = (−20 ± √(400 + 40))/(−10) = (−20 ± √440)/(−10). √440 = 2√110 ≈ 20.976. t = (−20 + 20.976)/(−10) ≈ −0.098 (reject) or t = (−20 − 20.976)/(−10) ≈ 4.10 s. (b) h(t) = −5(t² − 4t) + 2 = −5(t − 2)² + 20 + 2 = −5(t − 2)² + 22. Maximum height 22 m at t = 2 s. (c) Δ = 440 > 0: two real solutions (two times when h = 0, one before launch, one after landing). (d) Δ = v² + 40 < 0 has no solution for real v, since v² ≥ 0 and 40 > 0. The equation always has real solutions — the projectile always returns to ground level.
