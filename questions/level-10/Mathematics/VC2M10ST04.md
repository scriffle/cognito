# VC2M10ST04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10ST04. Analyse claims, inferences and conclusions of statistical reports in the media and other places, by linking claims to displays, statistics and representative data.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Statistical claims in media should be evaluated critically — linking the claim to the data, display and method behind it.
  - Key questions: Is the sample representative? Is the statistic appropriate? Does the display accurately represent the data? Does the conclusion follow from the evidence?
  - Misleading statistics arise from biased samples, inappropriate measures, manipulated graphs, and overgeneralised conclusions.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Civics and Citizenship.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **critically analyse statistical claims** by examining the data, methods and displays that underpin them.
- You are learning to identify when statistics are used misleadingly and to evaluate whether conclusions are justified.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Link a statistical claim to its underlying data and method.
- Identify whether a sample is representative.
- Evaluate whether the statistic used (mean, median, percentage) is appropriate.
- Detect misleading features in statistical displays.
- Judge whether a conclusion is supported by the evidence presented.

## Core content

**1. Claims and evidence.** A statistical claim is a statement that uses data to support an argument. Every claim should be traceable to: (1) a data source, (2) a method of collection, (3) a statistical measure, and (4) an appropriate display. If any link in this chain is weak, the claim may be unreliable. *Quick check:* "9 out of 10 dentists recommend Brand X." What questions would you ask about this claim?

**2. Representative samples.** A sample is representative if it accurately reflects the population it claims to represent. Biased samples: voluntary response (only strongly opinionated respond), convenience (survey friends only), undercoverage (missing groups). A poll of 100 Twitter users does not represent all Australians. *Quick check:* A school surveys only Year 12 students and concludes "students love the new uniform." Is this representative?

**3. Appropriate statistics.** Mean vs median: for skewed data, the median better represents "typical." Percentages can mislead with small samples ("50% of respondents" might be 1 out of 2). Relative vs absolute numbers: "doubled" (from 1 to 2) sounds dramatic but may be insignificant. *Quick check:* "Average CEO salary is $500,000." Would the mean or median be higher? Why?

**4. Misleading displays.** Graphs can mislead by: truncating the y-axis (making small differences look large), using unequal intervals, using 3D effects that distort proportions, cherry-picking time periods, or using pictographs where area grows faster than the data. A bar chart starting at 95% makes a 2% difference look enormous. *Quick check:* A graph shows sales "doubling" but the y-axis goes from 100 to 102. What is misleading?

**5. Overgeneralisation and unsupported conclusions.** Claims may go beyond what the data supports: generalising from a small or non-representative sample, implying causation from correlation, ignoring confounders, or extrapolating trends beyond the data range. "This study proves that..." is almost always an overstatement — studies provide evidence, not proof. *Quick check:* "A study of 20 university students shows phone use reduces sleep." Can this be generalised to all Australians?

**6. Worked example — analysing a media claim.**
Headline: "New study shows vitamin D prevents cancer — take supplements now!"

*Follow the thinking:*
- What does the study actually show? An association between higher vitamin D levels and lower cancer rates.
- Sample: 500 adults in one city over 2 years. Not necessarily representative of all people.
- Statistics: 15% lower cancer rate in the high-vitamin-D group. Is this statistically significant? (Need p-value, confidence interval.)
- Display: a bar chart showing the two groups — check if y-axis is truncated.
- Conclusion evaluation: association ≠ causation. People with higher vitamin D may be outdoors more, exercise more, have healthier diets. The headline overgeneralises to "prevents cancer" and prescribes action ("take supplements") not supported by an observational study.
- Better conclusion: "Higher vitamin D levels are associated with lower cancer rates in this sample. Further research, including clinical trials, is needed."

**7. A checklist for evaluating claims.** (1) Who conducted the study and who funded it? (2) How large and representative is the sample? (3) What statistic is used and is it appropriate? (4) Does the display accurately represent the data? (5) Does the conclusion follow from the evidence? (6) Are there alternative explanations? *Quick check:* A toothpaste company's own study says their product is "clinically proven." What concern does the funding source raise?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian media regularly reports statistics on housing prices, crime rates, health outcomes, and education — critical evaluation is an essential citizenship skill. The ABS provides open data; news organisations may select and frame it to support a narrative. During COVID-19, Victorians were exposed to extensive statistical reporting — understanding sampling, case rates, and graph interpretation was directly relevant to daily decision-making.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Statistics about First Nations people are frequently misrepresented in media — using deficit-framing, inappropriate comparisons, or non-representative samples. Critical statistical literacy enables Aboriginal communities to challenge harmful narratives, identify when data is used out of context, and advocate for strengths-based reporting. Data sovereignty — the right to control data about one's community — is connected to this critical evaluation. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Media statistical claims affect all communities — from health messaging to economic reporting to immigration data. Students from diverse backgrounds may encounter statistics about their communities and need the skills to evaluate accuracy and fairness. Understanding sample bias helps identify when communities are underrepresented in research.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Find a statistical claim in today's news (online or print). Identify: what data source? What sample? What statistic? Is the conclusion justified? Write a brief evaluation.
2. **Civic.** A politician claims "crime has increased 20% under the current government." Investigate: 20% from what base? Over what period? Is it all crime or specific types? Does the comparison use the same measurement method? What might make this claim misleading?
3. **Vocational.** An advertisement says "4 out of 5 mechanics recommend Brand Y oil." Evaluate: how were the mechanics selected? Was the survey independent? Were they given options or just asked about one brand? What information is missing?
4. **Ethical.** A graph in a news article about Indigenous health shows a y-axis starting at 60 (years of life expectancy), making a gap of 8 years look enormous visually. Redraw it with a y-axis from 0. How does this change perception? Discuss when truncating the axis is justified and when it's misleading.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Compare how the same data set (e.g. immigration statistics) is reported by two different news sources. Identify differences in: which statistics are chosen, how graphs are displayed, what conclusions are drawn. Discuss how framing affects perception.

## Memorable takeaways
- Every claim should link to data source, sample, statistic, and display.
- Check: is the sample representative? Is the statistic appropriate?
- Graphs can mislead: truncated axes, 3D effects, cherry-picked periods.
- Association ≠ causation. Studies provide evidence, not proof.
- Ask: who funded it? How large is the sample? Are there alternative explanations?

## Watch out for these traps
- **Assuming all statistics are reliable.** The source, sample and method all matter — impressive numbers can come from flawed studies.
- **Being fooled by large numbers.** "A study of 10,000 people" sounds reliable, but if the sample is biased (e.g. all from one website), size doesn't help.
- **Ignoring what's NOT reported.** Look for missing information: sample size, confidence intervals, comparison groups, time periods.
- **Taking headlines at face value.** Headlines are designed to attract attention, not to accurately represent findings. Always read the methodology.
- **Assuming visual proportions match data proportions.** 3D charts, pictographs, and truncated axes all distort visual interpretation.

A note on certainty. The tools for evaluating statistical claims (checking sample representativeness, appropriateness of statistics, fairness of displays) are well-established methodological principles. Applying them requires judgment — there is no single formula for "this claim is right/wrong." But the questions you ask (Who? How many? How selected? What's missing?) are the same questions professional statisticians ask, and they reliably identify the strengths and weaknesses of any statistical argument.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *9/10 dentists.* Questions: How many dentists total? How were they selected? Were they paid by the company? What were the alternatives offered? "Recommend" over what — nothing, or another specific product?
2. *Year 12 uniform survey.* Not representative — only one year level, likely about to leave and less affected. Need all year levels for a representative school opinion.
3. *CEO salary.* Mean would be higher than median — a few extremely high CEO salaries pull the mean up (positively skewed). Median better represents the "typical" CEO.
4. *Sales graph 100 to 102.* The y-axis exaggerates a 2% increase to look like a dramatic rise. If the axis started at 0, the bars would look almost identical.
5. *20 university students.* Cannot generalise — students are not representative of all ages, occupations and lifestyles. Sample too small and too narrow.
6. *Vitamin D.* Headline overgeneralises from observational association to causal prescription. Worked through in example.
7. *Company-funded study.* Potential conflict of interest — the funder benefits from positive results, which may influence study design, reporting, or interpretation.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *News claim.* Answers vary. Apply the checklist: source, sample, statistic, display, conclusion.
2. *Crime increase.* 20% from what base? If crime went from 5 to 6 per 10,000, that's 20% but still very low. Need: same measurement methods, same time span, same crime categories. May be misleading if comparing a pandemic year (low base) to a normal year.
3. *Mechanics ad.* Questions: sample size? Selection method? (Chosen by the company? Random?) Was the question "do you recommend Brand Y?" (leading) or "which brands would you recommend?" (open). Missing: sample size, survey independence, full results.
4. *Truncated axis.* With y-axis from 0: the bars for 70 and 78 years look similar (both tall). With axis from 60: the gap appears much larger visually. Truncating is sometimes justified when the context makes it clear (e.g. if everyone knows life expectancy is around 70–85), but it's misleading when it distorts perception for a general audience. Fair practice: clearly label the axis and note the non-zero start.
5. *Comparing sources.* Answers vary. Common differences: one source may highlight total numbers (large and alarming), another may use per-capita rates (smaller and contextualised). One may use a truncated graph; another may show the long-term trend. Framing shapes whether readers feel alarmed or reassured by the same data.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Three claims evaluated. Example: (1) News article: "Renewable energy jobs grew 15% last year" — source is industry report (potential bias), sample is one country, statistic appropriate (percentage growth), graph shows 5-year trend (fair). Reliable. (2) Ad: "Clinically proven to reduce wrinkles" — funded by manufacturer, sample 30 people, "clinically proven" undefined, before/after photos may be manipulated. Unreliable. (3) Social media: "This diet cured my diabetes" — anecdote (n=1), no control, no professional verification. Very unreliable. Ranking: 1 > 2 > 3. Fair rewrite of (3): "One person reports improved blood sugar after dietary changes, but individual experiences vary and should not replace medical advice." Journalists have responsibility to report methodology, sample sizes, and limitations. Public needs enough statistical literacy to ask "how do they know?" and "is this typical?"
