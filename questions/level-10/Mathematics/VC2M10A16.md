## Core content

**1. Why graphical and numerical methods?** Some equations cannot be solved exactly by algebra at this level — for example, 2ˣ = 3x or sin(x) = x/2. Graphical and numerical methods give approximate solutions to any desired degree of accuracy. They also help verify algebraic solutions and reveal how many solutions exist. *Quick check:* Can you solve 2ˣ = x + 5 exactly by algebra? How might you find the solution(s)?

**2. Solving by graphing — intersection method.** To solve f(x) = g(x), graph y = f(x) and y = g(x) on the same axes. Solutions are the x-coordinates of intersection points. Example: solve x² = 2x + 3 graphically. Graph y = x² and y = 2x + 3. They intersect at x = −1 and x = 3. Check algebraically: x² − 2x − 3 = (x−3)(x+1) = 0 ✓. *Quick check:* How would you solve 2ˣ = 8 − x graphically?

**3. Solving by graphing — x-intercept method.** Rearrange the equation to f(x) = 0, then graph y = f(x). Solutions are x-intercepts (where the graph crosses the x-axis). Example: solve x³ − 4x = 0 graphically. Graph y = x³ − 4x. The curve crosses the x-axis at x = −2, 0, 2. *Quick check:* To solve x² − 5x + 3 = 0 graphically, what would you graph?

**4. Systematic guess-check-and-refine.** Also called "interval bisection" or "trial and improvement." Steps: (1) Find an interval [a, b] where the function changes sign (one end positive, one negative — a solution lies between). (2) Test the midpoint. (3) Determine which half-interval contains the solution. (4) Repeat until you reach the desired accuracy.

Example: solve x³ = 20 (i.e. find ∛20).
- x = 2: 8 (too small). x = 3: 27 (too big). Solution between 2 and 3.
- x = 2.5: 15.625 (too small). Solution between 2.5 and 3.
- x = 2.7: 19.683 (too small). x = 2.8: 21.952 (too big). Solution between 2.7 and 2.8.
- x = 2.71: 19.902... (too small). x = 2.72: 20.123... (too big). Solution ≈ 2.71 to 2 d.p.

*Quick check:* Use one step of guess-check-refine to narrow the solution of x² = 7 (between which integers does √7 lie?).

**5. Using digital tools.** Desmos/GeoGebra: graph both sides and read intersection coordinates. Spreadsheets: set up a column of x-values, calculate f(x), look for sign changes. CAS calculators: use solve or nsolve commands. Digital tools let you zoom in on intersections for greater accuracy. *Quick check:* In Desmos, how would you solve eˣ = 5 − x?

**6. Worked example — finding all solutions.**
Solve x³ − 3x + 1 = 0 using graphical and numerical methods.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Graph y = x³ − 3x + 1 in Desmos.
- Observe: the curve crosses the x-axis three times (cubic can have up to 3 real roots).
- Read approximate x-intercepts: x ≈ −1.9, x ≈ 0.3, x ≈ 1.5.
- Refine the first root: f(−2) = −1, f(−1.8) = 0.568. Sign change between −2 and −1.8.
  - f(−1.9) = −0.259. Sign change between −1.9 and −1.8.
  - f(−1.88) = 0.073. Solution ≈ −1.88 (to 2 d.p.).
- Repeat for other roots. Always check the graph to confirm you've found ALL solutions.
- **Key insight:** The graph tells you HOW MANY solutions exist, so you know when you've found them all.

**7. Considering completeness.** A graph reveals the total number of intersections. Without looking at the graph, you might stop at one solution and miss others. Always: (1) Sketch or graph the function over a wide domain. (2) Count the number of x-intercepts or intersections. (3) Find each one. (4) Check there are no solutions outside your viewing window (consider end behaviour). *Quick check:* The equation x⁴ − 5x² + 4 = 0 has how many solutions? How do you know from the graph?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Engineers use numerical methods when designing structures — the Melbourne Metro Tunnel's boring path required solving equations of intersecting geological layers that had no neat algebraic solution. Weather modelling at the Bureau of Meteorology uses iterative numerical methods to refine predictions. Financial analysts use trial-and-improvement to find internal rates of return (IRR) on investments. AMSI and the Australian Mathematical Sciences Institute promote computational thinking alongside algebraic fluency.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Iterative refinement mirrors traditional practices of progressively improving — adjusting the angle of a fish trap based on water flow, refining the curve of a boomerang through successive trials. While these are physical rather than numerical iterations, the underlying principle of systematic adjustment towards an optimal result is shared. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Numerical methods are universal problem-solving strategies. From ancient Babylonian methods for square roots (iterative averaging) to modern computing, the guess-check-refine approach transcends cultural boundaries. Students from all backgrounds use this reasoning when estimating, budgeting, or adjusting recipes.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to know when your savings of A = 1000(1.05)ᵗ equal $1500. Graph y = 1000(1.05)ᵗ and y = 1500 in Desmos. Read off the intersection. Then use guess-check-refine to find t to 1 decimal place.
2. **Civic.** A council models traffic flow as f(x) = x³ − 12x² + 36x (vehicles per minute at hour x after 6am). Graph this and find all times when flow equals 20 vehicles/min. Are there multiple solutions? Interpret each.
3. **Vocational.** An electrician needs to solve the equation 2R² + 5R = 100 for resistance R. Use guess-check-refine to find R to 1 decimal place (start: R = 5 gives 75, R = 6 gives 102 — solution between 5 and 6).
4. **Ethical.** A student uses Desmos to solve x² = 2ˣ and finds x = 2 and x = 4. But there is a third solution near x = −0.8. How could the student have missed it? What practice would prevent this?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Babylonian scribes (c. 1800 BCE) solved √2 by iterative averaging: guess g, improve with (g + 2/g)/2. Start with g = 1. Apply three iterations. How close to √2 = 1.41421... do you get?

## Memorable takeaways
- Graphical: solutions are where graphs intersect (or cross the x-axis).
- Numerical: guess-check-refine narrows intervals systematically.
- Sign change between a and b means a solution exists between them.
- The graph tells you HOW MANY solutions exist — always check completeness.
- Digital tools (Desmos, spreadsheets) make these methods practical and accurate.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Stopping too early.** One solution found doesn't mean there aren't more. Always check the graph for the total count.
- **Viewing window too small.** Solutions outside your Desmos window will be missed. Zoom out first to see the big picture.
- **Confusing "close to zero" with "equals zero."** In guess-check-refine, look for sign changes, not just small values.
- **Assuming integer solutions.** Many equations have non-integer solutions. Don't round too early — refine to the specified accuracy.
- **Not considering end behaviour.** A polynomial of degree n can have up to n real roots. Check whether the graph's behaviour at the extremes suggests more solutions.

A note on certainty. Graphical and numerical methods give approximate solutions — their accuracy depends on how much you refine. However, two things are certain: (1) if a continuous function changes sign on an interval, a solution exists in that interval (Intermediate Value Theorem); (2) the graph of a polynomial of degree n crosses the x-axis at most n times. These theoretical guarantees tell you solutions exist and bound how many there can be, even when you can only find them approximately.
