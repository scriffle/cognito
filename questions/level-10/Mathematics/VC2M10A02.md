# VC2M10A02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10A02. Simplify algebraic products and quotients using exponent laws.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Exponent laws are shortcuts for multiplying and dividing repeated factors.
  - Mastering these laws lets you simplify complex algebraic expressions confidently.
  - The laws work for numerical and algebraic bases, and extend to zero and negative exponents.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **simplify algebraic products and quotients** by applying the **exponent (index) laws**.
- You are learning to handle expressions with multiple variables and combined operations.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- State and apply the exponent laws for multiplication, division, powers of powers, and zero/negative exponents.
- Simplify products like 3x²y × 4x³y².
- Simplify quotients like 12a⁵b³ ÷ 4a²b.
- Combine multiple laws in a single simplification.

## Core content

**1. Recap — what exponents mean.** x⁴ means x × x × x × x (four factors of x). The **base** is x; the **exponent** (or index) is 4. Exponent laws are rules that let you work with these efficiently without writing out every factor. *Quick check:* Write 2⁵ as a product of factors. What is its value?

**2. Multiplication law.** When multiplying same-base terms, **add** the exponents: **aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ**. Example: x³ × x⁵ = x⁸. With coefficients: 3x² × 4x³ = 12x⁵ (multiply the numbers, add the exponents). *Quick check:* Simplify 5a⁴ × 2a³.

**3. Division law.** When dividing same-base terms, **subtract** the exponents: **aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ**. Example: x⁷ ÷ x² = x⁵. With coefficients: 12x⁵ ÷ 4x² = 3x³. *Quick check:* Simplify 20b⁶ ÷ 5b².

**4. Power of a power.** When raising a power to another power, **multiply** the exponents: **(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ**. Example: (x³)⁴ = x¹². For a product in brackets: (2x³)² = 2² × (x³)² = 4x⁶. *Quick check:* Simplify (3y²)³.

**5. Zero and negative exponents.** **a⁰ = 1** (for a ≠ 0) — anything to the power of zero equals 1. **a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ** — a negative exponent means "one over." Example: x⁻² = 1/x². Example: 2x⁻³ = 2/x³. *Quick check:* Simplify 5x⁰ and write x⁻⁴ as a fraction.

**6. Worked example — simplifying a multi-variable expression.**
Simplify: (6a³b²) × (2a²b⁴) ÷ (3a⁴b³).

*Follow the thinking:*
- Multiply the numerator first: 6a³b² × 2a²b⁴ = 12a⁵b⁶ (multiply coefficients; add exponents for each variable).
- Divide by the denominator: 12a⁵b⁶ ÷ 3a⁴b³ = 4a¹b³ = 4ab³ (divide coefficients; subtract exponents for each variable).
- Final answer: 4ab³.
- Check: substitute a = 2, b = 1. Original: (6×8×1)(2×4×1) ÷ (3×16×1) = 96×8 ÷ 48 = 768 ÷ 48 = 16. Simplified: 4×2×1 = 8. Hmm — let me recheck. 6(8)(1) = 48; 2(4)(1) = 8; product = 384. Divisor: 3(16)(1) = 48. 384 ÷ 48 = 8. Simplified: 4(2)(1) = 8 ✓.

**7. Combining laws in practice.** Real problems often need multiple laws at once. Strategy: (1) expand any powers of products first; (2) multiply all terms in the numerator; (3) divide by the denominator; (4) simplify coefficients; (5) apply the subtraction law to each variable. Write your working clearly — one law per step reduces errors. *Quick check:* Simplify (2x²y)³ ÷ (4x³y²).

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Scientists at the Australian Synchrotron and CSIRO use exponent laws when working with very large and very small quantities — scientific notation relies entirely on these rules. Engineers at Engineers Australia simplify formulas with multiple variables before programming calculations. AMSI's research training uses index laws in analysis.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Exponential growth and decay models are used in environmental science to describe population dynamics and carbon-dating — tools applied to archaeological work on First Nations sites (such as the dating of Madjedbebe at 65,000+ years). Understanding the algebra behind these models supports critical engagement with scientific claims about Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Exponential notation was developed across cultures — Indian mathematicians used place-value systems implying powers of ten; Islamic scholars transmitted and extended Greek power concepts. Students working with scientific notation apply these multicultural contributions daily.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A computer's storage doubles each year. After 3 years it has 2³ = 8 times the original. Simplify 2³ × 2⁴ and explain what it means in terms of years of doubling.
2. **Civic.** A council measures pollution concentrations in scientific notation: 3.2 × 10⁴ particles per litre. A filter reduces this by a factor of 10². Write the new concentration using exponent laws.
3. **Vocational.** An engineering formula contains the expression (4x²y³)² ÷ (8x³y²). Simplify it completely.
4. **Ethical.** A student simplifies x⁵ ÷ x⁵ as x⁰ = 0 rather than x⁰ = 1. Explain why x⁰ = 1 and why this matters.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research how Indian mathematicians used powers of ten in their place-value number system. Then simplify: (10³)² × 10⁴ ÷ 10⁷.

## Memorable takeaways
- Multiply: add exponents (aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ).
- Divide: subtract exponents (aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ).
- Power of a power: multiply exponents ((aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ).
- Zero exponent: a⁰ = 1. Negative exponent: a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.
- Coefficients multiply/divide separately from variables.

## Stretch yourself
Simplify the following expression completely, showing every law you apply at each step:

(3a²b⁴)³ × (2a³b)²  ÷  (6a⁵b⁷)²

Then evaluate your simplified expression when a = 2 and b = 1. Verify by substituting a = 2 and b = 1 into the original (unsimplified) expression and confirming the same result. Finally, explain what would happen if a = 0 — which parts of the expression are defined and which are not? 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Adding exponents when you should multiply (power of a power).** (x³)² = x⁶, not x⁵. Multiply the exponents.
- **Forgetting to apply the power to the coefficient.** (3x²)³ = 27x⁶, not 3x⁶.
- **Subtracting exponents in the wrong order.** x³ ÷ x⁵ = x⁻², not x². The numerator exponent comes first.
- **Thinking a⁰ = 0.** a⁰ = 1 for any a ≠ 0. This follows from aⁿ ÷ aⁿ = 1.

A note on certainty. The exponent laws are proven consequences of the definition of exponentiation. They hold exactly for all real-number bases (with appropriate restrictions: a⁰ requires a ≠ 0; negative bases with fractional exponents require care). These are not rules to memorise by rote — they follow logically from what exponents mean.

2. *Pollution filter.* 3.2 × 10⁴ ÷ 10² = 3.2 × 10² = 320 particles per litre.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** (3a²b⁴)³ = 27a⁶b¹². (2a³b)² = 4a⁶b². Numerator: 27a⁶b¹² × 4a⁶b² = 108a¹²b¹⁴. (6a⁵b⁷)² = 36a¹⁰b¹⁴. Divide: 108a¹²b¹⁴ ÷ 36a¹⁰b¹⁴ = 3a²b⁰ = 3a².
