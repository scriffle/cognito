# VC2M10AA05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AA05. Describe, interpret and sketch parabolas, hyperbolas, circles and exponential functions from their equations.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Each function type — parabola, hyperbola, circle, exponential — has a characteristic equation form and distinctive graphical features.
  - Key features (vertices, asymptotes, centres, intercepts, growth rates) can be identified directly from the equation.
  - Transformations (translations, reflections, dilations) modify the standard form systematically.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **recognise function types from their equations** and to **sketch their graphs** by identifying key features.
- You are learning to describe transformations of standard forms and to match equations to graph shapes.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Sketch parabolas in turning-point form, identifying vertex, axis of symmetry and direction.
- Sketch hyperbolas of the form y = a/x, identifying asymptotes and quadrant location.
- Sketch circles from x² + y² = r² and (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r², identifying centre and radius.
- Sketch exponential functions, identifying y-intercept, asymptote and growth/decay.
- Describe transformations that convert a standard form into a given equation.

## Core content

**1. Parabolas.** The standard form y = a(x − h)² + k has vertex at (h, k) and axis of symmetry x = h. If a > 0, the parabola opens upward; if a < 0, it opens downward. The magnitude of a determines the width — larger |a| means narrower. For y = (x − 2)² − 4: vertex (2, −4), axis x = 2, opens upward. *Quick check:* Identify the vertex and direction of y = −(x + 1)² + 3.

**2. Hyperbolas.** The rectangular hyperbola y = a/x has asymptotes along the x-axis (y = 0) and y-axis (x = 0). For a > 0, the curve lies in quadrants 1 and 3; for a < 0, in quadrants 2 and 4. The curve never touches or crosses either axis. Translated forms y = a/(x − h) + k shift the asymptotes to x = h and y = k. *Quick check:* For y = 3/x, state the asymptotes and the quadrants containing the curve.

**3. Circles.** The equation x² + y² = r² represents a circle centred at the origin with radius r. The general form (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r² has centre (h, k) and radius r. A circle is not a function — it fails the vertical line test. For x² + y² = 25: centre (0, 0), radius 5. *Quick check:* State the centre and radius of (x − 3)² + (y + 2)² = 16.

**4. Exponential functions.** The function y = aˣ (a > 0, a ≠ 1) has y-intercept (0, 1), horizontal asymptote y = 0, and is always positive. If a > 1, the function increases (growth); if 0 < a < 1, it decreases (decay). Transformations: y = 2ˣ + 3 shifts the asymptote to y = 3; y = 2ˣ⁻¹ shifts the graph 1 unit right. *Quick check:* Identify the y-intercept, asymptote and growth/decay behaviour of y = (½)ˣ.

**5. Transformations of standard forms.** Starting from y = x², the equation y = −(x + 1)² + 3 involves: reflection in the x-axis (the negative), translation 1 unit left (+1 inside the bracket) and 3 units up (+3 outside). Systematically reading transformations from the equation is essential for efficient sketching. *Quick check:* Describe the transformations that convert y = x² into y = 2(x − 4)² − 1.

**6. Worked example — sketching a transformed parabola.**
Sketch y = −2(x − 1)² + 8.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Standard form: y = a(x − h)² + k with a = −2, h = 1, k = 8.
- Vertex: (1, 8). Axis of symmetry: x = 1.
- Opens downward (a < 0) and is narrower than y = x² (|a| = 2 > 1).
- y-intercept: set x = 0: y = −2(−1)² + 8 = −2 + 8 = 6. Point: (0, 6).
- x-intercepts: set y = 0: −2(x − 1)² + 8 = 0 → (x − 1)² = 4 → x − 1 = ±2 → x = 3 or x = −1.
- Plot vertex, intercepts, and draw a smooth downward parabola.

**7. Matching equations to graphs.** Given a set of graphs and equations, identify the type first (parabola, hyperbola, circle, exponential), then match key features. A curve with two branches in opposite quadrants and no intercepts is a hyperbola. A closed curve is a circle. A curve approaching a horizontal line is exponential. A U-shaped or inverted-U curve is a parabola. *Quick check:* A curve passes through (0, 1), is always positive, and approaches the x-axis as x → −∞. What type of function is it?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Architects and engineers in Melbourne use parabolic arcs in bridge design and hyperbolic curves in cooling tower profiles. The circular geometry of the MCG is analysed using circle equations. Exponential models describe population growth in Victorian urban planning.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Curved forms appear throughout First Nations art and design — parabolic arcs in boomerang profiles and circular forms in ceremonial grounds reflect deep geometric understanding predating European contact.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The conic sections (parabola, hyperbola, circle, ellipse) were studied systematically by Apollonius of Perga (3rd century BCE) in ancient Greece, while exponential growth models were developed by Euler and Bernoulli in 18th-century Europe.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A ball is thrown and follows the path y = −(x − 3)² + 9. Sketch the path, label the highest point, and find where the ball lands.
2. **Civic.** A circular park has equation (x − 5)² + (y − 5)² = 100 (units in metres). Find its centre and radius. Determine whether the point (15, 5) lies inside, on, or outside the park.
3. **Vocational.** An electrician models voltage decay across a capacitor as V = 12 × (0.8)ᵗ. Sketch the graph for t ≥ 0 and identify the initial voltage and long-term behaviour.
4. **Ethical.** A student sketches y = 1/x and draws the curve passing through the origin. Explain why this is incorrect.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research Apollonius of Perga and the conic sections. Then sketch y = 2/(x − 1) + 3, labelling asymptotes and key points.

## Memorable takeaways
- Read the equation to identify the function type: squared terms → parabola/circle; reciprocal → hyperbola; base raised to x → exponential.
- Vertex form y = a(x − h)² + k gives the vertex directly at (h, k).
- Asymptotes of y = a/(x − h) + k are x = h and y = k.
- Circle centre and radius come directly from (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r².
- Transformations follow consistent rules: inside the bracket affects x (opposite direction); outside affects y (same direction).

## Watch out for these traps
- **Confusing (x + 1)² with a shift right.** The expression (x + 1)² = (x − (−1))² represents a shift 1 unit LEFT. Inside the bracket, the sign is opposite.
- **Forgetting that a circle is not a function.** It fails the vertical line test — each x-value (within the domain) corresponds to two y-values.
- **Drawing a hyperbola through the origin.** The curve y = a/x is undefined at x = 0; there is no point on the curve where x = 0.
- **Mixing up growth and decay.** For y = aˣ: a > 1 gives growth; 0 < a < 1 gives decay.
