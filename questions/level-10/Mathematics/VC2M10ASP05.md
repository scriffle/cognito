# VC2M10ASP05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10ASP05. Apply Pythagoras' theorem and trigonometry to solve three-dimensional problems in right-angled triangles.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Space).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Three-dimensional problems are solved by identifying right-angled triangles within 3D shapes.
  - Pythagoras' theorem extends to 3D via the space diagonal: d² = l² + w² + h².
  - Trigonometric ratios (sin, cos, tan) apply to right-angled triangles extracted from 3D contexts.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **apply Pythagoras' theorem and trigonometry to three-dimensional problems** involving prisms, pyramids, cones and other solids.
- You are learning to identify and extract right-angled triangles from 3D shapes to solve for unknown lengths and angles.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Calculate space diagonals of rectangular prisms.
- Find angles of elevation and depression in 3D contexts.
- Identify right-angled triangles within pyramids, cones and other solids.
- Apply trigonometric ratios to find heights, slant heights and angles in 3D.
- Solve multi-step 3D problems requiring both Pythagoras and trigonometry.

## Core content

**1. The space diagonal.** In a rectangular prism with dimensions l × w × h, the face diagonal is √(l² + w²) and the space diagonal is √(l² + w² + h²). This follows from applying Pythagoras twice: first on the base, then vertically. For a 3 × 4 × 12 prism: face diagonal = √(9 + 16) = 5, space diagonal = √(9 + 16 + 144) = √169 = 13. *Quick check:* Find the space diagonal of a 6 × 8 × 10 rectangular prism.

**2. Angles in rectangular prisms.** The angle between the space diagonal and the base can be found using trigonometry. If the space diagonal makes angle θ with the base: tan(θ) = height / face diagonal. For the 3 × 4 × 12 prism: tan(θ) = 12/5, so θ = tan⁻¹(2.4) ≈ 67.4°. *Quick check:* A room is 5 m × 12 m × 3 m. Find the angle the space diagonal makes with the floor.

**3. Right-angled triangles in pyramids.** A right pyramid has its apex directly above the centre of the base. Key triangles: (a) the triangle formed by the apex, the centre and a base vertex gives the slant edge; (b) the triangle formed by the apex, the centre and the midpoint of a base edge gives the slant height. For a square pyramid with base edge 10 and height 12: half-diagonal = 5√2, slant edge = √(50 + 144) = √194 ≈ 13.93. *Quick check:* A square pyramid has base edge 8 cm and height 15 cm. Find the slant height (apex to midpoint of base edge).

**4. Cones and trigonometry.** A cone with radius r and height h has slant height l = √(r² + h²). The semi-vertical angle α satisfies tan(α) = r/h. For r = 5, h = 12: l = √(25 + 144) = 13 and α = tan⁻¹(5/12) ≈ 22.6°. *Quick check:* A cone has radius 7 cm and slant height 25 cm. Find the height and the semi-vertical angle.

**5. Angles of elevation and depression in 3D.** In 3D problems, the angle of elevation from a point to a higher point is measured in the vertical plane containing both points. The horizontal distance must first be calculated using Pythagoras in the horizontal plane. For a point A on the ground 30 m east and 40 m north of a building base, and the building is 50 m tall: horizontal distance = √(900 + 1600) = 50 m. Angle of elevation = tan⁻¹(50/50) = 45°. *Quick check:* A drone is directly above a point that is 20 m east and 15 m north of an observer. The drone is at 60 m altitude. Find the angle of elevation.

**6. Worked example — multi-step 3D problem.**
A communications tower stands on level ground. From point A, due east of the tower, the angle of elevation to the top is 30°. From point B, due north of the tower, the angle of elevation is 45°. The distance AB = 100 m. Find the height of the tower.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Let the tower height be h, distance from base to A be d₁, distance from base to B be d₂.
- tan(30°) = h/d₁ → d₁ = h/tan(30°) = h√3.
- tan(45°) = h/d₂ → d₂ = h.
- A is due east, B is due north, so d₁² + d₂² = AB² = 10 000.
- 3h² + h² = 10 000 → 4h² = 10 000 → h = 50 m.

**7. Compound 3D shapes.** Real-world structures often combine shapes — a silo (cylinder topped by a cone), a house (prism topped by a triangular prism). Solving problems requires decomposing the structure into component shapes, identifying the relevant right-angled triangles, and applying Pythagoras and trigonometry to each. *Quick check:* A tent has a rectangular base 4 m × 3 m and a ridge pole running along the longer side at height 2 m. Find the length of a slant edge from a corner of the base to the nearest end of the ridge.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Three-dimensional spatial calculations are fundamental to architecture, construction and mining — major Victorian industries. Engineers use 3D trigonometry for structural analysis, telecommunications tower placement and land surveying.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations peoples demonstrate sophisticated three-dimensional spatial reasoning in the construction of shelters, fish traps and stone arrangements. The geometry of these structures involves implicit understanding of angles, heights and spatial relationships.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Ancient Egyptian builders used 3D geometric reasoning for pyramid construction. The relationship between height, slant height and base dimensions in pyramids was understood practically long before formal mathematical proof.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A rectangular shipping container is 12 m × 2.4 m × 2.6 m. Can a pole 12.5 m long fit inside the container diagonally?
2. **Civic.** A flagpole stands on top of a building. From a point 50 m from the base of the building, the angle of elevation to the bottom of the flagpole is 60° and to the top is 65°. Find the height of the flagpole.
3. **Vocational.** A builder needs to install a support strut from a floor corner to the opposite ceiling corner in a room 5 m × 4 m × 3 m. Find the length and angle with the floor.
4. **Ethical.** A student calculates the slant height of a pyramid using the full base edge instead of half the base edge. Explain the error.
5. **Cross-cultural.** The Great Pyramid of Giza has a square base of side 230 m and original height 146 m. Find the slant height and the angle the slant face makes with the base.

## Memorable takeaways
- Space diagonal: d = √(l² + w² + h²).
- Identify right-angled triangles within 3D shapes — sketch them separately.
- Apply Pythagoras in the horizontal plane first, then use trigonometry vertically.
- Slant height of a pyramid uses half the base edge, not the full edge.
- Cone: l = √(r² + h²), and tan(α) = r/h for the semi-vertical angle.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Using the full base edge instead of half.** Slant height calculations in pyramids require the distance from the centre to the midpoint of a base edge, not a vertex.
- **Forgetting to find horizontal distance first.** In 3D elevation problems, calculate the horizontal distance in the ground plane before applying trigonometry.
- **Confusing slant height and slant edge.** Slant height goes to the midpoint of a base edge; slant edge goes to a base vertex.
- **Applying 2D Pythagoras directly in 3D.** The space diagonal requires three dimensions — apply Pythagoras twice or use the 3D formula.
