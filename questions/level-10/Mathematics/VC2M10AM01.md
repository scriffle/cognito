## Core content

**1. Volume of a cone.** V = ⅓πr²h, where r is the base radius and h is the perpendicular height. A cone holds exactly one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same base and height. For a cone with r = 3 cm and h = 12 cm: V = ⅓π(9)(12) = 36π ≈ 113.1 cm³. *Quick check:* Calculate the volume of a cone with radius 5 cm and height 9 cm.

**2. Surface area of a cone.** SA = πr² + πrl, where l is the slant height. The first term is the base area; the second is the curved (lateral) surface. The slant height relates to r and h by Pythagoras: l = √(r² + h²). For r = 3, h = 4: l = 5, SA = 9π + 15π = 24π ≈ 75.4 cm². *Quick check:* Find the surface area of a cone with radius 6 cm and slant height 10 cm.

**3. Volume and surface area of a sphere.** V = ⅔πr³. SA = 4πr². A sphere encloses the maximum volume for a given surface area — it is the most efficient shape. For r = 7 cm: V = ⅔π(343) = 4372π/3 ≈ 1436.8 cm³. SA = 4π(49) = 196π ≈ 615.8 cm². *Quick check:* Calculate the volume and surface area of a sphere with radius 10 cm.

**4. Volume and surface area of a pyramid.** For a pyramid with base area A and height h: V = ⅓Ah. A square-based pyramid with base side s has A = s². The surface area is the base area plus the sum of the triangular face areas. Each triangular face has area ½ × base × slant height. *Quick check:* Find the volume of a square pyramid with base side 8 cm and height 12 cm.

**5. Composite objects.** A composite object combines two or more standard shapes. For a hemisphere on top of a cylinder (both radius r, cylinder height h): total volume = πr²h + ⅔πr³. For the surface area, do not include the shared circular face: SA = 2πrh + πr² + 2πr². Careful identification of shared surfaces is essential. *Quick check:* A solid consists of a cone (r = 4, h = 6) on top of a cylinder (r = 4, h = 10). Find the total volume.

**6. Worked example — comparing volumes.**
Compare the volumes of a cone, sphere and cylinder, all with radius r and height 2r.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Cylinder: V = πr²(2r) = 2πr³.
- Cone: V = ⅓πr²(2r) = ⅔πr³.
- Sphere: V = ⅔πr³.
- Ratio: cone : sphere : cylinder = ⅔ : ⅔ : 2 = 1 : 2 : 3.
- This elegant relationship was discovered by Archimedes and is one of the most celebrated results in geometry.

**7. Working backwards — finding dimensions from volume.** Given the volume, solve for an unknown dimension. If a cone has volume 150π cm³ and height 18 cm: 150π = ⅓πr²(18) → r² = 25 → r = 5 cm. *Quick check:* A sphere has volume 288π cm³. Find the radius.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Engineers designing the Bolte Bridge pylons and grain silos across regional Victoria use cone, sphere and cylinder volume calculations routinely. The Melbourne Star observation wheel's capsules approximate spherical segments.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Three-dimensional spatial reasoning is central to First Nations construction practices — from dome-shaped shelters (approximating hemispheres) to the engineering of canoe hulls. These structures demonstrate sophisticated understanding of volume and surface area.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Archimedes (3rd century BCE) proved the 1:2:3 volume ratio of cone, sphere and cylinder — he considered this his greatest achievement. The calculation of π itself has a rich multicultural history spanning Babylonian, Egyptian, Chinese and Indian mathematics.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** An ice cream cone has radius 3 cm and height 12 cm. How many millilitres of ice cream can it hold? (1 cm³ = 1 mL.)
2. **Civic.** A water tank is a cylinder (r = 2 m, h = 4 m) with a hemispherical top. Find the total capacity in litres (1 m³ = 1000 L).
3. **Vocational.** A jeweller makes a solid gold pyramid pendant with square base side 1.5 cm and height 2 cm. Gold has density 19.3 g/cm³. Find the mass.
4. **Ethical.** Two containers hold the same volume: a sphere and a cylinder with equal radius. Which uses less material (surface area)? Why might a manufacturer choose the sphere?
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research Archimedes' discovery of the cone-sphere-cylinder ratio. Then verify it numerically for r = 5 cm.

## Memorable takeaways
- Cone volume = ⅓ × cylinder volume (same base and height).
- Sphere: V = ⅔πr³, SA = 4πr².
- Pyramid: V = ⅓ × base area × height.
- Cone SA requires slant height l = √(r² + h²).
- For composite objects, add volumes but be careful with shared surfaces when calculating SA.

## Stretch yourself
A grain silo consists of a cylinder of radius 3 m and height 10 m, topped with a cone of height 4 m (same radius). (a) Calculate the total volume. (b) Calculate the total exterior surface area (including the circular base but not the shared circle between cone and cylinder). (c) If the silo is to be painted at a cost of $12/m², find the total cost. (d) A spherical silo with the same total volume is proposed. Find its radius and compare the surface areas. Discuss which design is more material-efficient. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Using height instead of slant height for cone SA.** The curved surface formula uses slant height l, not perpendicular height h.
- **Forgetting the ⅓ factor.** Both cones and pyramids have V = ⅓ × base × height. Omitting ⅓ triples the answer.
- **Double-counting shared surfaces.** In composite objects, internal surfaces (where two shapes join) are not part of the external surface area.
- **Confusing radius and diameter.** If a sphere has diameter 10 cm, the radius is 5 cm — use r = 5 in all formulas.
