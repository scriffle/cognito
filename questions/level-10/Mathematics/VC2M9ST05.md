# VC2M9ST05 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9ST05. Plan and conduct statistical investigations involving the collection and analysis of different kinds of data; report findings and discuss the strength of evidence to support any conclusions.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Statistical investigations follow a cycle: pose a question, plan, collect, analyse, interpret, report.
  - The quality of conclusions depends on the quality of data collection (sample size, method, bias).
  - Strong evidence requires large enough samples, appropriate analysis, and honest reporting of limitations.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan and conduct statistical investigations** — from posing a question through to reporting findings and evaluating the strength of evidence.
- You are learning that good statistical practice involves careful planning, appropriate data collection, honest analysis, and transparent reporting of what the evidence does and does not support.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Pose a statistical question that can be investigated with data.
- Plan data collection: decide on population, sample, method and variables.
- Identify potential sources of bias and minimise them.
- Collect and organise data appropriately.
- Analyse using suitable displays and summary statistics.
- Draw conclusions and discuss their strength.
- Report methods, findings and limitations.

## Core content

**1. Posing a statistical question.** A good statistical question expects variability in the answer and can be investigated with data. "What is the average screen time of Year 10 students?" is statistical (expects variation). "What is 5 × 7?" is not (one answer, no variability). Good questions are specific, measurable, and connected to a population. *Quick check:* Which is a better statistical question: "Do students like maths?" or "What proportion of Year 10 students at our school rate maths enjoyment 4 or 5 out of 5?"

**2. Planning data collection.** Decide: **Population** (who/what you want to know about), **sample** (who you actually collect data from), **sampling method** (random, stratified, convenience), **variables** (what you measure). Random sampling reduces bias. Sample size matters — larger samples give more reliable results. *Quick check:* You want to know the average commute time of Melbourne workers. Is surveying your friends an appropriate sample? Why or why not?

**3. Sources of bias.** **Selection bias:** sample isn't representative (e.g. only surveying morning gym-goers about exercise habits). **Response bias:** question wording influences answers (leading questions). **Non-response bias:** people who don't respond may differ from those who do. **Measurement bias:** inaccurate instruments or inconsistent methods. Identifying and minimising bias strengthens your conclusions. *Quick check:* A survey about phone use is conducted online only. What bias might this introduce?

**4. Collecting and organising data.** Use consistent methods. Record all observations (don't discard "inconvenient" data). Organise in a table or spreadsheet: each row = one observation, each column = one variable. Include metadata: when, where, how collected. *Quick check:* Why is it important to record your data collection method?

**5. Analysing data.** Calculate appropriate summary statistics: mean, median, mode, range, IQR, standard deviation (for spread). Create appropriate displays (link to VC2M9ST04). Look for patterns, outliers, comparisons between groups. Use technology (spreadsheets, statistical software) for larger datasets. *Quick check:* You compare test scores between students who studied with music and those who didn't. What summary statistics would you compare?

**6. Worked example: investigating sleep and academic performance.**

Question: Do Year 10 students who sleep 8+ hours perform better on maths tests than those who sleep less?

*Follow the thinking:*
- Population: all Year 10 students. Sample: 60 students (30 per sleep group) selected randomly from the year level.
- Variables: hours of sleep (self-reported, categorical: <8h / 8+h) and most recent maths test score (numerical, 0–100).
- Collection: anonymous survey + teacher-provided scores (with ethics approval).
- Analysis: parallel box plots comparing distributions. Median comparison. Calculate mean and IQR for each group.
- Results: 8+ hours group → median 72, mean 71.5, IQR 15. <8 hours group → median 64, mean 63.8, IQR 20.
- Conclusion: there IS a difference (8 marks in median). But is it strong evidence? Considerations: sample size (60 is moderate), self-reported sleep (may be inaccurate), other factors (study time, stress) not controlled, correlation ≠ causation.
- Report: "Students who reported 8+ hours of sleep scored higher on average, but this observational study cannot establish causation. The difference is suggestive, not definitive."

**7. Strength of evidence and reporting.** Strong evidence needs: large sample, representative selection, controlled variables, consistent measurement, replicability. Weak evidence comes from: small samples, convenience sampling, uncontrolled variables, self-report, one-off observations. Always report: (1) what you found, (2) the strength of evidence, (3) limitations, (4) what further investigation would help. Honest reporting includes what the data does NOT tell you. *Quick check:* A student surveys 8 friends and concludes "teenagers prefer Instagram to TikTok." Discuss the strength of this evidence.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The ABS conducts the Census — Australia's largest statistical investigation — following rigorous planning, sampling and reporting principles. VicHealth conducts health surveys across Victoria using stratified random sampling. Melbourne schools conduct internal surveys (wellbeing, satisfaction) that follow the statistical investigation cycle.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Data sovereignty — the right of First Nations peoples to govern the collection, ownership and application of data about them — is a critical issue in statistical investigations involving Indigenous communities. The Mayi Kuwayu Study (National Study of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Wellbeing) follows community-led protocols for ethical data collection and reporting. Statistical investigations should follow Indigenous Data Sovereignty principles. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Cross-cultural research in Melbourne requires sensitivity to language barriers, cultural differences in survey response, and community-specific contexts. Translation and cultural adaptation of data collection instruments are important considerations in diverse populations.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Plan a statistical investigation to answer: "Do students who eat breakfast perform better in morning classes?" Specify: question, population, sample (minimum 30), sampling method, variables, data collection method, potential biases, and how you would analyse the results.
2. **Civic.** A council wants to know if residents support a new bike lane. Design an investigation: how would you sample 200 residents to avoid bias? What questions would you ask? What display would you use for results? What limitations would you report?
3. **Vocational.** A cafe owner wonders if playing background music increases customer spending. Plan an investigation: what variables would you measure? How would you control other factors? What sample size seems reasonable? How would you determine if any difference is meaningful?
4. **Ethical.** A researcher concludes from 15 student surveys that "social media causes anxiety in teenagers." Critically evaluate: identify at least three weaknesses in this conclusion. What would a stronger investigation look like?
5. **Cross-cultural.** You want to investigate whether students from different language backgrounds have different preferences for group vs individual work. Discuss: how would you collect this data sensitively? What biases might arise? How would you report findings without stereotyping?

## Memorable takeaways
- Statistical investigation cycle: question → plan → collect → analyse → interpret → report.
- Sample matters: larger, random, representative = stronger evidence.
- Bias can hide in selection, response, non-response and measurement.
- Correlation ≠ causation. Observational studies suggest, they don't prove.
- Report honestly: findings + strength of evidence + limitations.
- Strong evidence: large sample, controlled variables, representative selection.

## Stretch yourself
Conduct a full statistical investigation. Question: "Is there a relationship between the amount of physical activity Year 10 students do per week and their self-reported stress levels?" (a) Plan: define population, sampling method, sample size (aim for 40+), variables (how will you measure "physical activity" and "stress"?), data collection instrument (design a short survey). (b) Identify at least 3 potential sources of bias and explain how you would minimise each. (c) Describe your analysis plan (what displays, what statistics, how you'll assess relationship strength). (d) Discuss what strength of evidence would convince you there IS a relationship vs. random variation. (e) Write a 100-word conclusion template that honestly reports findings including limitations. Total: 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I concluded causation from an observational study." Only controlled experiments can establish causation. Surveys and observations show association/correlation — report it as such.
- "I surveyed my friends and generalised to all teenagers." Convenience samples are biased. Acknowledge the limitation or use random sampling.
- "I ignored outliers." Outliers should be investigated, not deleted. They might be errors (remove) or genuine extreme values (keep and discuss).
- "I only reported the result I wanted." Report ALL findings, including null results (no difference found). Cherry-picking is dishonest statistics.

A note on certainty. The statistical investigation process is well established. However, statistical conclusions always carry uncertainty — they are probabilistic, not absolute. Even well-designed studies can produce misleading results by chance. The honest reporting of confidence levels and limitations is what separates good statistics from bad.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Better question.* "What proportion of Year 10 students at our school rate maths enjoyment 4 or 5 out of 5?" — specific, measurable, expects variability, targets a defined population.
2. *Friends as a sample.* Not appropriate — convenience sample. Friends likely share similar demographics, suburb, socioeconomic status. Not representative of all Melbourne workers. Random sampling from the workforce would be better.
3. *Online-only survey bias.* Excludes people without internet access or digital literacy — likely older adults, lower socioeconomic groups, rural residents. These groups may have different phone usage patterns.
4. *Recording method.* So others can evaluate your work, replicate the investigation, and identify potential bias or errors in collection. Transparency enables critique and confidence.
5. *Music/no-music comparison.* Means and medians (centre), IQR or standard deviation (spread), sample sizes. Also: box plots for visual comparison.
6. *8 friends, Instagram conclusion.* Very weak evidence: tiny sample (8), convenience sample (friends — not representative), single data point, no analysis of uncertainty. Cannot generalise to "teenagers" from 8 friends.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Breakfast investigation.* Question: "Do students who eat breakfast score higher on a morning focus test?" Population: all students in our year level. Sample: 40 (stratified by class). Method: survey breakfast habits, then administer a 10-minute focus test in period 1. Variables: breakfast (yes/no) and test score (0–20). Bias: self-report on breakfast (response bias), students who eat breakfast may also sleep more (confounding). Analysis: parallel box plots, compare medians and means.
2. *Bike lane.* Random sample from electoral roll (stratified by distance from proposed lane). Ask: "Would you use a bike lane on [street]? (Yes/No/Unsure)" + rating of support (1–5). Display: bar chart (support levels). Limitations: non-response bias (residents may not open mail), people near the lane may feel differently from those far away.
3. *Cafe music.* Variables: daily average spend per customer (numerical), music condition (categorical: music/no music). Control: same day of week, similar weather, same menu. Method: alternate weeks with/without music over 8 weeks. Sample: all customers during those weeks. Meaningful difference: compare means and run a simulation to see if difference could arise by chance.
4. *Social media study weaknesses.* (1) Tiny sample (15) — too small for reliable generalisation. (2) "Causes" implies causation from what's likely a correlation/observational study. (3) Self-report for both social media use and anxiety — may be inaccurate. (4) No control group. (5) Convenience sample. A stronger study: 200+ participants, validated anxiety measure, longitudinal design, control for confounders (life events, pre-existing conditions).
5. *Language and group work.* Sensitive collection: anonymous survey, frame as "preference" not "ability," avoid singling out groups. Biases: social desirability (students may respond as they think they "should"), small numbers in some language groups. Reporting: show distributions and overlap, emphasise that variability within groups exceeds differences between groups. Avoid statements that imply a language background determines preference.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** (a) Population: Year 10 students at our school. Sample: 50 (random selection from roll). Variables: physical activity (hours/week, self-report), stress (Perceived Stress Scale 1–10, validated). Survey: anonymous online form. (b) Biases: (1) Self-report inaccuracy — minimise with specific prompts ("list activities and duration"). (2) Non-response — follow up with reminders, offer flexible completion time. (3) Social desirability — emphasise anonymity. (c) Scatter plot (activity vs stress), calculate correlation coefficient, compare stress levels between "active" (5+ hours) and "less active" groups with box plots. (d) A correlation coefficient |r| > 0.3 with 50 data points would suggest a moderate relationship unlikely due to chance. A difference in group medians greater than 1.5 scale points might be meaningful. (e) Template: "Our investigation found a [moderate/weak] [negative/positive] relationship between weekly physical activity and self-reported stress (r = ___). Students reporting [more/less] activity tended to report [lower/higher] stress. However, this observational study cannot establish causation — active students may also have other protective factors. Limitations include self-report data, a sample from one school, and uncontrolled variables."
