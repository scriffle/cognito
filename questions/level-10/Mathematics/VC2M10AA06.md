## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AA06. Use polynomial understanding to sketch curves and describe their features from their equation.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Polynomial curve sketching combines algebraic analysis (roots, turning points, end behaviour) with graphical representation.
  - The degree determines the maximum number of turning points (n − 1 for degree n) and the end behaviour pattern.
  - The leading coefficient's sign determines whether the graph ultimately rises or falls.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **sketch polynomial curves** by analysing their equations for roots, intercepts, turning points and end behaviour.
- You are learning to connect factorised forms to x-intercepts and to predict graph shapes from degree and leading coefficient.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Determine x-intercepts by factorising the polynomial and setting each factor to zero.
- Find the y-intercept by evaluating P(0).
- Describe end behaviour based on degree and leading coefficient.
- State the maximum number of turning points for a polynomial of given degree.
- Produce a labelled sketch showing intercepts, turning points (approximate) and end behaviour.

## Core content

**1. Degree and end behaviour.** The degree and leading coefficient determine how the curve behaves for very large and very small x. Odd degree, positive leading coefficient: bottom-left to top-right. Odd degree, negative: top-left to bottom-right. Even degree, positive: both ends up. Even degree, negative: both ends down. *Quick check:* Describe the end behaviour of P(x) = −2x⁴ + x² − 1.

**2. Finding x-intercepts from factors.** If P(x) = (x − a)(x − b)(x − c), the x-intercepts are at x = a, b, c. Repeated factors produce tangent points: if P(x) = (x − 2)²(x + 1), the curve touches (but does not cross) the x-axis at x = 2, and crosses at x = −1. *Quick check:* State the x-intercepts of P(x) = x(x − 3)(x + 2) and describe the curve's behaviour at each.

**3. Finding the y-intercept.** Set x = 0 and evaluate: for P(x) = 2x³ − 5x² + x + 3, P(0) = 3. The y-intercept is (0, 3). In factored form P(x) = (x − 1)(2x − 3)(x + 1), the y-intercept is (−1)(−3)(1) = 3 ✓. *Quick check:* Find the y-intercept of P(x) = (x − 4)(x + 1)(x − 2).

**4. Turning points.** A polynomial of degree n has at most n − 1 turning points (local maxima and minima). A cubic has at most 2 turning points; a quartic has at most 3. The exact location of turning points requires calculus or technology, but the number and general position can be inferred from the roots and end behaviour. *Quick check:* What is the maximum number of turning points for a degree-5 polynomial?

**5. Sketching a cubic.** For P(x) = −x³ + 4x: factorise as −x(x² − 4) = −x(x − 2)(x + 2). x-intercepts: 0, 2, −2. y-intercept: 0. End behaviour: odd degree, negative leading coefficient → top-left to bottom-right. The curve crosses at each intercept (no repeated factors). Plot intercepts, draw end behaviour arrows, and connect with a smooth curve having at most 2 turning points. *Quick check:* Sketch P(x) = x³ − 9x, labelling all intercepts.

**6. Worked example — sketching a quartic.**
P(x) = (x + 1)²(x − 2)(x − 4).

*Follow the thinking:*
- x-intercepts: x = −1 (repeated — tangent), x = 2, x = 4 (both single — crossing).
- y-intercept: P(0) = (1)²(−2)(−4) = 8. Point: (0, 8).
- Degree: 4 (even). Leading coefficient: positive (1 × 1 × 1 × 1 = 1). End behaviour: both ends up.
- Maximum turning points: 3.
- Sketch: curve rises from the left, touches x-axis at −1 (tangent), rises to y-intercept, descends through x = 2, descends further then turns, and rises through x = 4.

**7. Using technology to verify.** Graphing tools (Desmos, CAS calculators) confirm hand-drawn sketches. Technology can locate turning points precisely and reveal whether a sketch is qualitatively correct. However, algebraic analysis should precede technology — the sketch should be attempted by hand first, then verified. *Quick check:* A hand-drawn sketch of a cubic shows 3 turning points. Is this possible? Explain.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Polynomial models are used in Victorian engineering for road curvature design, in economics for cost-revenue analysis, and in environmental science for modelling population dynamics.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The understanding of curves and their behaviour connects to the spatial reasoning of First Nations peoples — boomerang flight paths, for instance, follow complex curved trajectories that were intuitively understood long before formal polynomial mathematics.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The systematic study of curves traces from Apollonius of Perga's conic sections through Descartes' analytic geometry (17th century) to modern computational graphing.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A roller coaster profile is modelled by h(x) = −x³ + 12x² − 36x (height in metres). Find the x-intercepts and sketch the profile for x ≥ 0.
2. **Civic.** A traffic engineer models vehicle speed as v(t) = t(t − 4)(t − 8) through a speed zone. Find when the speed is zero and describe the graph's shape.
3. **Vocational.** A packaging designer needs a box with volume V(x) = x(10 − 2x)(8 − 2x). Find the values of x where V = 0 and sketch the volume function.
4. **Ethical.** A student's sketch of y = x⁴ − 4x² shows the curve crossing the x-axis at only two points. Is this correct? Factorise and determine all intercepts.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research Descartes' contribution to analytic geometry. Then sketch P(x) = (x − 1)(x + 2)(x − 3)², labelling all features.

## Memorable takeaways
- End behaviour is determined by degree (odd/even) and sign of the leading coefficient.
- A polynomial of degree n has at most n x-intercepts and at most n − 1 turning points.
- Repeated factors create tangent points; single factors create crossing points.
- The y-intercept is always P(0) — substitute x = 0.
- Sketch by hand first, verify with technology.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting repeated factors change the crossing behaviour.** A single factor crosses; a double factor touches (tangent); a triple factor has an inflection crossing.
- **Drawing too many turning points.** A degree-n polynomial has at most n − 1 turning points — never more.
- **Ignoring the leading coefficient's sign.** The sign determines whether the curve ultimately rises or falls.
- **Not checking the y-intercept.** Always evaluate P(0) to anchor the sketch.
