# VC2M10AA01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AA01. Polynomials and the factor and remainder theorems.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - A polynomial is a sum of terms of the form aₙxⁿ, where n is a non-negative integer; the degree is the highest power of x.
  - The remainder theorem states that the remainder when P(x) is divided by (x − a) equals P(a).
  - The factor theorem states that (x − a) is a factor of P(x) if and only if P(a) = 0.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **identify, classify and manipulate polynomials**, and to apply the **factor and remainder theorems** to factorise and solve polynomial equations.
- You are learning to connect algebraic division with evaluation, and to use these theorems to analyse polynomial behaviour efficiently.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- State the degree, leading coefficient and constant term of a polynomial.
- Apply the remainder theorem to find the remainder when dividing P(x) by (x − a).
- Apply the factor theorem to determine whether (x − a) is a factor of P(x).
- Factorise cubic polynomials by combining the factor theorem with quadratic factorisation.
- Solve polynomial equations using these techniques.

## Core content

**1. What is a polynomial?** A polynomial in x is an expression of the form aₙxⁿ + aₙ₋₁xⁿ⁻¹ + … + a₁x + a₀, where the coefficients aₖ are real numbers and n is a non-negative integer. The **degree** is the highest power of x with a non-zero coefficient. The **leading coefficient** is the coefficient of the highest-power term, and the **constant term** is a₀. For example, P(x) = 2x³ − 5x² + x − 7 has degree 3, leading coefficient 2 and constant term −7. *Quick check:* What are the degree, leading coefficient and constant term of P(x) = −3x⁴ + x² − 8?

**2. The remainder theorem.** When a polynomial P(x) is divided by (x − a), the remainder equals P(a). This avoids long division entirely — simply substitute x = a into the polynomial and evaluate. For P(x) = x³ − 2x + 1 divided by (x − 3): P(3) = 27 − 6 + 1 = 22. The remainder is 22. *Quick check:* Find the remainder when P(x) = 2x³ + x − 5 is divided by (x − 1).

**3. The factor theorem.** The factor theorem is a special case of the remainder theorem: if P(a) = 0, then (x − a) is a factor of P(x). Conversely, if (x − a) is a factor, then P(a) = 0. The value a is called a **root** (or zero) of the polynomial. For P(x) = x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6: P(1) = 1 − 6 + 11 − 6 = 0, so (x − 1) is a factor. *Quick check:* Is (x − 2) a factor of P(x) = x³ − 3x² + 4? Justify.

**4. Factorising a cubic polynomial.** Once one linear factor (x − a) is found using the factor theorem, the remaining quadratic factor is obtained by polynomial division or inspection. The quadratic may then factorise further. For P(x) = x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6: since (x − 1) is a factor, divide to get x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3). Therefore P(x) = (x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3). *Quick check:* Given that (x + 1) is a factor of P(x) = x³ + 2x² − x − 2, factorise P(x) completely.

**5. Solving polynomial equations.** Setting P(x) = 0 and factorising gives the roots. From part 4, x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = 0 has solutions x = 1, 2, 3. The number of real roots of a polynomial of degree n is at most n. A cubic always has at least one real root. *Quick check:* Solve x³ − 4x² + x + 6 = 0 given that x = −1 is a root.

**6. Worked example — factorising with the remainder and factor theorems combined.**
P(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Test x = 1: P(1) = 2 − 3 − 11 + 6 = −6 ≠ 0.
- Test x = −1: P(−1) = −2 − 3 + 11 + 6 = 12 ≠ 0.
- Test x = 3: P(3) = 54 − 27 − 33 + 6 = 0. So (x − 3) is a factor.
- Divide: 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 = (x − 3)(2x² + 3x − 2).
- Factorise the quadratic: 2x² + 3x − 2 = (2x − 1)(x + 2).
- Fully factorised: P(x) = (x − 3)(2x − 1)(x + 2).
- Roots: x = 3, x = ½, x = −2.

**7. Sketching polynomial graphs.** The degree and leading coefficient determine the **end behaviour** of the graph. An odd-degree polynomial with positive leading coefficient rises from bottom-left to top-right; a negative leading coefficient reverses this. An even-degree polynomial opens upward (positive leading coefficient) or downward (negative). The x-intercepts correspond to the real roots. *Quick check:* Describe the end behaviour of P(x) = −x³ + 4x.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Engineers at CSIRO and in Victorian manufacturing use polynomial models for signal processing, control systems and structural analysis. The Australian Synchrotron employs polynomial fitting to model particle trajectories.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Pattern recognition in polynomial relationships parallels the systematic reasoning embedded in First Nations knowledge systems. 2. **Civic.** A council models pedestrian traffic flow through an intersection as P(t) = t³ − 9t² + 20t (in hundreds), where t is hours after 6 am. Find when the flow is zero and interpret the result.

## Memorable takeaways
- The remainder when dividing P(x) by (x − a) is simply P(a) — no long division needed.
- If P(a) = 0, then (x − a) is a factor — this is the factor theorem.
- To factorise a cubic, find one root by testing, divide out the linear factor, then factorise the resulting quadratic.
- A polynomial of degree n has at most n real roots.
- The degree and sign of the leading coefficient determine the end behaviour of the graph.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Testing only positive integers.** Roots can be negative or fractional. For a polynomial with integer coefficients, rational roots have the form p/q where p divides the constant term and q divides the leading coefficient.
- **Forgetting to test x = −a as well as x = a.** Both positive and negative values must be tried.
- **Errors in evaluation.** When computing P(a), handle signs carefully — especially with negative values of a and even powers.
- **Assuming a cubic always has three distinct real roots.** A cubic has at least one real root, but repeated roots or complex roots are possible.
