# VC2M9ST02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9ST02. Analyse how different sampling methods, and different samples using the same method, can affect the results of surveys and how choice of representation can be used to support a particular point of view.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Different sampling methods (random, stratified, convenience, voluntary) produce different results from the same population.
  - Even using the same method, different samples give different results — this is sampling variability.
  - Choice of graph type, scale, and statistic can be used to support (or mislead) a particular point of view.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse how sampling methods affect survey results** and how data representation choices can support or distort a message.
- You are learning to recognise when statistics are being used to persuade rather than inform.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Compare results from different sampling methods applied to the same question.
- Explain why different samples using the same method produce different estimates.
- Identify how choice of graph, scale, or statistic supports a particular point of view.
- Critically evaluate media representations of data for bias.
- Design fair sampling strategies that minimise bias.

## Core content

**1. Sampling methods.** Random: every member has equal chance of selection. Stratified: population divided into groups, random sample from each proportionally. Convenience: whoever is easiest to reach. Voluntary: people choose to participate. Systematic: every kth person. Each method produces different samples and potentially different results. *Quick check:* A school wants to survey student satisfaction. Describe how each of these methods — random, stratified (by year level), and convenience (lunchtime in the cafeteria) — would select students differently.

**2. How method affects results.** Convenience sampling over-represents accessible groups. Voluntary sampling attracts people with strong opinions (often negative). Random sampling reduces systematic bias but still has variability. Stratified sampling ensures all subgroups are represented proportionally. Example: surveying "Do you support the new uniform policy?" — voluntary respondents (angry students) might show 80% opposed, while random sample shows 55% opposed. *Quick check:* A radio station asks listeners to call in about a proposed freeway. Why might this produce different results from a random phone survey of residents?

**3. Sampling variability.** Even with the same method (e.g. random), different samples give different results. Take 10 random samples of 30 students' heights — each sample mean will differ slightly. This is natural variability, not error. Larger samples have less variability (estimates cluster closer to the true value). *Quick check:* Two random samples of 50 Victorians are asked their weekly exercise hours. Sample A gets mean = 4.1 hours, Sample B gets mean = 3.7 hours. Is one "wrong"? Why are they different?

**4. Representing data to support a point of view.** The SAME data can look dramatically different depending on: (a) graph type chosen, (b) axis scale and starting point, (c) which statistic is reported (mean vs median), (d) what data is included/excluded, (e) how categories are grouped. A truncated y-axis makes small differences look huge. A pie chart can make 30% look dominant. Reporting mean income instead of median inflates the "typical" value. *Quick check:* A company's profit grew from $10M to $10.5M (5% growth). Show how a graph could make this look like massive growth versus steady performance.

**5. Misleading representations in media.** Common techniques: truncated axes (start y-axis at 90 instead of 0), 3D graphs that distort proportions, cherry-picking time periods, using area/volume to represent linear quantities (doubling a pictograph's height quadruples its area), unlabelled axes, and selective reporting. Always ask: what's the full picture? What's been left out? *Quick check:* A political party shows a bar chart of unemployment dropping from 5.2% to 5.0% — but the y-axis starts at 4.8%. Describe what this looks like versus what a y-axis starting at 0% would show.

**6. Worked example — same data, two stories.**
Data: Average teacher salaries by state (fictional): VIC $92K, NSW $95K, QLD $88K, WA $91K, SA $86K.

*Story 1 — "Victorian teachers underpaid":* Bar chart with y-axis from $85K to $96K. Victoria's bar looks much shorter than NSW. Headline focuses on the $3K gap.

*Story 2 — "Australian teachers well-paid":* Bar chart with y-axis from $0 to $100K. All bars look similar — differences are tiny. Headline focuses on how all states are near $90K.

Same data. Different representations. Different conclusions. Neither is "wrong" — but each supports a point of view. Critical literacy means recognising the choices made and asking what story isn't being told.

**7. Designing fair sampling and representation.** To minimise bias: use random or stratified sampling. Report sample size, method, and response rate. Choose representations that don't distort (start axes at 0 unless there's good reason, use appropriate graph types, report multiple statistics). Acknowledge limitations. *Quick check:* You're surveying your community about a new park. Design a sampling strategy that is fair and a representation plan that is honest. What would an unfair version look like?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Electoral Commission (VEC) commissions polls before elections — polling methodology is hotly debated (phone vs online, random vs panel). Melbourne media (Herald Sun vs The Age) reporting the same economic data can produce very different impressions through representation choices. The Victorian Curriculum and Assessment Authority (VCAA) uses stratified sampling to assess state-wide student achievement. The ABS Victorian regional data uses careful sampling to ensure rural/regional voices are proportionally represented.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Sampling methods historically excluded or misrepresented First Nations communities: remote communities were undersampled, surveys used culturally inappropriate methods, and results were represented through deficit framing. Data sovereignty means communities choosing their own sampling methods (yarning circles, community-controlled surveys) and representation formats. When non-Indigenous researchers design samples that exclude remote communities, the resulting data — however well-represented graphically — tells an incomplete and potentially harmful story. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Sampling methods that rely on English-language phone surveys systematically under-represent communities with other home languages. Convenience sampling at shopping centres may over-represent some cultural groups and miss others entirely. Students from diverse backgrounds can analyse whether survey designs are likely to include their communities and what representation choices might distort perceptions of their communities.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your school surveys students about canteen satisfaction. Compare the likely results from: (a) random sample of 50 students, (b) voluntary online poll, (c) surveying students in the canteen at lunchtime. Which method would give the most reliable result? Why?
2. **Civic.** A council wants to know if residents support a new bike lane. They survey 200 people at a cycling event. Identify the sampling bias. Design a better method. Then show how the results could be graphed to support either the pro-bike or anti-bike position.
3. **Vocational.** A real estate agent reports "House prices up 15% this quarter!" but used a convenience sample of only their own premium sales. How might a random sample of ALL sales in the area tell a different story? Design a fair sampling approach.
4. **Ethical.** A fast-food company commissions a survey showing "8 out of 10 customers are satisfied." Analyse: who was sampled (only existing customers), what question was asked, and how a pie chart showing 80% green makes this look overwhelmingly positive. Redesign for honesty.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Australia's Census aims for universal coverage, but voluntary surveys in other countries achieve 30–60% response. Compare: how does the "voluntary" aspect create a non-random sample? Whose voices are amplified and whose are lost? How might results be represented to hide this gap?

## Memorable takeaways
- Different sampling methods produce systematically different results.
- Same method, different sample → different estimates (sampling variability).
- Larger samples reduce variability but don't fix method bias.
- Graphs can tell any story: truncated axes, selective data, chosen statistics.
- Always ask: who was sampled, how, and what representation choices were made?

## Watch out for these traps
- **Assuming "random" means "haphazard."** True random sampling requires a systematic process (random number generator, complete population list) — not just "asking whoever."
- **Thinking larger samples always fix everything.** A million-person voluntary poll is still biased by self-selection.
- **Not seeing the y-axis.** Always check where axes start and what scale is used before interpreting a graph visually.
- **Confusing sampling variability with sampling bias.** Variability (natural fluctuation) decreases with larger samples. Bias (systematic error from method) does NOT decrease with larger samples.
- **Accepting the first representation uncritically.** The same data supports multiple stories — always ask "what story ISN'T being told?"

A note on certainty. The mathematics of sampling distributions is exact — we can precisely calculate how much variability to expect for a given sample size and method. What remains uncertain is the human element: whether a sampling method truly reaches who it claims to reach, whether respondents answer honestly, and whether representation choices are made in good faith. Statistical literacy combines mathematical precision with critical judgement about these human factors.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *School satisfaction.* Random: use student ID list, randomly select 50 — captures range of opinions. Stratified: select proportionally from each year level — ensures no year is over/under-represented. Convenience (cafeteria): over-represents students who eat there (maybe happier with canteen), misses those who leave campus or bring food.
2. *Radio call-in.* Voluntary response — only people who feel strongly (and listen to that station) call in. Likely over-represents opponents (anger motivates action). Random phone survey captures a broader range of opinion intensities, including the "don't care" middle.
3. *Two sample means (4.1 vs 3.7).* Neither is "wrong." Both are estimates. Natural sampling variability means different random samples give different results. The population mean is probably somewhere near both. With only 50 per sample, this much variation is expected.
4. *5% growth graph.* Truncated y-axis ($9.5M to $11M): the bar nearly doubles in visual height — looks like massive growth. Full y-axis ($0 to $12M): the increase is barely visible — looks like steady performance. Same data, completely different visual impression.
5. *Unemployment graph.* Y-axis 4.8%–5.3%: the drop from 5.2% to 5.0% occupies most of the chart — looks like unemployment halved. Y-axis 0%–10%: the drop is barely visible — looks like essentially no change. The mathematical truth (0.2 percentage points) is the same, but the visual "story" is entirely different.
6. *Teacher salaries.* Analysed in worked example — same data supports "underpaid" or "well-paid" depending on axis choice.
7. *Park survey design.* Fair: stratified random sample (proportional by age, suburb, park-user vs non-user). Represent with full y-axis, report mean AND median, show response rate. Unfair: survey only at the skate park (young users), show truncated axis making support look unanimous.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Canteen survey.* Random (most reliable — captures full range including non-canteen users). Voluntary poll attracts complainers. Cafeteria survey only reaches current users (likely more satisfied). Random sample might show 60% satisfied; voluntary might show 40%; cafeteria might show 75%. The differences are entirely due to method.
2. *Bike lane at cycling event.* Massive bias — cyclists are overwhelmingly pro-bike-lane. Better: stratified random sample of residents (proportional by distance from proposed lane, age, transport mode). Pro-bike graph: pie chart with large green "support" slice. Anti-bike graph: bar chart showing that 60% of non-cyclists oppose, with y-axis truncated to emphasise the opposition majority in that subgroup.
3. *Real estate.* Agent's convenience sample of premium sales inflates the average. Random sample of ALL sales (including apartments, lower-value suburbs) would likely show smaller growth — perhaps 3–5% instead of 15%. Fair approach: random sample of all completed sales, report median (less affected by outliers) and include property type breakdown.
4. *Fast-food satisfaction.* Sampling bias: only existing customers (people who already chose to eat there). People who stopped going due to dissatisfaction aren't captured. The question probably offered "satisfied/very satisfied/neutral/dissatisfied" — combining "satisfied" and "very satisfied" gives 80%. A pie chart with a large green slice looks overwhelming. Honest redesign: survey general population (not just customers), break down by frequency of visit, show the full response distribution rather than binary satisfied/unsatisfied.
5. *Census vs voluntary.* Voluntary surveys lose: hard-to-reach people, people who don't trust institutions, people who are busy/stressed, people with language barriers, people without internet access. The resulting "sample" over-represents: engaged, educated, connected, English-speaking populations. Results might be represented without mentioning response rate — a graph showing "65% support policy X" looks like the whole population, but actually only represents the ~40% who responded. The missing 60% might feel very differently.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Real survey example: "Herald Sun reports 72% of Victorians support mandatory sentencing (Newspoll, n=1100, random phone survey)." Sampling method: random phone — reasonable but may under-represent young people (less likely to answer unknown numbers) and non-English speakers. Under convenience (surveying outside courthouse): likely higher support (people affected by crime). Under voluntary (online poll on news site): attracts strong opinions, likely polarised — either very high or very low support depending on readership. Graph 1 (supporting headline): pie chart with 72% in bold red "SUPPORT" — looks overwhelming. Graph 2 (opposing): bar chart showing support by age group — 45% among 18–24, 85% among 65+. Reveals the headline masks major demographic variation. Fairer representation: report with confidence interval, break down by demographics, show full question wording. Fairer sampling: ensure proportional age and language representation through stratification.
