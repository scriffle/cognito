# VC2M10AA10 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AA10. Experiment with functions and relations using digital tools, making conjectures and verifying them.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Digital tools allow dynamic exploration of functions — parameters can be adjusted in real time to observe effects.
  - The experimental-conjecture-verification cycle mirrors authentic mathematical research practice.
  - Technology builds mathematical intuition that complements formal algebraic understanding.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **use digital tools** (graphing calculators, Desmos, CAS) to **explore functions and relations**, form conjectures, and verify them algebraically.
- You are learning to distinguish between observing a pattern (conjecture) and proving it (verification).

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Use graphing technology to explore families of functions by varying parameters.
- Observe patterns and formulate mathematical conjectures.
- Verify conjectures using algebraic reasoning.
- Investigate inverse functions graphically using reflection in y = x.
- Use technology to explore relationships between a function and its derivative.

## Core content

**1. Exploring parameter effects.** Using Desmos or a CAS calculator, graph y = a(x − h)² + k and use sliders for a, h and k. Observe: a controls width and direction, h shifts horizontally, k shifts vertically. Moving from observation to statement: "Increasing |a| makes the parabola narrower" is a conjecture; proving it from the equation confirms it. *Quick check:* What effect does increasing h have on y = (x − h)²?

**2. Families of functions.** A family of functions shares a common form but differs in one parameter. The family y = x² + k consists of all vertical translations of y = x². Technology reveals that every member has the same shape — only the vertical position changes. Exploring families develops understanding of how structure determines behaviour. *Quick check:* Describe the family y = mx + 1 as m varies. What feature do all members share?

**3. Forming conjectures.** A conjecture is an educated guess based on observed evidence. After graphing y = x², y = x⁴, y = x⁶: conjecture — "All y = xⁿ with even n pass through (0, 0), (1, 1) and (−1, 1), and open upward." This can be verified: xⁿ evaluated at 0, 1, −1 gives 0, 1, 1 for any even n, and positive leading coefficient means upward end behaviour. *Quick check:* After graphing y = x³, y = x⁵, y = x⁷, formulate a conjecture about their common features.

**4. Investigating intersections.** Technology allows visual exploration of how many times two functions intersect. For y = x² + k, the number of intersections with y = 4x − 3 depends on k. Set x² + k = 4x − 3 → x² − 4x + (k + 3) = 0. Δ = 16 − 4(k + 3) = 4 − 4k. Two intersections when k < 1, one when k = 1, none when k > 1. Technology confirms this. *Quick check:* For what values of k does y = x² + k have no x-intercepts?

**5. Inverse functions graphically.** Graph y = 2ˣ and its reflection in y = x using technology. The reflected curve is y = log₂(x). Observe: domain and range swap, intercepts swap, asymptotes swap from horizontal to vertical. Technology makes the reflection visible and confirms the inverse relationship. *Quick check:* Graph y = x³ and reflect it in y = x. What is the equation of the inverse?

**6. Worked example — conjecture and verification.**
Conjecture: The sum of the roots of ax² + bx + c = 0 equals −b/a.

*Using technology:*
- Graph y = x² − 5x + 6. Roots: x = 2, 3. Sum = 5 = −(−5)/1 ✓.
- Graph y = 2x² + x − 6. Roots: x = 1.5, −2. Sum = −0.5 = −1/2 ✓.
- Graph y = 3x² − 12x + 9. Roots: x = 1, 3. Sum = 4 = −(−12)/3 ✓.

*Algebraic verification:* If the roots are r and s, then ax² + bx + c = a(x − r)(x − s) = a(x² − (r + s)x + rs). Comparing coefficients: b = −a(r + s), so r + s = −b/a ✓.

**7. Exploring derivatives graphically.** Technology can display a function and its derivative simultaneously. For y = x², the derivative dy/dx = 2x — a straight line. Observe: where the parabola has a turning point, the derivative crosses zero. Where the parabola is increasing, the derivative is positive. This visual exploration prepares for formal calculus. *Quick check:* Using technology, describe the relationship between the turning points of y = x³ − 3x and the zeros of its derivative.

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** VCE Mathematics courses require CAS calculator proficiency. Victorian universities and STEM workplaces use computational tools as standard practice for mathematical exploration and modelling.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The conjecture-verification cycle — forming a hypothesis from observation, then testing it — parallels the empirical knowledge-building of First Nations peoples, who developed sophisticated ecological models through observation and verification over millennia.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The experimental approach to mathematics has roots in diverse traditions — from the geometric constructions of Euclid to the numerical explorations of Ramanujan, who often conjectured results from patterns before formal proofs were found.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Use technology to graph y = ax² for a = 0.5, 1, 2, 4. Describe how the width of the parabola changes and formulate a conjecture. Verify algebraically.
2. **Civic.** A council wants to know how changing the speed limit v affects stopping distance d = v²/20. Use technology to explore this relationship and state a conjecture about doubling the speed.
3. **Vocational.** An engineer graphs R(T) = 100(1 + 0.004T) and R(T) = 120 on the same axes to find the temperature at which resistance reaches 120 Ω. Use the graph to estimate, then verify algebraically.
4. **Ethical.** A student explores y = x² and y = 2x using technology and concludes "exponentials always grow faster than polynomials." Is this a valid generalisation from the evidence? Discuss.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research Ramanujan's experimental approach to mathematics. Then use technology to investigate: for what values of n does the equation x² + nx + n = 0 have two positive roots?

## Memorable takeaways
- Technology allows rapid exploration — adjust a parameter and instantly see the effect.
- A conjecture is a pattern observed; verification requires algebraic proof.
- Families of functions share structure — varying one parameter reveals how that parameter controls behaviour.
- Inverse functions are reflections in y = x; technology makes this visible.
- The experimental-conjecture-verification cycle is authentic mathematical practice.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Confusing observation with proof.** Seeing a pattern in three examples does not prove it holds for all cases.
- **Over-generalising from limited data.** A conjecture from a few graphs may fail for parameters not yet tested.
- **Relying solely on technology.** Technology shows what happens but not why. Algebraic verification explains the underlying reason.
- **Misreading graphs.** Zoom level, window settings and resolution can create misleading visual impressions.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Effect of increasing h in y = (x − h)².* The parabola shifts to the right.
2. *Family y = mx + 1.* All members pass through (0, 1) — they share the y-intercept. The gradient m determines the steepness and direction.
3. *Conjecture for odd-power functions.* All pass through (0, 0), (1, 1), (−1, −1). They have odd symmetry (rotational symmetry about the origin) and rise from bottom-left to top-right.
4. *y = x² + k has no x-intercepts.* When k > 0: x² + k = 0 has no real solution since x² ≥ 0 and k > 0.
5. *Inverse of y = x³.* y = ∛x (the cube root function).
6. *Sum of roots conjecture.* Verified: r + s = −b/a, by expanding a(x − r)(x − s) and comparing coefficients.
7. *Turning points and derivative zeros.* y = x³ − 3x has turning points where dy/dx = 3x² − 3 = 0, i.e. at x = ±1. The derivative is zero at the turning points of the original function.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Width conjecture.* As a increases, the parabola becomes narrower. Algebraically, for y = ax², the x-value needed to reach a given y is x = √(y/a), which decreases as a increases.
2. *Stopping distance.* Doubling v quadruples d, since d is proportional to v². Verify: d(2v) = (2v)²/20 = 4v²/20 = 4d(v).
3. *Resistance.* Graphically, R = 120 at approximately T = 50. Algebraically: 120 = 100(1 + 0.004T) → T = 50°C.
4. *Generalisation.* Not valid from two functions alone. While 2ˣ eventually exceeds x² for large x, the student has not tested other polynomial-exponential pairs. The generalisation is actually true (for any base > 1 and any polynomial) but requires formal proof.
5. *Two positive roots.* By Vieta's formulas: sum = −n > 0 requires n < 0; product = n > 0 requires n > 0. These are contradictory, so no value of n gives two positive roots.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** (a) Period of sin(bx) = 360°/b (or 2π/b in radians). Conjecture: period = 360/b. (b) Verified from the definition: sin(bx) completes one cycle when bx = 360°, so x = 360/b. (c) Amplitude of a sin(x) = |a|. (d) y = 3sin(2x): amplitude 3, period 180°. Technology confirms. (e) A conjecture says "I noticed this pattern"; a proof says "this must always be true because..."
