# VC2M10AST01 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10AST01. Calculate and interpret mean and standard deviation of data and use these to compare data sets.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10 Advanced, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The mean measures the centre of a data set; the standard deviation measures spread around the mean.
  - Standard deviation quantifies how far data values typically lie from the mean.
  - Comparing data sets requires both a measure of centre (mean) and a measure of spread (standard deviation).

## Core content

**1. The mean.** The mean (x̄) is the sum of all values divided by the number of values: x̄ = Σxᵢ/n. For the data set {4, 7, 8, 10, 11}: x̄ = 40/5 = 8. The mean represents the "balance point" of the data. *Quick check:* Find the mean of {12, 15, 18, 20, 25}.

**2. Deviation from the mean.** Each value's deviation is xᵢ − x̄. Deviations sum to zero. Squaring deviations removes negative signs and emphasises larger deviations. For {4, 7, 8, 10, 11} with x̄ = 8: deviations are −4, −1, 0, 2, 3. Squared deviations: 16, 1, 0, 4, 9. *Quick check:* For data {3, 5, 7, 9, 11} with mean 7, find the deviations and squared deviations.

**3. Standard deviation — calculation.** Population standard deviation: σ = √(Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/n). For {4, 7, 8, 10, 11}: σ = √(30/5) = √6 ≈ 2.45. Sample standard deviation (s) uses n − 1 in the denominator: s = √(Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/(n − 1)). At this level, either formula may be used — context determines which is appropriate. *Quick check:* Calculate the population standard deviation of {3, 5, 7, 9, 11}.

**4. Interpreting standard deviation.** A small standard deviation means data values are clustered close to the mean (consistent). A large standard deviation means data values are spread out (variable). If two students have test score means of 75 but standard deviations of 3 and 12, the first student is more consistent. *Quick check:* Data set A has mean 50, SD 2. Data set B has mean 50, SD 8. Which is more consistent?

**5. Comparing data sets.** To compare data sets meaningfully, consider both centre and spread. A comparison should state: (a) which set has the higher/lower mean, (b) which set has the greater/lesser spread, and (c) what this implies in context. Higher mean with lower SD suggests consistently better performance. *Quick check:* Class X has mean 72, SD 5. Class Y has mean 68, SD 12. Compare the classes.

**6. Worked example — comparing two data sets.**
Two machines fill bottles. Machine A: {500, 502, 498, 501, 499} mL. Machine B: {495, 510, 488, 505, 502} mL.

*Calculations:*
- Machine A: x̄ = 2500/5 = 500. Deviations: 0, 2, −2, 1, −1. Squared: 0, 4, 4, 1, 1. σ = √(10/5) = √2 ≈ 1.41.
- Machine B: x̄ = 2500/5 = 500. Deviations: −5, 10, −12, 5, 2. Squared: 25, 100, 144, 25, 4. σ = √(298/5) = √59.6 ≈ 7.72.
- Both machines have the same mean (500 mL), but Machine A (SD ≈ 1.41) is far more consistent than Machine B (SD ≈ 7.72).

**7. Effect of outliers.** Outliers disproportionately affect the mean and inflate the standard deviation. Adding an extreme value pulls the mean toward it and increases the sum of squared deviations. For {4, 7, 8, 10, 11}, adding 50: new mean = 15, new SD ≈ 15.6 — a dramatic increase. When outliers are present, the median and interquartile range may be more appropriate summary statistics. *Quick check:* The data set {20, 22, 21, 23, 20, 100} has an outlier. Would the mean or median better represent the centre?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Quality control in Victorian manufacturing relies on mean and standard deviation to monitor consistency. Weather data analysis, sports statistics and educational assessment all use these measures.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations peoples have long observed and communicated patterns of variability in natural phenomena — seasonal rainfall, fish abundance, plant fruiting cycles. The mathematical formalisation of variability through standard deviation connects to this observational tradition.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** The concept of standard deviation was developed by Karl Pearson (1894) building on earlier work by Gauss and others. Statistical methods are now fundamental to research worldwide.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Two students' test scores over 5 tests: Student A: {85, 88, 82, 90, 85}. Student B: {70, 95, 65, 100, 80}. Calculate mean and SD for each. Who is more consistent?
2. **Civic.** Monthly rainfall (mm) for two towns over 6 months: Town X: {40, 45, 42, 38, 44, 41}. Town Y: {10, 80, 20, 70, 30, 40}. Compare the towns' rainfall patterns.
3. **Vocational.** A factory tests battery life (hours): {48, 52, 50, 49, 51, 50, 48, 52}. Calculate the mean and SD. If the acceptable range is mean ± 2 SD, what is this range?
4. **Ethical.** A company reports only the mean salary to job applicants. Discuss why including the standard deviation would give a more honest picture.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research Karl Pearson's development of standard deviation. Then calculate the mean and SD of {14, 16, 18, 12, 20, 10, 22, 8}.

## Memorable takeaways
- Mean = Σxᵢ/n — the balance point of data.
- Standard deviation measures typical distance from the mean.
- Small SD → consistent data; large SD → variable data.
- Compare data sets using both mean (centre) and SD (spread).
- Outliers inflate both mean and SD — consider median and IQR as alternatives.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting to square deviations.** Deviations sum to zero — squaring is essential before averaging.
- **Dividing by n vs n − 1.** Population SD uses n; sample SD uses n − 1. Check which is required.
- **Comparing SDs without context.** A SD of 5 is large for test scores out of 10 but small for scores out of 1000.
- **Ignoring outliers.** One extreme value can distort mean and SD significantly.
