# VC2M10ST05 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Plan and conduct statistical investigations of situations that involve bivariate data, including where the independent variable is time; evaluate and report findings.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Statistical investigations follow a cycle: plan → collect → display → analyse → evaluate → report. Bivariate data measures two variables on the same subjects. Time-series data uses time as the independent variable — revealing trends, seasonal patterns and irregular variation.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan and conduct bivariate statistical investigations**, including time-series analysis.
- You should formulate questions, choose displays, analyse trends/associations, and report with limitations.

## Core content

**Investigation cycle.** Question → Plan (variables, sample, method) → Collect → Display → Analyse → Evaluate → Report.

**Time-series features.** Time on x-axis, variable on y-axis, connected points. Look for: trend (long-term direction), seasonal variation (repeating cycle), irregular variation (unexpected fluctuations).

**Bivariate analysis.** Scatterplot for two numerical variables. Describe: direction, strength, linearity. Line of good fit for predictions within data range.

**Worked example.** "Has Melbourne's temperature changed over 50 years?" BOM data → time-series graph → upward trend (~1°C increase) → year-to-year variation but clear overall warming → consistent with climate science. Limitation: urban heat island effect.

**Evaluating.** Limitations, confounders, representativeness. "My data suggests..." not "proves." Acknowledge weaknesses — this strengthens the work.

**Reporting.** Question → method → findings (with displays) → conclusion → limitations.

**Victorian context.** BOM publishes Victorian climate data. Melbourne Water tracks dam levels over time. ABS provides population and employment time series. Ethical research WITH Aboriginal communities (not about them) respects data sovereignty. Seasonal ecological knowledge is time-series understanding maintained for millennia.

## Memorable takeaways
- Cycle: question → plan → collect → display → analyse → evaluate → report.
- Time series: trend + seasonal + irregular variation.
- Conclusions linked to evidence, stated cautiously.
- Every investigation has limitations — acknowledge them.
- Plan BEFORE collecting (or data may be unusable).

## Watch out for these traps
- Collecting data without a clear plan or question.
- Confusing trend (long-term direction) with seasonal pattern (repeating cycle).
- Overstating conclusions: "suggests" not "proves."
- Not connecting time-series points (unlike scatterplots).
- Ignoring limitations instead of acknowledging them.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Screen time bivariate question.* "Is there a relationship between daily screen time and hours of sleep for Year 10 students?"
- *Altitude vs temperature display.* Scatterplot (not time-series — altitude isn't time).
- *Monthly rainfall pattern.* Seasonal: higher winter, lower summer. Regular annual cycle.
- *School enrolments: up trend, December dip.* Trend = growth. Seasonal = annual cycle around year endings.
- *Ice cream vs drowning confounder.* Hot weather drives both — not a causal relationship between them.
