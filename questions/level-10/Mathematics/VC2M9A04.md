# VC2M9A04 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9A04. Find the gradient of a line segment, the midpoint of the line interval and the distance between 2 distinct points on the Cartesian plane.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Gradient measures steepness: rise over run between any two points.
  - The midpoint is the average of the coordinates.
  - Distance between two points uses Pythagoras' theorem on the coordinate plane.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **find the gradient, midpoint and distance** for a line segment defined by two points on the Cartesian plane.
- You are learning that these three tools let you describe and analyse any line segment precisely using coordinates alone.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Calculate the gradient of a line segment from two points.
- Interpret positive, negative, zero and undefined gradients.
- Find the midpoint of a line interval.
- Calculate the distance between two points.
- Apply these to practical coordinate-geometry problems.

## Core content

**1. Gradient (slope).** The gradient of a line through (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) is: **m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)**. This is "rise over run." A positive gradient slopes up-right; a negative gradient slopes down-right; zero gradient is horizontal; undefined gradient (division by zero) is vertical. *Quick check:* Find the gradient of the line through (1, 3) and (4, 9).

**2. Interpreting gradient.** The gradient tells you how steep a line is and which direction it goes. m = 2 means "for every 1 unit right, the line goes up 2 units." m = −½ means "for every 2 units right, the line goes down 1 unit." Parallel lines have equal gradients. Perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to −1 (negative reciprocals). *Quick check:* A line has gradient 3. What is the gradient of a line perpendicular to it?

**3. Midpoint.** The midpoint of the segment from (x₁, y₁) to (x₂, y₂) is: **M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)**. It is simply the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates. *Quick check:* Find the midpoint of the segment from (2, 5) to (8, 11).

**4. Distance.** The distance between (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) is: **d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]**. This is Pythagoras' theorem applied to the horizontal and vertical differences. *Quick check:* Find the distance between (1, 2) and (4, 6).

**5. Connecting the three tools.** Gradient, midpoint and distance work together. To check if a quadrilateral is a parallelogram: show opposite sides have equal gradients (parallel) and equal lengths (distance formula). To find the centre of a circle given two endpoints of a diameter: find the midpoint. *Quick check:* A triangle has vertices A(0, 0), B(6, 0), C(3, 4). Find the length of side AC.

**6. Worked example: analysing a property boundary.**

A surveyor maps two corner posts of a property at coordinates (10, 25) and (46, 49) on a grid where each unit = 1 metre.

*Follow the thinking:*
- Gradient: m = (49 − 25)/(46 − 10) = 24/36 = ²⁄₃. The boundary rises 2 m for every 3 m across.
- Midpoint: M = ((10 + 46)/2, (25 + 49)/2) = (28, 37). A fence post should go here.
- Distance: d = √(36² + 24²) = √(1 296 + 576) = √1 872 ≈ 43.3 m. The boundary is about 43.3 m long.
- Check: 36² + 24² = 1 296 + 576 = 1 872. √1 872 ≈ 43.3 ✓.

**7. Special cases and connections.** Horizontal segment: gradient = 0, distance = |x₂ − x₁|. Vertical segment: gradient is undefined, distance = |y₂ − y₁|. The distance formula always works, but for horizontal or vertical segments, you can just subtract coordinates. The midpoint formula always gives a point on the segment. *Quick check:* What is the gradient and distance for the segment from (3, 7) to (3, 15)?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Land surveyors across Victoria use coordinate geometry to establish property boundaries — gradient determines slope of terrain, midpoint locates intermediate survey markers, and distance calculates boundary lengths. The Melbourne rectangular street grid makes coordinate geometry intuitive: finding the midpoint of a block or the distance between intersections uses these formulas directly. Urban planners use gradient calculations for drainage (water flows down the steepest gradient).
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations land management requires precise knowledge of terrain — gradient determines water flow patterns essential for managing Country. The positioning of fish traps, weirs and channels at Budj Bim (Gunditjmara Country) reflects applied understanding of slope and distance across the landscape. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Navigation apps used across Melbourne's communities calculate distances and routes using coordinate geometry principles. Architecture and construction in diverse building traditions — from flat-roofed Mediterranean designs to steeply pitched Northern European styles — involve gradient calculations for roof pitch and drainage.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your house is at coordinates (2, 5) on a street map and your school is at (8, 13). Find the straight-line distance, the midpoint (where you would be halfway through your walk), and the gradient of the direct path.
2. **Civic.** A council plans a cycling path from point A(0, 0) to point B(600, 200) on a planning grid (units in metres). Calculate the gradient of the path, its length, and the midpoint where a rest stop should be placed.
3. **Vocational.** A roof drops from a height of 6.2 m at one wall to 4.6 m at the opposite wall, 8 m across. Place these as coordinates and find the gradient (pitch) of the roof. Building code requires a minimum gradient of 1/6 for drainage. Does this roof comply?
4. **Ethical.** A surveillance camera at coordinates (0, 3) has a range of 25 m. A property boundary point is at (20, 15). Is the boundary point within the camera's range? Discuss the ethical implications of camera placement relative to neighbouring properties.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Three community centres form a triangle at A(1, 2), B(9, 2) and C(5, 8). The council wants to place a shared facility equidistant from all three. Find the midpoints of each side, and use them to estimate the centre. Verify by calculating the distance from your centre to each vertex.

## Memorable takeaways
- Gradient: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) — rise over run.
- Midpoint: average the x's, average the y's.
- Distance: d = √[(Δx)² + (Δy)²] — Pythagoras on coordinates.
- Positive gradient → up-right. Negative → down-right. Zero → horizontal. Undefined → vertical.
- Parallel lines: equal gradients. Perpendicular: gradients multiply to −1.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I subtracted coordinates in the wrong order." Be consistent: if you use (y₂ − y₁) in the numerator, use (x₂ − x₁) in the denominator. Swapping one but not the other gives the wrong sign.
- "I forgot to square root." The distance formula gives d². Don't forget the final √ step.
- "I found the midpoint but it doesn't look right." The midpoint should be between the two points in both x and y. If it's outside the segment, you've made an arithmetic error.
- "I said the gradient is undefined when it's zero." Zero gradient = horizontal line (perfectly valid). Undefined = vertical line (division by zero because x₁ = x₂).
