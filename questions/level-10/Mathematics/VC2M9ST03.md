# VC2M9ST03 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9ST03. Represent the distribution of multiple data sets for numerical variables using comparative representations such as back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots and histograms; describe data, using terms including 'skewed', 'symmetric' and 'bi-modal'; compare data distributions using mean, median and range to describe and interpret numerical data sets with consideration of centre, spread and shape, and the effect of outliers on these measures.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Statistics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Multiple data sets can be compared visually using back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots, comparative histograms, and other paired displays.
  - Distribution shape matters: symmetric, positively skewed, negatively skewed, bi-modal — each tells a different story.
  - Mean, median, and range are affected differently by outliers; the choice of measure should match the distribution shape.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **represent and compare multiple data distributions** using appropriate displays and summary statistics.
- You are learning to describe distribution shape and understand how outliers affect centre and spread.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Construct back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots and comparative histograms.
- Describe distributions as symmetric, positively skewed, negatively skewed, or bi-modal.

## Core content

**1. Comparative representations.** When comparing two data sets, display them side by side. Back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot: shared stem in the middle, leaves for each group spread left and right. Comparative histograms: same scale, stacked or side-by-side. Both reveal shape, centre, spread, and outliers for direct comparison. *Quick check:* When would you choose a back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot over comparative histograms? What about the reverse?

**2. Back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots.** Stem in the centre, one data set's leaves go left (read outward), the other goes right. Example — test scores for Class A and Class B:

Class A | Stem | Class B
9 7 5 | 5 | 2 6 8
8 6 4 3 | 6 | 1 3 5 7 9
7 5 2 | 7 | 0 4 6 8
3 1 | 8 | 2 5
| 9 | 1

Read: Class A has more scores in 60s–70s; Class B is more spread and slightly higher. *Quick check:* From the plot above, find the median for each class. Which class has a wider range?

**3. Distribution shape — key vocabulary.** Symmetric: mirror image about the centre (mean ≈ median). Positively skewed (right-skewed): tail stretches right, mean > median (e.g. income, house prices). Negatively skewed (left-skewed): tail stretches left, mean < median (e.g. exam scores with a ceiling effect). Bi-modal: two distinct peaks — suggests two subgroups in the data (e.g. heights when mixing two age groups). *Quick check:* Describe the likely shape of: (a) adult heights in Australia, (b) house prices in Melbourne, (c) marathon finish times.

**4. Centre: mean vs median.** Mean: sum divided by count — affected by every value, pulled toward outliers. Median: middle value — resistant to outliers. For symmetric data: mean ≈ median, either works. For skewed data: median better represents the "typical" value. For bi-modal data: both mean and median may fall between the peaks (neither represents a typical value well). *Quick check:* House prices: $450K, $480K, $510K, $520K, $2.1M. Calculate mean and median. Which better represents the "typical" price?

**5. Spread: range and the effect of outliers.** Range = maximum − minimum. Simple but highly sensitive to outliers — one extreme value inflates the range dramatically. Compare: {20, 22, 25, 27, 30} has range 10. Add one outlier: {20, 22, 25, 27, 30, 95} has range 75. The interquartile range (IQR) is more resistant. When comparing distributions, note whether differences in range are due to genuine spread or just one extreme value. *Quick check:* Two data sets have the same median but different ranges. What could explain this? Does it mean one is "better"?

**6. Worked example — comparing two distributions.**
Data: Daily maximum temperatures (°C) for Melbourne (July) and Brisbane (July):

Brisbane: 19, 19, 20, 20, 20, 21, 21, 21, 21, 22, 22, 22, 23, 23, 24

Back-to-back stem-and-leaf:

4 3 3 3 2 2 2 1 1 | 1 |

Actually, let me use proper stems:

Comparison:

- Brisbane: roughly symmetric, centred around 21°C. Mean = 21.2, median = 21, range = 5.

- Both are approximately symmetric, so mean and median are close for each.

**7. Choosing measures for comparison.** Match your summary to the shape: symmetric → mean and range work well. Skewed → median and IQR preferred. Bi-modal → report both modes and consider whether the data should be separated into subgroups. When comparing two groups, use the SAME measures for both — don't use mean for one and median for the other. *Quick check:* Income data for two suburbs: Suburb A is positively skewed, Suburb B is symmetric. Which measures should you use to compare them fairly?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Bureau of Meteorology (BOM) compares temperature and rainfall distributions across Victorian regions (alpine vs coastal vs inland). Melbourne's famous "four seasons in one day" means daily temperature ranges are wide — comparing Melbourne's spread to other capitals shows this statistically. VicRoads compares traffic flow distributions between peak and off-peak to plan infrastructure. The Victorian Population Health Survey compares health measure distributions across demographic groups.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Comparative data about First Nations communities requires careful consideration: comparing against non-Indigenous populations without context reinforces deficit narratives. Distributions may be bi-modal when data combines urban and remote communities — treating this as one population masks important within-group variation. Data sovereignty means First Nations communities choosing what comparisons are meaningful, what shape their data stories take, and which measures of centre best represent community experience. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Bi-modal distributions often appear in migration data — income, employment, education — reflecting different waves of migration and settlement patterns. Students from diverse backgrounds can explore how combining data from distinct subgroups creates distributions whose shape tells a story about social patterns and how separating data by meaningful groups reveals structure hidden in the combined distribution.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Collect the ages of 15 people you know from two different contexts (family vs school, or two sports teams). Create a back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot. Describe the shape of each distribution and compare using mean, median, and range. Are there outliers? How do they affect your measures?
2. **Civic.** A council compares response times for two fire stations. Station A: roughly symmetric around 8 minutes. Station B: positively skewed with most responses under 7 minutes but occasional 20+ minute responses to remote areas. Which measures best compare them? What does the skewness in B tell you about geography?
3. **Vocational.** A factory produces bolts on two machines. Machine A: symmetric distribution around 50.0 mm. Machine B: symmetric around 50.0 mm but with wider spread. Both have the same mean — so are they performing equally? What does the difference in spread tell you about quality?
4. **Ethical.** A news report says "average income in Suburb X is $120,000" (using the mean). The data is positively skewed with a few millionaires. What would the median be relative to this? Who benefits from reporting the mean? Design a more honest representation of this suburb's income distribution.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Compare the age distributions of two countries: Australia (roughly symmetric, median ~38) and a country with high birth rate (positively skewed toward young, median ~19). What does each shape tell you about the population? How would services need to differ?

## Memorable takeaways
- Back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots compare two distributions on the same scale.
- Shape vocabulary: symmetric, positively skewed, negatively skewed, bi-modal.
- Symmetric: mean ≈ median. Skewed: mean pulled toward the tail; median better.
- Outliers inflate mean and range; they barely affect median.
- Always match your summary measure to the distribution shape.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Reading back-to-back plots backwards.** Left-side leaves read outward from the stem (right to left). Remember: leaves always increase away from the stem.
- **Using mean for skewed data.** The mean is dragged toward the tail — it doesn't represent the typical value. Use median for skewed distributions.
- **Saying "bi-modal" when there's a slight bump.** True bi-modality means two clearly distinct peaks, usually suggesting two subgroups mixed together.
- **Ignoring shape when comparing.** Two distributions with the same mean can look completely different. Always describe shape alongside numerical summaries.
- **Forgetting outliers affect range dramatically.** One extreme value can double the range while barely changing the median. Report this — don't just state the range without comment.

A note on certainty. Descriptive statistics are exact — the mean IS the mean, the median IS the median for any given data set. These are not estimates or approximations. What requires judgement is the interpretation: whether a distribution is "skewed enough" to prefer median over mean, whether a value is "extreme enough" to call an outlier, and whether two distributions are "different enough" to matter. The numbers are certain; their meaning requires human interpretation.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**
1. *Stem-and-leaf vs histogram.* Stem-and-leaf: better for small data sets (< 50), preserves individual values, easier to find exact median. Histograms: better for larger data sets, allows grouped intervals, easier to see overall shape. Use stem-and-leaf for precision; histograms for large-scale pattern.
2. *Medians from the example.* Class A: 15 values, median = 8th value. Leaves (sorted): 55, 57, 59, 63, 64, 66, 68, 72, 75, 77, 81, 83... Class A median ≈ 68. Class B median ≈ 71. Class B has wider range (91 − 52 = 39 vs Class A's 83 − 55 = 28).
3. *Distribution shapes.* (a) Adult heights: approximately symmetric (bell-shaped). (b) House prices: positively skewed (tail stretches toward expensive homes). (c) Marathon times: positively skewed (most cluster near 4–5 hours, long tail toward 6–7 hours).
4. *House prices.* Mean = (450 + 480 + 510 + 520 + 2100)/5 = 4060/5 = $812K. Median = $510K (middle value). Median ($510K) far better represents the "typical" price — the $2.1M outlier inflates the mean by $300K above what most houses cost.
5. *Same median, different range.* One set might be more spread out (more variability in the data), or one might have an outlier inflating its range. Doesn't mean one is "better" — depends on context. A factory wants small range (consistency); a diverse community might naturally have large range.
6. *Temperature comparison.* Analysed in worked example — Melbourne lower centre, slightly more variable; Brisbane higher centre, less variable; both symmetric.
7. *Income comparison.* Since Suburb A is skewed, use median and IQR for BOTH suburbs (even though mean works for B). Using the same measures ensures a fair comparison. Reporting mean for both would inflate Suburb A's "typical" income unfairly.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Ages comparison.* Answers vary. Family data likely has wider range and possibly bi-modal (adults + children). School data likely more clustered. Key: describe shape, note outliers, justify measure choice.
2. *Fire stations.* Station A symmetric → mean (8 min) and range work. Station B skewed → median better (perhaps 6.5 min with IQR 5–8). The skewness tells us B handles most calls quickly but has a few distant areas causing the tail. Median comparison: B is actually faster for most calls despite the occasional slow response.
3. *Factory bolts.* Same mean (50.0 mm) means both are centred correctly. But wider spread in Machine B means more bolts fall outside tolerance — more will be rejected as defective. Spread matters for quality control, not just centre. Machine A produces more consistent (reliable) output.
4. *Income reporting.* With positive skew and millionaires pulling the mean up, median might be $75K–$85K. Reporting $120K mean makes the suburb sound wealthier than most residents actually are — benefits real estate agents, misleads buyers. Honest representation: report median, show histogram revealing the skew, note the gap between mean and median.
5. *Age distributions.* Symmetric ~38 (Australia): roughly equal numbers in each age group, needs services balanced across ages (schools, workforce, aged care). Skewed ~19 (high birth rate): mostly young people, needs massive education infrastructure, fewer aged services. Shape directly determines resource allocation. Bi-modal shapes (e.g. post-war baby boom) create rolling challenges as the bulge ages through the system.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** Comparing commute times (minutes) for Route A (freeway) vs Route B (back streets), n=25 each. Route A: stem-and-leaf shows roughly symmetric around 28 minutes, tight cluster, range 22–35. Mean 28.1, median 28, range 13. Route B: positively skewed, most values 18–24 but tail to 45 minutes (when a train crossing delays traffic). Mean 24.8, median 22, range 27. The outliers (long delays) inflate Route B's mean by ~3 minutes above the median and triple its range relative to what most trips experience. For comparison: median better for Route B (skewed), either works for Route A (symmetric). Conclusion: Route B is usually faster (median 22 vs 28) but less predictable (larger range, skewed by occasional long delays). If you need reliability, Route A is better despite being slower on average. If you can tolerate occasional long trips, Route B saves time most days. The shape of the distribution — not just the averages — determines which route suits your needs.
