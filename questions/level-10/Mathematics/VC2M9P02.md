# VC2M9P02 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M9P02. Calculate relative frequencies from given or collected data to estimate probabilities of events involving 'and', inclusive 'or' and exclusive 'or'.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Probability).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Relative frequency = frequency of outcome / total trials. It estimates probability from data.
  - "And" means both events occur (intersection). "Or" means at least one occurs (union).
  - Inclusive "or" allows both; exclusive "or" means one or the other but not both.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **calculate relative frequencies** from data and use them to estimate probabilities of compound events involving "and," inclusive "or" and exclusive "or."
- You are learning that real-world data provides experimental probability estimates that approach theoretical probabilities as the number of trials increases.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Calculate relative frequency from a frequency table or dataset.
- Use relative frequency as an estimate of probability.
- Calculate P(A and B) from data.
- Calculate P(A or B) using the addition rule for inclusive "or."
- Distinguish between inclusive "or" (A or B or both) and exclusive "or" (A or B but not both).
- Apply these concepts to real-world data.

## Core content

**1. Relative frequency.** Relative frequency = (number of times an outcome occurs) / (total number of trials). In 200 coin tosses, heads appears 108 times: relative frequency of heads = 108/200 = 0.54. This is an estimate of the probability. With more trials, it approaches the true probability. *Quick check:* A die is rolled 150 times. The number 3 appears 28 times. What is the relative frequency of rolling a 3?

**2. Using relative frequency to estimate probability.** When theoretical probability is unknown or impractical to calculate, we use data. A factory tests 500 light bulbs and 12 are defective: P(defective) ≈ 12/500 = 0.024 = 2.4%. The more data we have, the more reliable the estimate. *Quick check:* In a survey of 400 students, 180 walk to school. Estimate P(a randomly selected student walks).

**3. "And" — intersection.** P(A and B) is the probability that both events occur. From data: count outcomes where both A and B happen, divide by total. In a class of 30: 12 play sport AND music. P(sport and music) = 12/30 = 0.4. For independent events: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). For dependent events, use conditional probability or data directly. *Quick check:* In 200 customers, 45 bought coffee AND cake. Estimate P(coffee and cake).

**4. Inclusive "or" — union.** P(A or B) inclusive means A occurs, or B occurs, or both occur. **Addition rule:** P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). We subtract P(A and B) to avoid counting the overlap twice. In the class of 30: 18 play sport, 15 play music, 12 do both. P(sport or music) = 18/30 + 15/30 − 12/30 = 21/30 = 0.7. *Quick check:* In 100 cars, 40 are white, 25 are SUVs, 10 are white SUVs. What is P(white or SUV)?

**5. Exclusive "or."** P(A or B exclusive) means A occurs or B occurs but NOT both. P(A or B exclusive) = P(A or B inclusive) − P(A and B) = P(A) + P(B) − 2×P(A and B). From the car example: P(white exclusive or SUV) = 40/100 + 25/100 − 2×(10/100) = 45/100 = 0.45. This counts only cars that are white-but-not-SUV or SUV-but-not-white. *Quick check:* Using the class data above (18 sport, 15 music, 12 both, total 30), find P(sport exclusive or music).

**6. Worked example: school canteen data.**

The canteen surveys 250 students. Results: 120 bought a hot meal, 90 bought a drink, 50 bought both.

*Follow the thinking:*
- P(hot meal) = 120/250 = 0.48.
- P(drink) = 90/250 = 0.36.
- P(hot meal and drink) = 50/250 = 0.20.
- P(hot meal or drink, inclusive) = 0.48 + 0.36 − 0.20 = 0.64.
- P(hot meal or drink, exclusive) = 0.48 + 0.36 − 2(0.20) = 0.44.
- Interpretation: 64% of students bought at least one of the two items. 44% bought exactly one (not both).
- Check: students buying neither = 1 − 0.64 = 0.36 → 90 students. Students buying exactly one = 0.44 × 250 = 110. Buying both = 50. Total: 90 + 110 + 50 = 250 ✓.

**7. Venn diagrams and two-way tables.** Venn diagrams visually show intersections and unions — the overlap region represents "and," the entire combined area represents "or." Two-way tables organise data into rows and columns for systematic counting. Both help avoid counting errors when calculating compound probabilities. *Quick check:* Construct a two-way table for the canteen data above. How many students bought a hot meal only (without a drink)?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Insurance companies in Melbourne use relative frequency data to estimate risk probabilities — the probability of a car accident AND flood damage in a given suburb determines premiums. VicHealth surveys use "and/or" probability to report health behaviours (e.g. proportion of Victorians who smoke OR are physically inactive OR both).
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations ecological knowledge draws on long-term observation to estimate event probabilities — the likelihood of certain weather patterns AND particular plant flowerings occurring together guides seasonal planning. This is relative frequency reasoning built over generations of data collection. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Market research in Melbourne's diverse retail sectors uses relative frequency to estimate customer behaviour — P(visiting a store AND making a purchase) drives staffing and stock decisions. Public health data across communities uses "or" probabilities to identify combined risk factors.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You track your morning routine for 40 school days. You eat breakfast on 32 days and catch the early bus on 25 days. On 20 days you do both. Estimate P(breakfast and early bus), P(breakfast or early bus inclusive), and P(breakfast or early bus exclusive).
2. **Civic.** A council surveys 500 households about waste: 320 recycle, 180 compost, 120 do both. Estimate P(recycle or compost inclusive) and P(neither). If the council targets households doing neither, how many letterboxes get the flyer?
3. **Vocational.** A mechanic records faults in 200 cars: 80 have brake issues, 60 have tyre issues, 25 have both. Estimate P(brake or tyre issue) and P(brake only, not tyre). The workshop assigns extra time if a car has both — what proportion requires extra time?
4. **Ethical.** A school surveys 300 students about social media: 250 use platform A, 200 use platform B, 180 use both. Estimate P(A or B), P(A only), P(B only). Discuss what these overlapping usage rates mean for data privacy (information shared across platforms).
5. **Cross-cultural.** At a multicultural food festival, 600 visitors are surveyed: 350 tried Asian cuisine, 280 tried European cuisine, 150 tried both. Estimate P(Asian or European), P(Asian exclusive or European), P(neither). What proportion tried only one type?

## Memorable takeaways
- Relative frequency = frequency / total trials. Estimates probability from data.
- P(A and B): both happen. Count the overlap.
- P(A or B) inclusive = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). At least one happens.
- P(A or B) exclusive = P(A) + P(B) − 2×P(A and B). Exactly one, not both.
- Always subtract the overlap to avoid double-counting.
- More data → more reliable estimates.

## Stretch yourself
A school of 400 students tracks participation in three programs: Sport (S), Music (M) and Art (A). Data: 200 in Sport, 150 in Music, 100 in Art, 60 in Sport and Music, 40 in Sport and Art, 30 in Music and Art, 20 in all three. (a) Draw a Venn diagram and find the number of students in each of the 8 regions (including "none"). (b) Calculate P(S or M or A) using the inclusion-exclusion principle: P(S∪M∪A) = P(S)+P(M)+P(A)−P(S∩M)−P(S∩A)−P(M∩A)+P(S∩M∩A). (c) Find P(exactly one program), P(exactly two programs), P(all three). ## Watch out for these traps
- "I forgot to subtract the overlap." P(A or B) ≠ P(A) + P(B) unless A and B can't both happen (mutually exclusive). Always check for overlap.
- "I confused inclusive and exclusive 'or'." Inclusive: A or B or both. Exclusive: A or B but not both. Context tells you which is meant.
- "I assumed events are independent without checking." P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) only if independent. If you have actual data, use the data directly rather than assuming independence.
- "I divided by the wrong total." Relative frequency uses the grand total of all observations, not just one category's total.

A note on certainty. The addition rule for probability is established mathematics. Relative frequency estimates approach true probabilities as sample size increases (the law of large numbers). Small samples give rough estimates; large samples give reliable ones. In all cases, the quality of the data matters — biased collection methods produce biased estimates regardless of sample size.
