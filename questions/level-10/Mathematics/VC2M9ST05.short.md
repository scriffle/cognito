# VC2M9ST05 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Plan and conduct statistical investigations involving the collection and analysis of different kinds of data; report findings and discuss the strength of evidence to support any conclusions.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Investigations follow a cycle: question → plan → collect → analyse → report. Evidence strength depends on sample size, method and bias control. Correlation ≠ causation.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **plan and conduct statistical investigations** and report findings with honest discussion of evidence strength.
- You should identify bias, choose appropriate methods, and distinguish strong from weak evidence.

## Core content

**Statistical questions.** Expect variability, are specific and measurable, target a defined population. "What proportion of Year 10 students rate maths 4+/5?" is good. "Do students like maths?" is vague.

**Planning.** Define population, sample, sampling method (random reduces bias), and variables. Larger, more representative samples give stronger evidence.

**Sources of bias.** Selection (unrepresentative sample), response (leading questions), non-response (who doesn't answer?), measurement (inconsistent methods). Identify and minimise each.

**Analysis.** Summary statistics (mean, median, IQR), appropriate displays, comparisons between groups. Use technology for larger datasets. Look for patterns, outliers, group differences.

**Worked example.** Sleep vs maths scores: 60 students, two groups. 8+ hours median 72; <8 hours median 64. Conclusion: suggestive difference but observational study — cannot prove causation. Self-report sleep may be inaccurate. Report limitations honestly.

**Strength of evidence.** Strong: large sample, random/representative, controlled variables, replicable. Weak: small sample, convenience, uncontrolled confounders, self-report. Always report findings + strength + limitations.

**Victorian and cultural context.** The ABS Census follows rigorous investigation principles. VicHealth uses stratified random sampling. Indigenous Data Sovereignty (Mayi Kuwayu Study) ensures community-led data governance for First Nations research. Cross-cultural research requires sensitivity to language and cultural context.

## Memorable takeaways
- Cycle: question → plan → collect → analyse → interpret → report.
- Larger, random, representative samples = stronger evidence.
- Correlation ≠ causation (observational studies suggest, don't prove).
- Report honestly: findings + evidence strength + limitations.
- Identify and minimise bias at every stage.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I concluded causation from a survey." Only controlled experiments establish causation. Surveys show correlation.
- "I surveyed friends and generalised." Convenience samples aren't representative. Acknowledge or fix.
- "I only reported the result I wanted." Report ALL findings, including null results. Cherry-picking is dishonest.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Better question: "Do students like maths?" vs the specific version?* Specific one — measurable, targets a population, expects variability.
- *Surveying friends about Melbourne commuters — appropriate?* No. Convenience sample, not representative of all workers.
- *Online-only survey — what bias?* Excludes people without internet/digital literacy.
- *8 friends → "teenagers prefer Instagram" — evidence strength?* Very weak. Tiny sample, convenience, can't generalise.
- *Observational study finds sleep correlates with scores — can we say "causes"?* No. Association only. Confounders not controlled.
