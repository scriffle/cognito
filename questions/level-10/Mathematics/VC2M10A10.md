# VC2M10A10 — Mathematics

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2M10A10. Solve problems involving gradients of parallel and perpendicular lines.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics (Algebra).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Parallel lines have equal gradients — they run in the same direction and never meet.
  - Perpendicular lines have gradients that are negative reciprocals — their product is −1.
  - These relationships let you determine whether lines are parallel or perpendicular, and construct new lines with desired properties.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Design and Technologies.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **use gradient relationships** to solve problems involving **parallel and perpendicular lines**.
- You are learning to find equations of lines that are parallel or perpendicular to a given line through a given point.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Calculate the gradient of a line from two points or from its equation.
- Determine whether two lines are parallel, perpendicular or neither.
- Find the equation of a line parallel or perpendicular to a given line through a specified point.
- Apply these relationships to solve geometric and real-world problems.

## Core content

**1. Gradient recap.** The **gradient** (m) of a line measures its steepness: m = rise/run = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). A positive gradient slopes up-left-to-right; negative slopes down. Zero gradient is horizontal; undefined gradient is vertical. *Quick check:* Find the gradient of the line through (1, 3) and (4, 9).

**2. Parallel lines.** Two lines are **parallel** if and only if they have **equal gradients**: m₁ = m₂. They run in the same direction and never intersect (assuming they are distinct lines). Example: y = 3x + 2 and y = 3x − 5 are parallel (both have gradient 3). *Quick check:* Are y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 7 parallel? How do you know?

**3. Perpendicular lines.** Two lines are **perpendicular** if their gradients are **negative reciprocals**: m₁ × m₂ = −1, or m₂ = −1/m₁. Example: if one line has gradient 2, a line perpendicular to it has gradient −1/2. If one has gradient −3/4, the perpendicular has gradient 4/3. *Quick check:* A line has gradient 5. What is the gradient of a line perpendicular to it?

**4. Finding the equation of a parallel line.** To find the equation of a line parallel to y = 2x + 3 passing through (1, 7): use gradient m = 2 (same as the given line). Point-gradient form: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) → y − 7 = 2(x − 1) → y = 2x + 5. *Quick check:* Find the equation of the line parallel to y = −3x + 1 through (2, 4).

**5. Finding the equation of a perpendicular line.** To find the line perpendicular to y = 2x + 3 through (1, 7): perpendicular gradient = −1/2. y − 7 = −1/2(x − 1) → y = −x/2 + 1/2 + 7 → y = −x/2 + 15/2. *Quick check:* Find the gradient of the line perpendicular to y = 4x − 2. Then write its equation through (8, 1).

**6. Worked example — designing perpendicular paths in a park.**
A walking path runs along the line y = (3/4)x + 2. A new path must cross it at right angles at the point (4, 5).

*Follow the thinking:*
- Given gradient: m₁ = 3/4.
- Perpendicular gradient: m₂ = −4/3 (negative reciprocal).
- Point: (4, 5). Equation: y − 5 = −4/3(x − 4).
- Expand: y − 5 = −4x/3 + 16/3.
- y = −4x/3 + 16/3 + 15/3 = −4x/3 + 31/3.
- Check gradient product: (3/4)(−4/3) = −1 ✓.
- Check point: −4(4)/3 + 31/3 = −16/3 + 31/3 = 15/3 = 5 ✓.

**7. Applications.** Parallel and perpendicular gradients are used in: urban planning (grid-pattern streets), construction (walls perpendicular to floors), navigation (course corrections at right angles), and coordinate geometry proofs (showing rectangles, squares, rhombuses). *Quick check:* How would you prove that a quadrilateral with vertices A, B, C, D is a rectangle using gradients?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Melbourne's Hoddle Grid has parallel streets (Swanston, Elizabeth, Queen) crossed by perpendicular streets (Collins, Bourke, Lonsdale) — a real-world application of gradient relationships. Surveyors use perpendicular offsets when mapping property boundaries. Engineers designing the Melbourne Metro Tunnel calculated perpendicular cross-sections at regular intervals. AMSI resources connect these ideas to architectural design.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Directional knowledge is central to First Nations navigation and land management — understanding perpendicular and parallel relationships in landscape features (ridgelines, watercourses, boundary markers) supports movement across Country. While these concepts are embedded in practice rather than Cartesian coordinates, the spatial reasoning is analogous. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Grid-pattern cities exist worldwide — from ancient Roman planned towns to modern Melbourne. Students from diverse backgrounds can compare the street layouts of cities their families come from and identify parallel and perpendicular structures.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A shelf bracket is attached to a vertical wall. The wall has undefined gradient; the shelf must be horizontal (gradient 0). Are the wall and shelf perpendicular? Explain using gradients.
2. **Civic.** A council plans a new street parallel to an existing street with equation y = (1/2)x + 4. The new street must pass through (6, 1). Find its equation.
3. **Vocational.** A surveyor needs to mark a fence line perpendicular to a property boundary. The boundary runs from (0, 0) to (8, 6). Find the gradient of the boundary and the gradient of the fence. Write the equation of the fence if it starts at the midpoint of the boundary.
4. **Ethical.** A student says "perpendicular gradients multiply to give 1." Identify the error, correct it, and give an example showing why the sign matters.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Research the Roman grid-pattern town of Timgad (Algeria). Then prove algebraically that the lines y = 2x + 1 and y = −x/2 + 6 are perpendicular.

## Memorable takeaways
- Parallel lines: m₁ = m₂ (equal gradients).
- Perpendicular lines: m₁ × m₂ = −1 (negative reciprocals).
- Point-gradient form: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁).
- To find a parallel line: keep the gradient, use the new point.
- To find a perpendicular line: take the negative reciprocal of the gradient, use the new point.

## Stretch yourself
A triangle has vertices at A(0, 0), B(6, 0) and C(2, 4). Find the gradient of each side. Determine whether any sides are perpendicular (and hence whether the triangle is right-angled). Then find the equation of the altitude from C to side AB (the altitude is perpendicular to AB through C). Find where this altitude meets AB. Calculate the length of the altitude and hence the area of the triangle. Finally, find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of AB and show that it passes through the midpoint of AB with the correct gradient. 250–300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- **Forgetting the negative in the reciprocal.** Perpendicular gradients are negative reciprocals: if m = 3, the perpendicular is −1/3, not 1/3.
- **Confusing "negative reciprocal" with "just flip."** The reciprocal of 2/3 is 3/2, but the negative reciprocal is −3/2.
- **Vertical and horizontal lines.** A vertical line (undefined gradient) is perpendicular to a horizontal line (gradient 0). The product rule m₁ × m₂ = −1 does not apply here — treat this as a special case.
- **Using the wrong point.** When writing the equation, use the specified point, not the y-intercept of the original line.

## Try drawing this
Draw a coordinate grid. Plot the line y = (2/3)x + 1. At the point (3, 3), draw the perpendicular line (gradient −3/2). Label both gradients. Show the right-angle symbol at the intersection. Write both equations beside their lines.

**Try these scenarios**
1. *Shelf and wall.* Vertical has undefined gradient; horizontal has gradient 0. By convention, they are perpendicular (the product rule is a special case here).
2. *New street.* m = 1/2. y − 1 = 1/2(x − 6) → y = x/2 − 2.
3. *Surveyor's fence.* Boundary gradient = 6/8 = 3/4. Perpendicular = −4/3. Midpoint = (4, 3). Equation: y − 3 = −4/3(x − 4) → y = −4x/3 + 25/3.
4. *Error correction.* The product should be −1, not +1. Example: gradients 2 and 1/2 multiply to give 1 (not perpendicular). Gradients 2 and −1/2 multiply to give −1 (perpendicular). The negative sign is essential.
5. *Timgad + proof.* Gradient of y = 2x + 1 is 2. Gradient of y = −x/2 + 6 is −1/2. Product: 2 × (−1/2) = −1. Therefore perpendicular.
