# VC2M10M04 — Mathematics (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Use mathematical modelling to solve practical problems involving direct and inverse proportion and scaling of objects; formulate problems and interpret solutions in terms of the situation, including the impact of measurement errors on the accuracy of results.
- **Year / Area:** Years 9–10, Mathematics.
- **Big ideas:** Direct proportion: y = kx (double one → double the other). Inverse proportion: y = k/x (double one → halve the other). Scaling: lengths ×k, areas ×k², volumes ×k³. Models require stated assumptions and error analysis.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **use mathematical modelling** to solve practical problems involving direct and inverse proportion and scaling.
- You should formulate problems, interpret solutions, and report on assumptions and measurement error.

## Core content

**Direct proportion.** y = kx. When one quantity doubles, the other doubles. Graph: straight line through the origin. Example: cost = price per litre × litres. The constant k is the constant of proportionality.

**Inverse proportion.** y = k/x, or xy = k. When one doubles, the other halves. Graph: hyperbola. Example: 4 workers × 6 hours = 24 worker-hours. Two workers take 12 hours.

**Identifying the type.** Double one quantity — does the other double (direct) or halve (inverse)? Not all relationships are proportional. Check the data fits y = kx or y = k/x before assuming.

**Scale factors.** When an object is scaled by factor k: lengths × k, areas × k², volumes × k³. A 1:10 model holding 5 L represents a real tank of 5 × 10³ = 5 000 L.

**Measurement errors.** Every measurement has uncertainty. Errors compound when multiplied (area, volume). A room 5.0 ± 0.1 m by 4.0 ± 0.1 m has area ranging from 19.1 to 20.9 m². Report results to appropriate significant figures.

**The modelling cycle.** (1) Identify the problem, (2) make assumptions, (3) build a model, (4) solve and interpret, (5) compare with reality, (6) refine if needed, (7) report assumptions and findings.

**Victorian and cultural context.** VicRoads uses proportional modelling for road-surfacing estimates. Melbourne Water tests dam spillways with scale models (volume ∝ k³). First Nations fish trap designs at Budj Bim scale proportionally to stream width. Boomerang design involves proportional relationships between arm length and flight characteristics. Recipe scaling across multicultural cuisines is direct proportion in daily practice.

## Memorable takeaways
- Direct: y = kx. Double one → double the other.
- Inverse: y = k/x. Double one → halve the other.
- Scaling: lengths ×k, areas ×k², volumes ×k³.
- Models need stated assumptions. Errors compound in area/volume calculations.

## Watch out for these traps
- "I scaled area by k instead of k²." Areas scale as the square. Lengths triple → area ×9.
- "I assumed every relationship is proportional." Check the data first — not everything is y = kx or y = k/x.
- "I ignored measurement error." Small dimension errors become large area/volume errors. Always report the impact.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *3 cups flour for 12 scones — how many for 20?* k = 3/12 = 0.25. For 20: 5 cups.
- *Journey: 3 hours at 80 km/h — time at 60 km/h?* Distance = 240 km. Time = 240/60 = 4 hours.
- *Cubes: side 2 cm vs side 6 cm — volume ratio?* Scale factor = 3. Ratio = 3³ = 27.
- *1:20 model holds 3.5 L — real volume?* 3.5 × 20³ = 3.5 × 8 000 = 28 000 L.
- *Tile scale factor 2.5 — glaze ratio?* Area scales: 2.5² = 6.25×.
