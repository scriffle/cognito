# VC2HG10S04 — Geography

## Snapshot

- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Geography (Geographical inquiry — skills).

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **interpret and evaluate** data to make **generalisations and predictions**, **analyse patterns and trends**, **infer relationships** and **make forecasts**.
- You are learning to reason carefully from evidence.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **trend**, **correlation**, **causation** and **forecast** in your own words.
- Read patterns at multiple scales.
- Distinguish correlation from causation.
- Make a defensible forecast with uncertainty.

## Core content

**1. Patterns at scale.** Local: a beach, a creek. Regional: a catchment, a city. National: a state, a country. Global: the planet. Patterns at one scale can hide or contradict patterns at another. *Quick check:* Why might a town's heat trend look different from the global trend?

**2. Trends and variability.** A **trend** is the long-term direction; **variability** is short-term fluctuation. Both matter. *Quick check:* Why do bushfire scientists separate trend from variability?

**3. Correlation vs causation.** **Correlation**: two variables change together. **Causation**: one drives the other. Confounders, reverse causation and coincidence can mimic causation. *Quick check:* Why does "ice-cream sales correlate with drowning" not mean ice-cream causes drowning?

**4. Inference and reasoning.** Strong inferences combine multiple lines of evidence — observation, model, theory, expert judgement. Note assumptions and uncertainty. *Quick check:* Why does naming assumptions strengthen an inference?

**5. Forecasts.** A **forecast** estimates a future state with uncertainty. Use scenarios (e.g., low/medium/high emissions), confidence intervals and time horizons. *Quick check:* Why are climate forecasts published as ranges?

**6. Worked example: heatwaves in Melbourne.** Trend: average summer temperatures rising. Variability: any single summer may be cooler. Correlation: heatwaves correlate with mortality, especially among older adults. Causation: heat physically stresses bodies. Forecast: by 2050, climate models project more days above 35 °C in Melbourne (medium emissions). *Follow the thinking:* pattern → trend → cause → forecast.

**7. Limitations and ethics.** Be honest about uncertainty. Avoid alarmism and false comfort. Disaggregate by group so vulnerable populations are not hidden in averages. *Quick check:* Why does disaggregation matter for heat-mortality forecasts?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** BoM Climate Change in Australia; CSIRO climate projections; Victorian Climate Projections 2019; ABS Census trend data.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations long-term observation of seasons and Country contributes to local forecasting. ## Watch out for these traps
- "One year proves a trend." It doesn't.
- "Correlation is causation." Not without reasoning.
- "Forecasts are predictions of fact." They are conditional on assumptions.
- "Averages tell the whole story." Disaggregate.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Town heat trend vs global.* Local effects (urban heat island; coastal moderation) can mask or amplify global trend.
2. *Trend vs variability separation.* Helps focus policy on the long-term shift rather than year-to-year noise.
3. *Ice-cream and drowning.* Both rise with summer; the confounder is season.
4. *Why naming assumptions.* Makes the inference testable.
5. *Why climate forecasts are ranges.* Future emissions and model differences create uncertainty.
6. *Why disaggregate for heat mortality.* Older adults, people with chronic illness and outdoor workers face higher risk.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Melbourne rainfall graph.* Declining trend; high inter-year variability; inference: drying climate.
2. *Whittlesea heatwave demand.* Population projections; building stock; vulnerable groups. Uncertainty: future migration.
3. *BoM seasonal outlook.* Plain language; probability ranges; visualisation.
4. *Forecasts-causing-panic debate.* For: avoid alarmism. Against: avoid complacency. Both sides accept clear communication.
5. *Gariwerd six-season alongside BoM.* Step 1: source from published community material. Step 2: discuss with respect. Step 3: avoid claiming ownership of knowledge.
