## Core content

**1. Custodial responsibility.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples describe a responsibility to Country given through Law, kinship and ceremony. This is not ownership in a market sense; it is an ongoing duty of care. *Quick check:* What is the difference between custody and ownership?

**2. Country.** **Country** includes lands, waters, skies, plants, animals, ancestors and people in relationship. It is plural — many Countries across the continent. *Quick check:* Why is "Country" not the same as "land"?

**3. Caring for Country practices.** **Cultural burning** at low intensity to manage fuel, biodiversity and cultural sites. **Seasonal harvesting** to maintain populations. **Songlines and stories** that transmit ecological knowledge. **Ranger programs** combining traditional and Western methods. **Cultural flows** in rivers. **Sea Country** management. *Quick check:* What is one ecological benefit of cultural burning?

**4. Joint management and Indigenous Protected Areas.** Many Australian parks are jointly managed; some Indigenous Protected Areas are wholly Indigenous-managed. They cover the largest share of Australia's conservation estate. *Quick check:* What is an Indigenous Protected Area?

**5. Worked example: Dja Dja Wurrung cultural burning.** In central Victoria, the Dja Dja Wurrung Clans Aboriginal Corporation, with Parks Victoria and CFA, has led cultural burns in Greater Bendigo National Park and elsewhere. Outcomes include reduced fuel loads, restored grassy woodlands and youth-led cultural transmission. *Follow the thinking:* one Country, many partners, measurable outcomes.

**6. Worked example: Yolŋu Sea Country.** In Arnhem Land, Yolŋu ranger programs manage marine debris, control crocodile populations and conduct cultural research, partnering with CSIRO. *Quick check:* What does sea-country management involve beyond fishing rules?

**7. Why this knowledge matters now.** Environmental crises demand multiple knowledge systems. First Nations management is long-tested, place-specific and currently expanding through ranger programs, Indigenous Protected Areas and Aboriginal corporations. *Quick check:* Why is "two-way knowledge" a strong design principle?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Dja Dja Wurrung, Yorta Yorta, Wadawurrung, Gunaikurnai, Bunurong/Boon Wurrung, Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung, Taungurung, Eastern Maar and Jardwadjali Peoples lead caring-for-Country work. Parks Victoria's joint-management agreements and the First Peoples' Assembly negotiate the framework.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Custodial responsibility is articulated through Lore, kinship and protocols. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material, including Aboriginal corporation websites and AIATSIS publications.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant farming and Landcare groups increasingly partner with Traditional Owners. Multilingual community programs make caring-for-Country accessible.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Custody is just culture; not management." It is also evidence-based management.
- "Aboriginal management is in the past." It is current and expanding.
- "Cultural burning is one technique." It is a knowledge system with many forms.
- "Specific knowledge is for everyone to use." It belongs to communities; cite published, community-approved material only.
