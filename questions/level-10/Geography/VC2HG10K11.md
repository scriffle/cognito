# VC2HG10K11 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG10K11. Geographical approaches to understanding the causes and consequences of a selected environmental issue.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Geography (Environmental change and management).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Geography offers a toolkit for environmental issues.
  - Place, scale and interconnection matter.
  - Good analysis links causes to consequences and responses.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Sustainability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning **geographical approaches** to understanding the **causes and consequences** of a selected **environmental issue**.
- You are learning to apply the geographic toolkit to one issue in depth.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **geographical approach**, **scale** and **causation chain** in your own words.
- Apply at least three geographic concepts to one issue.
- Describe causes, consequences and management responses at different scales.
- Communicate findings clearly.

## Core content

**1. The geographic toolkit.** Concepts: space, place, environment, interconnection, change, sustainability, scale. Methods: maps, GIS, field observation, satellite imagery, statistical data, qualitative interviews. *Quick check:* Why does scale matter when describing a problem?

**2. Causation in geographic problems.** Causes can be **proximate** (immediate, e.g., a fire ignition) or **systemic** (longer term, e.g., climate change drying fuel loads). Strong analysis links both. *Quick check:* What is the difference between proximate and systemic causes?

**3. Consequences.** Consequences fall on **places**, **populations** and **environmental functions**. They can be physical, economic, social, cultural and intergenerational. *Quick check:* Why are intergenerational consequences hard to weigh?

**4. Worked example: bushfire risk in Victoria.** **Causes** (proximate): lightning, arson, escaped burn-offs. **Causes** (systemic): climate change drying fuels, urban-rural interface growth, biodiversity changes, fire-history changes since colonisation disrupted cultural burning. **Consequences**: lives lost, homes destroyed, ecosystem damage, smoke health impacts, insurance costs, displaced wildlife. **Responses**: fuel management, building codes, evacuation planning, cultural burning programs (e.g., Dja Dja Wurrung), Black Summer Royal Commission recommendations. *Follow the thinking:* one issue across multiple scales and stakeholder groups.

**5. Worked example: plastic pollution in Port Phillip Bay.** **Causes**: stormwater carrying plastic; littering; microplastics from textiles and tyres; ocean currents bringing offshore plastic. **Consequences**: marine wildlife harm; food-web contamination; tourism impact; cleanup costs. **Responses**: container deposit schemes; plastic-bag bans; clean-up programs; product redesign. *Quick check:* Why does upstream redesign beat downstream cleanup?

**6. Scale analysis.** Local: a single beach or creek. Regional: a catchment or city. National: policy frameworks. Global: ocean currents and trade. *Quick check:* What is one issue that requires action at all four scales?

**7. Communicating findings.** Use maps, infographics, models and short text. Cite sources. Acknowledge uncertainty. Recommend responses scaled to your audience. *Quick check:* Why does an audience matter to a geographic report?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements (2020); Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action; Country Fire Authority; EPA Victoria.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Cultural burning by Traditional Owners — Dja Dja Wurrung Country, Bunurong/Boon Wurrung Country, Wadawurrung Country — is increasingly recognised in fire policy. The Firesticks Alliance and Victorian Aboriginal Heritage Council publish guidance. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual emergency communication is critical for diverse communities in fire zones. Migrant farming communities are partners in stewardship.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one environmental issue in Victoria. Apply at least four geographic concepts. Identify proximate and systemic causes. Describe consequences across two scales. Propose three responses including one Aboriginal-led approach. Cite reputable sources. 300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "One cause, one consequence." Most environmental issues are systems.
- "Local is enough." Most issues need multi-scale action.
- "Cultural burning is one technique." It is a long-developed knowledge system, not a single method.
- "Maps speak for themselves." A map needs framing, context and sources.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why scale matters.* Different drivers and responses operate at different scales; missing a scale misses solutions.
2. *Proximate vs systemic causes.* Proximate is immediate; systemic is the longer-term context that made the proximate cause possible.
3. *Why intergenerational consequences are hard to weigh.* Future people cannot speak now; effects compound; discount-rate choices are contested.
4. *Why upstream redesign beats cleanup.* Stops the problem at source rather than chasing it downstream.
5. *Issue across all four scales.* Climate change; plastic pollution; biodiversity loss.
6. *Why audience matters.* Different audiences need different framing, vocabulary and recommended actions.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Halls Gap fire story.* Causes: dry fuels; ignition; wind. Consequences: ecosystem damage; tourism loss; resident displacement.
2. *Yarra Ranges preparedness.* Vegetation management; evacuation planning; multilingual communication.
3. *Plastic-pollution brief.* Sources and pathways; ecological impact; policy options.
4. *Prescribed burning debate.* For: hazard reduction. Against: smoke and biodiversity impact. Both sides accept cultural-burning evidence.
5. *Dja Dja Wurrung partnership.* Step 1: Dja Dja Wurrung Clans Aboriginal Corporation contact. Step 2: agreed cultural protocols. Step 3: integrate cultural-burning knowledge into school content with credit.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: bushfire in the Yarra Ranges.* Concepts: place, change, interconnection, scale. Proximate causes: ignition and dry fuels. Systemic causes: climate change; fragmented landscapes; loss of cultural burning. Consequences: local (homes), regional (water-catchment damage). Responses: building codes; cultural burning with Dja Dja Wurrung partners; better warning systems. Sources cited.
