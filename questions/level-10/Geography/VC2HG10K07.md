- You are learning to read trade as a sustainability issue, not just an economic one.

By the end of this you should be able to:

## Core content

**1. Embodied carbon.** Every product carries the emissions used to make and transport it. When goods are imported, the emissions occurred elsewhere but the consumption is here. *Quick check:* If China makes steel for an Australian bridge, where do the emissions count?

**2. Production vs consumption accounting.** Most national emissions are reported on a production basis. Consumption-based accounting reallocates emissions to where goods are used. Australia's consumption emissions are higher than its production emissions for some categories. *Quick check:* Why does the accounting choice matter for fairness?

**3. Sustainability impacts of trade.** Resource extraction stress; biodiversity loss in source countries; air and water pollution; pollution offshoring; transport emissions; spread of invasive species. Also positive flows: green-tech transfer; renewable-energy investment; circular-economy initiatives. *Quick check:* What is one positive sustainability effect of trade?

**4. Embodied water and land.** Beyond carbon, products also carry embodied water and land use. A kilogram of beef carries thousands of litres of water. Imported textiles carry cotton land use elsewhere. *Quick check:* Why is the concept of "virtual water" useful?

**5. Worked example: imported lithium-ion batteries.** Lithium mined in Australia is shipped to China for cell production, then assembled into batteries shipped back to Australia for electric vehicles. Each step carries embodied carbon. Australia's consumption-based footprint for EV batteries thus includes Chinese manufacturing emissions. *Follow the thinking:* tracking emissions across borders requires system-level data.

**6. Policy responses.** Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (EU CBAM, from 2026 fully); supply-chain reporting (Modern Slavery Act and proposed climate-disclosure rules); circular-economy strategies; product-level carbon labelling. *Quick check:* What does a Carbon Border Adjustment do?

**7. Australian context.** Australia is a major exporter of iron ore, coal, gas and lithium. Coal and gas exports add to global emissions even when not counted in Australian emissions. The Climate Change Authority and the Productivity Commission report on these flows. *Quick check:* Why does Australia's coal export complicate its national emissions claim?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Port of Melbourne is the busiest container port. Victorian climate policy aims for net-zero by 2045. The Yallourn power station is closing in 2028, replaced by renewables.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal communities are affected by mining, climate change and trade decisions on Country. The Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation and First Nations Clean Energy Network publish on these issues. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Diaspora communities link Australia to climate-vulnerable countries — Pacific neighbours, parts of South and South-East Asia. These links inform Victorian advocacy and remittance flows.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Emissions only count where made." For fair accounting, consumption matters too.
- "Buying local is always greener." Sometimes; check transport, energy mix and method.
- "Carbon labelling solves everything." It helps; it doesn't replace systemic change.
- "Trade is bad for sustainability." It also moves clean tech and finance.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Where steel-making emissions count.* In production-based accounting, they count in China; in consumption-based, in Australia.
2. *Why accounting matters for fairness.* Countries that consume goods without making them can look cleaner than they are.
3. *Positive sustainability effect of trade.* Renewable-tech transfer and finance flows.
4. *Why virtual water is useful.* It reveals hidden water demand in imports.
5. *What CBAM does.* Charges imports for their carbon content so domestic producers are not undercut by higher-emission imports.
6. *Coal-export complication.* The emissions when burned overseas are large but not counted as Australia's.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *T-shirt choice.* Embodied carbon; labour conditions; transport.
2. *Melbourne CBD consumption emissions.* Procurement standards; food-waste reduction; sustainable-building codes.
3. *CBAM briefing.* Affected exports (steel, aluminium, cement); compliance cost; opportunity for clean-tech exports.
4. *Coal-export responsibility debate.* For: producer responsibility. Against: importer responsibility. Both sides agree on transition planning.
5. *Pacific climate-justice partnership.* Step 1: listen first. Step 2: align actions to community priorities. Step 3: commit to ongoing relationship.
