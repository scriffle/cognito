# VC2HG10S05 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG10S05. Consider ethical values and perspectives to justify conclusions related to a phenomenon, issue or challenge using geographical concepts.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Geography (Geographical inquiry — skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Conclusions carry ethical weight.
  - Different values produce different conclusions.
  - Justification requires reasoning, not opinion.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Ethical Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **consider ethical values and perspectives** to **justify conclusions** using **geographical concepts**.
- You are learning to defend conclusions with reasoning, not just data.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **ethical value**, **perspective** and **justification** in your own words.
- Identify three ethical values commonly at stake in geography.
- Use geographic concepts to structure a justified conclusion.
- Acknowledge perspectives you do not share.

## Core content

**1. Ethical values in geography.** **Equity** (fair share); **sustainability** (intergenerational fairness); **autonomy** (self-determination); **dignity**; **stewardship**; **cultural integrity**. *Quick check:* Which value is at stake when an export ban during a drought raises global food prices?

**2. Perspectives.** People bring different perspectives shaped by experience, culture, role and worldview. A Traditional Owner, a farmer, an irrigator, a tourist and a hydrologist may share evidence but interpret it differently. *Quick check:* Why is "the data speaks for itself" rarely true?

**3. Justification.** A justified conclusion connects evidence to a claim through reasoning, names assumptions and acknowledges trade-offs. *Quick check:* What is the difference between an opinion and a justified conclusion?

**4. Using geographic concepts.** Frame justification through space, place, environment, interconnection, change, sustainability and scale. *Quick check:* Which concept best frames the ethics of a Murray water decision?

**5. Worked example: rezoning a Melbourne suburb for housing.** Evidence: population growth, demand for affordable housing, biodiversity in remnant vegetation, transport capacity. Ethical values in tension: equity (housing access); sustainability (green cover, emissions); cultural integrity (cultural heritage on Country); autonomy (existing residents' voice). Conclusion: rezone with green-cover requirements, cultural-heritage protection and Wurundjeri consultation. *Follow the thinking:* concepts → evidence → values → justified conclusion.

**6. Worked example: cultural-burning policy.** Evidence: ecological outcomes; Traditional Owner expertise; safety. Values: cultural integrity; safety; sustainability. Justification: support Aboriginal-led cultural burning with state agency coordination. *Quick check:* Why does cultural integrity belong in this justification?

**7. Acknowledging other perspectives.** Strong conclusions acknowledge views you do not share, summarise them fairly and explain where you disagree and why. *Quick check:* Why does fair-mindedness strengthen, not weaken, your conclusion?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian Government's ethical-decision-making frameworks; Victorian Aboriginal Heritage Council guidance; EPA environmental-justice principles.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Cultural integrity, custodial responsibility and self-determination are central ethical values in Australian geography. Yoorrook Justice Commission reports and AIATSIS Code of Ethics guide practice. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant, refugee and faith communities bring varied ethical traditions; multilingual deliberation strengthens conclusions.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You support a new bike lane. Identify three ethical values to weigh.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Yarra debates parking removal. Suggest three values and one trade-off.
3. **Vocational.** A graduate planner writes a justification for a green roof. Outline three reasoning steps.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates whether to support a controversial mining project on Country. Identify two arguments on each side.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A school project recommends a Country acknowledgement protocol. Outline three respectful steps.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one Victorian planning or environmental decision. Identify three ethical values in tension. Use at least three geographic concepts to frame your justified conclusion. Acknowledge one perspective you do not share. Cite reputable sources. 300 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Justifying is just stating your view." It requires evidence and reasoning.
- "All perspectives are equally weighted." Some have more authority on specific questions (e.g., Traditional Owners on cultural heritage).
- "Ethics is fluffy." It is decision-making under values and trade-offs.
- "Acknowledging others weakens you." It strengthens credibility.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Export-ban value.* Equity (between countries) and sustainability of trade trust.
2. *Why "data speaks" rarely true.* Framing and values shape interpretation.
3. *Opinion vs justified conclusion.* The conclusion ties evidence and reasoning together.
4. *Concept for Murray water.* Sustainability and interconnection.
5. *Why cultural integrity in cultural burning.* It is Aboriginal-led knowledge being applied on Country.
6. *Why fair-mindedness strengthens.* It pre-empts objections and shows reasoning quality.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Bike-lane values.* Safety; equity of road space; environment.
2. *Parking removal.* Equity (mobility); safety (active transport); accessibility.
3. *Green roof justification.* Evidence on stormwater and heat; ethical value (sustainability); recommendation.
4. *Mining-on-Country debate.* For: economic benefit. Against: cultural and environmental harm. Both sides accept free, prior and informed consent.
5. *Country acknowledgement protocol.* Step 1: invite Traditional Owner input. Step 2: avoid token language. Step 3: review annually.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Decision: housing rezoning in a Melbourne suburb.* Values: equity, sustainability, cultural integrity. Concepts: place, interconnection, change. Justification: rezone with green-cover, cultural-heritage and consultation requirements. Acknowledge concerns from existing residents; explain trade-offs. Sources cited.
