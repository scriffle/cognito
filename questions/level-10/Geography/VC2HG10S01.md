# VC2HG10S01 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG10S01. Develop a range of questions for a geographical inquiry related to a phenomenon, issue or challenge.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Geography (Geographical inquiry — skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Good questions drive good inquiry.
  - Geographic questions probe space, place, scale and change.
  - Questions evolve as inquiry progresses.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy; Ethical Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop a range of questions** for a **geographical inquiry** related to a phenomenon, issue or challenge.
- You are learning to design questions that open inquiry, not close it.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **inquiry question**, **focus question** and **subsidiary question** in your own words.
- Write three types of geographic questions (descriptive, analytical, evaluative).
- Apply the geographic concepts when framing questions.
- Refine questions as evidence comes in.

## Core content

**1. What is an inquiry question?** An **inquiry question** is the main question that guides your investigation. Good ones are open, specific, geographic and answerable with available evidence. *Quick check:* What is wrong with "Is climate change bad?" as an inquiry question?

**2. Levels of question.** **Descriptive**: What, where, when, how much? **Analytical**: Why, how, what links to what? **Evaluative**: So what, what should be done, by whom? *Quick check:* Which level does "Why does the Mallee have lower SEIFA scores?" sit at?

**3. Using geographic concepts.** Frame questions through space, place, environment, interconnection, change, sustainability and scale. *Quick check:* Reframe "Why is housing expensive?" using interconnection.

**4. Subsidiary questions.** Big inquiry questions branch into smaller subsidiary questions that you can answer with data, fieldwork, interviews and maps. *Quick check:* What is one subsidiary question for "How sustainable is Port Phillip Bay?"

**5. Worked example: bushfire risk in the Otways.** **Inquiry question**: How is bushfire risk changing in the Otway Ranges and what management responses are working? **Descriptive**: How has fire frequency changed since 2000? **Analytical**: What drivers explain the change? **Evaluative**: Which management responses, including cultural burning, have improved outcomes? *Follow the thinking:* one big question, branching into answerable parts.

**6. Worked example: wellbeing in your local area.** Inquiry question: How does wellbeing vary across your council area and why? Subsidiary: which SEIFA scores; which services; which interventions. *Quick check:* What ethical considerations apply to interviewing people about wellbeing?

**7. Refining questions.** As evidence comes in, narrow or widen questions, drop dead ends and add new branches. Keep an explicit "questions log" alongside data. *Quick check:* Why should questions evolve, not stay fixed?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Department of Education curriculum exemplars; Geography Teachers' Association of Victoria (GTAV) examples.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Inquiry on Country requires respectful questions and Traditional Owner permission. AIATSIS publishes ethical research guidelines including the GERAIS code. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Inquiry involving diverse communities benefits from multilingual question design and culturally informed interviewing.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You want to investigate plastic pollution at a local beach. Write three questions (one per level).
2. **Civic.** Your council in Brimbank investigates housing affordability. Suggest one inquiry question and three subsidiary questions.
3. **Vocational.** A graduate at the Bureau of Meteorology designs a heatwave-risk inquiry. Outline three questions.
4. **Ethical.** A class plans to interview residents about climate change. Identify two ethical considerations.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A school inquiry partners with Wurundjeri Elders. Outline three respectful question-design steps.

## Memorable takeaways
- Good questions open inquiry.
- Use the three levels.
- Frame with geographic concepts.
- Branch into subsidiary questions.
- Refine as you go.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one local Victorian issue. Write one inquiry question and six subsidiary questions across the three levels, using at least four geographic concepts. Note one ethical consideration. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Yes/no questions are fine." They rarely drive inquiry.
- "One question is enough." Strong inquiries have layered questions.
- "Questions cannot change." They should evolve.
- "Ethics is paperwork." It shapes who you can ask and what you can publish.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Problem with "Is climate change bad?".* Yes/no; vague; not geographic; not answerable with one place's data.
2. *Level of "Why does the Mallee have lower SEIFA scores?".* Analytical.
3. *Reframe with interconnection.* "How is housing in Melbourne interconnected with migration, planning and global investment markets?"
4. *Subsidiary for Port Phillip Bay.* What is the trend in seagrass extent over the past 20 years?
5. *Ethical considerations for interviews.* Consent; cultural safety; data storage.
6. *Why questions evolve.* Evidence reshapes what is worth asking.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Plastic pollution.* Descriptive: how much plastic at the beach per visit. Analytical: where is it coming from. Evaluative: which interventions reduce it most.
2. *Brimbank housing.* Inquiry: How is housing affordability changing in Brimbank? Subsidiary: by suburb; by tenure; by service access.
3. *Heatwave risk.* What is the trend; who is most exposed; what early warning works.
4. *Resident interviews.* Informed consent; protection of vulnerable participants.
5. *Wurundjeri Elders inquiry.* Step 1: invitation and protocols. Step 2: co-design questions. Step 3: feedback and credit.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: Werribee River health.* Inquiry: How is the Werribee River's health changing and what management responses are most effective? Subsidiary questions span environment, change, interconnection and sustainability. Ethical consideration: data on private land requires consent. Sources cited.
