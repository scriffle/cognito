# VC2HG10K04 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG10K04. The environmental, economic and political constraints, including climate change, on the world's capacity to sustainably feed projected future global populations.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Geography (Biomes and food security).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Feeding a growing world is a system problem.
  - Environment, economy and politics all bind the system.
  - Climate change tightens every constraint.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Sustainability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Intercultural Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **environmental, economic and political constraints**, including **climate change**, on the world's capacity to **sustainably feed projected future populations**.
- You are learning to read global food security as a coupled system.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **food security**, **carrying capacity** and **yield gap** in your own words.
- Identify three environmental, three economic and three political constraints.
- Describe one climate-change mechanism that affects food supply.
- Discuss how Australia connects to global food security.

## Core content

**1. The big numbers.** World population is projected to be about 9.7 billion by 2050. Feeding that population sustainably requires roughly 50% more food than today on roughly the same land. *Quick check:* Why is "more land" not a simple answer?

**2. Environmental constraints.** Water scarcity; soil degradation; biodiversity loss; pollinator decline; extreme weather; sea-level rise affecting deltas; warming oceans affecting fisheries. *Quick check:* Why does soil loss take decades to reverse?

**3. Economic constraints.** Input costs (fertiliser, fuel, seed); commodity price volatility; concentration of seed and chemical companies; food waste (about a third of food produced); inequality in access. *Quick check:* Why does food waste matter for food security?

**4. Political constraints.** Trade rules and tariffs; conflict and displacement; subsidies that distort production; export bans during shocks; weak governance; corruption. *Quick check:* Why might an export ban during a drought hurt global food security?

**5. Climate change as a multiplier.** Heat stress reduces yields of staples (wheat, rice, maize); shifts growing zones poleward; alters rainfall patterns; increases pest range; raises sea levels into productive deltas; acidifies oceans. *Quick check:* Why is climate change called a "threat multiplier"?

**6. Worked example: wheat under climate pressure.** Major wheat producers include China, India, Russia, USA, Australia and the EU. Heatwaves at flowering reduce yields; drought lowers reserves; export disruptions ripple through prices. Australia's wheat belt is shifting; CSIRO breeds heat-tolerant varieties. *Follow the thinking:* environment + economy + politics + climate intersect in one commodity.

**7. Pathways to sustainable feeding.** Higher productivity through research; reducing waste; diet shifts (less meat where appropriate); regenerative agriculture; equitable trade; investment in low-income farmers; Indigenous and traditional knowledge integration. *Quick check:* Why is "more production" not enough on its own?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria is a significant grain, dairy and horticulture producer and exporter. Agriculture Victoria, CSIRO and the Bureau of Meteorology publish data. The 2017–2019 drought tested resilience across the state.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations food systems — yam daisy, kangaroo, native grains, bush foods — managed for tens of thousands of years offer insights for sustainable feeding. Bruce Pascoe's work has popularised native-grain research. The Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation supports related enterprises. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Australia exports food to Asia, the Middle East and the Pacific. Migrant food traditions shape demand; for example, halal-certified meat exports support South-East Asian markets.

## Watch out for these traps
- "There is enough food; it's just distributed badly." Distribution matters, but production is also constrained.
- "Climate change is a future problem." It is reducing yields now.
- "Indigenous food knowledge is folklore." Native grains and bush foods are subjects of current agronomy research.
- "Trade always helps food security." It usually does, but bans and tariffs can hurt.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "more land" is not simple.* Most arable land is in use; expanding into forests harms biodiversity and the climate.
2. *Why soil loss takes decades to reverse.* Topsoil forms slowly; recovery requires sustained practice changes.
3. *Why food waste matters.* About a third of food produced is wasted; reducing waste increases effective supply without new land.
4. *Why export bans hurt.* They raise prices for importing countries and reduce global trust in trade.
5. *Why climate change is a threat multiplier.* It worsens existing stresses on water, soil, pests and markets simultaneously.
6. *Why "more production" is not enough.* Without addressing waste, equity and politics, production alone leaves people hungry.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Dinner shopping.* Global wheat price; shipping disruptions; weather in export regions.
2. *Wyndham food-security forum.* Local food production; food-rescue networks; multicultural food access.
3. *Humanitarian briefing.* Environmental: drought and soil. Economic: market collapse. Political: conflict access.
4. *Eat-less-meat debate.* For: grain efficiency and emissions. Against: cultural and economic impact. Both sides accept evidence-based dietary advice.
5. *Native-grain partnership.* Step 1: contact community organisations. Step 2: agree purpose and IP arrangements. Step 3: integrate findings with academic and farmer networks.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Crop: wheat.* Environment: heat at flowering reduces yields. Economy: input cost rises hit smallholders. Politics: export bans cause shocks. Climate change makes heatwaves more likely, raises fertiliser costs through energy prices, and stresses trade systems. Pathways: heat-tolerant varieties; equitable trade rules. Sources cited.
