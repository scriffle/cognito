# VC2HG10K11 — Geography (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Geographical approaches to understanding the causes and consequences of a selected environmental issue.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Geography.
- **Big ideas:** Geography offers a toolkit. Place, scale and interconnection matter. Good analysis links causes to consequences and responses.

## What you are learning
- You are learning **geographical approaches** to the **causes and consequences** of a selected **environmental issue**.
- You should be able to apply the geographic toolkit in depth.

## Core content

**Toolkit.** Concepts: space, place, environment, interconnection, change, sustainability, scale. Methods: maps, GIS, field observation, satellite imagery, statistical data, qualitative interviews. **Causes** are **proximate** (immediate, e.g., ignition) and **systemic** (longer term, e.g., climate change drying fuels). **Consequences** fall on places, populations and environmental functions — physical, economic, social, cultural and intergenerational.

**Worked example: Victorian bushfire risk.** Proximate causes: lightning, arson, escaped burn-offs. Systemic causes: climate change drying fuels; urban-rural interface growth; loss of cultural burning since colonisation. Consequences: lives, homes, ecosystems, smoke health, insurance costs. Responses: fuel management; building codes; evacuation planning; cultural burning programs (Dja Dja Wurrung); Black Summer Royal Commission recommendations.

**Worked example: plastic pollution in Port Phillip Bay.** Causes: stormwater; littering; microplastics from textiles and tyres; offshore plastic. Consequences: marine wildlife harm; food-web contamination; tourism cost. Responses: container deposit schemes; plastic-bag bans; clean-up programs; product redesign.

**Scale.** Local → regional → national → global. Most issues need action at multiple scales. Communicate findings with maps, infographics and clear text; cite sources; acknowledge uncertainty.

**Cultural context.** Cultural burning by Traditional Owners is increasingly recognised in fire policy; the Firesticks Alliance publishes guidance. Multilingual emergency communication is critical for diverse communities. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Geography offers a toolkit.
- Causes are proximate and systemic.
- Consequences span scales.
- First Nations knowledge belongs at the policy table.

## Watch out for these traps
- "One cause, one consequence." Most environmental issues are systems.
- "Local is enough." Most issues need multi-scale action.
- "Maps speak for themselves." Maps need framing, context and sources.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why scale matters.* Different drivers and responses operate at different scales.
- *Proximate vs systemic.* Proximate is immediate; systemic is the longer-term context.
- *Why upstream redesign beats cleanup.* Stops the problem at source.
- *Why audience matters.* Different audiences need different framing and recommendations.
- *Short summary of bushfire analysis.* Proximate ignition meets systemic climate-and-fuel context; consequences hit homes, ecosystems and health; responses span fuel management, building codes and cultural burning.
