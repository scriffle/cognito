# VC2HG10K03 — Geography (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** The environmental, economic and social sustainability of farming in Australia.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Geography.
- **Big ideas:** Sustainability has three dimensions. Each is necessary. Practices are evolving.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **environmental, economic and social sustainability** of farming in Australia.
- You should be able to read farming through a sustainability lens.

## Core content

**Three dimensions.** **Environmental**: soil, water, biodiversity, climate. **Economic**: viability, profitability, market access. **Social**: rural communities, workers, mental health, cultural connection. None alone is enough.

**Environmental practices**: conservation tillage; cover cropping; integrated pest management; rotational grazing; revegetation; water-sensitive irrigation; renewable energy; biodiversity planting. **Economic practices**: diversification; value-add processing; cooperatives; long-term contracts; risk management; succession planning. **Social practices**: rural-community support; farmer mental-health services; fair workers' conditions; multilingual support; First Nations partnerships.

**Worked example.** A Goulburn Valley orchard. Environmental: shade-cloth, recycled irrigation, integrated pest management, native windbreaks. Economic: diversified crops, multiple markets, succession plan, cooperative. Social: Fair Work conditions for local and migrant workers, school partnerships, Yorta Yorta engagement. Each dimension reinforces the others.

Climate change is shifting growing conditions; farmers adapt through new varieties, timing changes and water management. Aboriginal-led initiatives — native-grass cropping, bush-food production, ranger programs, cultural burning — integrate all three dimensions; the Indigenous Land and Sea Corporation supports many.

**Cultural context.** Yorta Yorta, Dja Dja Wurrung, Wadawurrung and other Victorian peoples lead joint-management and farming partnerships. Migrant farmers contribute strongly in market gardening, viticulture and dairy. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Three dimensions together.
- Environment + economy + society.
- First Nations farming integrates dimensions.
- Climate change is the long-run driver.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Sustainability is just environmental." It is also economic and social.
- "Big farms aren't sustainable." Some are; size is not the only factor.
- "Indigenous farming is small-scale." Many initiatives are commercial scale.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why "profitable but eroding soil" isn't sustainable.* Short-term profit without protecting soil reduces long-term productivity.
- *Why mental health belongs in sustainability.* Farmer wellbeing affects safety, productivity and family.
- *Aboriginal-led benefit.* Long-term land care; integrated practice; community connection.
- *Climate-adaptation step.* Drought-resistant varieties; irrigation-efficiency investment; insurance.
- *Short summary of Goulburn Valley orchard.* Combines water-efficient irrigation, diversified crops and cooperative marketing with Fair Work employment and Yorta Yorta partnership — all three dimensions reinforce each other.
