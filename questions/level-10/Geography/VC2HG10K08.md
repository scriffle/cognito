# VC2HG10K08 — Geography

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HG10K08. The impacts on places of people's travel, recreational or cultural choices, and how these can be managed, including those impacting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and their Country and Place.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **impacts on places** of people's **travel, recreation and cultural choices**, and **how these can be managed**, including impacts on **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples** and **Country and Place**.
- You are learning to read tourism and recreation as geographic forces.

By the end of this you should be able to:

- Identify three impacts of tourism on places.

- Discuss management options that respect First Nations leadership.

## Core content

**1. Why tourism reshapes places.** Visitors bring money, jobs and infrastructure but also crowds, waste, water demand and cultural pressure. Places change physically and socially. *Quick check:* Why does a small town's character shift when tourism grows fast?

**2. Recreational choices.** Hiking, four-wheel driving, surfing, festivals and event tourism all leave footprints. Some sites can absorb visitors; others have low **carrying capacity**. *Quick check:* What is one sign that a popular trail is exceeding its carrying capacity?

**3. Cultural choices.** Cultural tourism — art, food, heritage and First Nations experiences — is a fast-growing sector. Done well, it supports communities and understanding. Done badly, it commodifies culture without consent. *Quick check:* What is the difference between cultural tourism and cultural appropriation?

**4. Impacts on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Country.** Visitor traffic on sacred sites; damage to rock art; disruption of cultural practice; pressure on water and land. Also: opportunities for community-led tourism, ranger programs and economic development. *Quick check:* Why was the climb at Uluru closed in 2019?

**5. Worked example: the Great Ocean Road.** Twelve Apostles attracts millions of visitors a year. Pressures: erosion from coastal viewing platforms, traffic congestion, sewage, parking, biodiversity stress. Responses: visitor caps, ranger investment, Wadawurrung and Eastern Maar partnerships, signage in language, and infrastructure upgrades. *Follow the thinking:* one icon site requires multi-agency, multi-cultural management.

**6. Management tools.** Visitor caps; ranger programs; boardwalks and rerouting; permits; visitor education; revenue-sharing with Traditional Owners; co-management or sole-Indigenous-management of national parks. *Quick check:* Why is revenue-sharing important?

**7. Free, prior and informed consent.** Decisions about cultural sites need consent from Traditional Owners, ideally early and ongoing. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples sets this principle. *Quick check:* What does "free, prior and informed consent" mean in plain English?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Great Ocean Road; Grampians (Gariwerd); Phillip Island; Wilsons Promontory. Each has Traditional Owner partnerships and visitor-management plans. Parks Victoria publishes joint-management arrangements.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wadawurrung, Eastern Maar, Gunaikurnai, Dja Dja Wurrung and Jardwadjali Peoples lead joint management on parts of Country. Cultural tourism enterprises like Brambuk (Gariwerd) and Worn Gundidj operate with community ownership. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant and international tourists shape the market: Chinese, Indian, US, UK and New Zealand are leading inbound markets. Multilingual signage and cultural translation matter.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Tourism is always good." It can damage sites and communities.
- "Closing sites is anti-tourism." It can protect future visiting.
- "Indigenous tourism is just culture-as-product." Many enterprises are community-owned and educational.
- "Visitors won't change behaviour." Good design and education work.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Small-town character shift.* Housing costs rise, services shift to visitors, locals can be priced out.
2. *Trail exceeding capacity.* Widening, erosion, braiding off-track, litter, wildlife displacement.
3. *Cultural tourism vs appropriation.* Tourism is consented and shared on community terms; appropriation takes without consent or context.
4. *Uluru climb closure.* Long-standing Anangu request; safety; cultural respect; new tourism focus.
5. *Why revenue-sharing matters.* Recognises Traditional Owner authority and funds community priorities.
6. *Free, prior and informed consent.* Decisions require advance, voluntary, well-informed agreement.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Twelve Apostles weekend.* Parking, viewing-platform crowding, waste management.
2. *Surf Coast Shire priorities.* Visitor caps; multilingual visitor education; Traditional Owner partnership funding.
3. *Visitor-cap proposal.* Carrying-capacity data; equity of access; revenue protection.
4. *Closed-sites debate.* For: protection. Against: education and economy. Both sides accept Traditional Owner authority.
5. *Gariwerd excursion.* Step 1: book with Brambuk. Step 2: prepare students on protocols. Step 3: follow rangers' directions and avoid restricted areas.
