# VC2HG10S01 — Geography (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Develop a range of questions for a geographical inquiry related to a phenomenon, issue or challenge.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Geography.
- **Big ideas:** Good questions drive good inquiry. Geographic questions probe space, place, scale and change. Questions evolve.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop a range of questions** for a **geographical inquiry**.
- You should be able to design questions that open inquiry rather than close it.

## Core content

A strong **inquiry question** is open, specific, geographic and answerable with available evidence. Use three **levels**: **descriptive** (what, where, when, how much); **analytical** (why, how, what links to what); **evaluative** (so what, what should be done, by whom).

Frame questions with geographic concepts — space, place, environment, interconnection, change, sustainability, scale. Big inquiry questions branch into **subsidiary questions** answerable through data, fieldwork, interviews and maps.

**Worked example: Otway bushfire risk.** Inquiry: How is bushfire risk changing in the Otway Ranges and what management responses are working? Descriptive: How has fire frequency changed since 2000? Analytical: What drivers explain it? Evaluative: Which responses, including cultural burning, have improved outcomes?

**Worked example: local wellbeing.** Inquiry: How does wellbeing vary across your council area and why? Subsidiary: SEIFA scores; services; interventions.

As evidence arrives, narrow or widen questions, drop dead ends and add branches. Keep a "questions log" alongside data.

**Cultural context.** Inquiry on Country requires respectful questions and Traditional Owner permission; AIATSIS GERAIS guidelines apply. Diverse-community inquiry benefits from multilingual design and culturally informed interviewing. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Good questions open inquiry.
- Use the three levels.
- Frame with geographic concepts.
- Refine as you go.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Yes/no questions are fine." They rarely drive inquiry.
- "One question is enough." Strong inquiries are layered.
- "Questions cannot change." They should evolve with evidence.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why "Is climate change bad?" fails.* Yes/no; vague; not geographic; not answerable from one place.
- *Reframe housing with interconnection.* "How is housing in Melbourne interconnected with migration, planning and global investment?"
- *Bay subsidiary.* What is the trend in seagrass extent over 20 years?
- *Interview ethics.* Informed consent; cultural safety; data storage.
- *Short summary of question design.* Open, layered, concept-framed and refined with evidence makes geographic questions powerful.
