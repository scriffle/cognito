# VC2HC10S04 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC10S04. Evaluate how the principles of justice (fairness, equality and access) are achieved through legal institutions and processes.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Evaluating democratic institutions and systems).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Justice has three working principles in Australian thinking.
  - Achievement is uneven across institutions, regions and communities.
  - Evaluation must compare to specific evidence, not slogans.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Ethical Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate** how the **principles of justice** (**fairness, equality and access**) are **achieved** through legal institutions and processes.
- You are learning to grade the working of the legal system against named principles.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **fairness**, **equality** and **access** in your own words.
- Identify three legal institutions and one principle each emphasises.
- Evaluate a case or process against the three principles.
- Identify one current reform or initiative that targets a gap.

## Core content

**1. Fairness.** **Fairness** is about treating like cases alike and providing due process. It includes the right to be heard, the right to challenge evidence and the right to a reasoned decision. *Quick check:* Why does "the same rule applied differently" still risk being unfair?

**2. Equality.** **Equality** is about applying the law without privileging some over others. It includes equality before the law in formal terms and substantive equality in practice (which may require different supports to reach the same access). *Quick check:* What is the difference between formal and substantive equality?

**3. Access.** **Access** is the practical ability to use the system: cost, language, distance, time, complexity. Without access, rights become theoretical. *Quick check:* Which barrier to access is most often discussed in Victoria?

**4. Worked example: a Victorian tenancy dispute at VCAT.** Fairness: clear procedures and the right to present a case. Equality: the same rules apply to landlords and tenants in principle. Access: lower cost than higher courts; interpreter services; online options. Gaps: short notice periods can disadvantage tenants without legal support; some areas lack legal-aid coverage. *Follow the thinking:* identify institution → score each principle → identify gaps → propose action.

**5. Legal-aid and community legal centres.** Victoria Legal Aid means-tests eligibility. Community legal centres extend access through pro-bono partnerships and community programs. Funding shortfalls regularly leave some people unrepresented. *Quick check:* Why is means-testing essential and limiting at the same time?

**6. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander justice.** The Koori Court applies culturally responsive practices within Magistrates' Court jurisdiction. The Victorian Aboriginal Legal Service represents Aboriginal Victorians. Overrepresentation in custody remains a justice issue addressed through Closing the Gap and reform programs. *Quick check:* Why is overrepresentation an evaluation question, not just a statistic?

**7. Reforms targeting gaps.** Examples include: expansion of community legal centres; specialist family-violence courts; restorative-justice options; technology-enabled VCAT hearings; therapeutic jurisprudence in mental-health and drug courts. *Quick check:* What is one limitation of technology-enabled hearings for justice principles?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's Office of Public Prosecutions, Victoria Legal Aid, VCAT, Federation of Community Legal Centres, the Victorian Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission, the Koori Court and the Victorian Law Reform Commission together shape justice in Victoria.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Victorian Aboriginal Legal Service, the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria, the Marram-Ngala Ganbu Koori Family Hearing Day in the Children's Court, and treaty negotiations all bring First Nations perspectives. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Court interpreter services, multilingual legal-aid clinics, and Community Justice Centres support access for non-English-speaking communities.

## Memorable takeaways
- Three principles: fairness, equality, access.
- Formal and substantive both count.
- Access turns rights into reality.
- Evaluate against evidence, not slogans.
- Reform targets specific gaps.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Same rules = fair." Same rules can fail substantive equality.
- "Lower cost = full access." Cost is one barrier; language and time also matter.
- "Statistics speak for themselves." They show patterns; evaluation requires interpretation.
- "Specialist courts undermine equality." Or they extend substantive equality; depends on design.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Same rule, different application.* Differential interpretation, inconsistent enforcement, or different access to representation can produce unfair outcomes.
2. *Formal vs substantive equality.* Formal: same rules for all. Substantive: same access and outcomes in practice, sometimes requiring different supports.
3. *Most-discussed barrier.* Cost of legal representation; legal aid funding limits.
4. *Why means-testing is essential and limiting.* It targets help to those most in need; it excludes others who still cannot afford private representation.
5. *Why overrepresentation is an evaluation question.* It signals systemic issues that justice institutions should address as part of their work.
6. *Limitation of technology-enabled hearings.* Some participants face digital-access or privacy barriers; complex cases may need in-person hearings.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Court verdict story.* Note whether procedures were followed (fairness), whether parties had similar resources (equality), and whether the case was accessible (access).
2. *Wyndham Justice Forum.* A duty lawyer; a community-legal-centre solicitor; a person with lived experience of the system.
3. *Community legal-worker checks.* Eligibility for legal aid; language support needs; safety concerns (e.g., family violence).
4. *Online VCAT debate.* Advantages: cheaper, accessible from regional areas. Concerns: digital divide, privacy of home environments.
5. *Multilingual family eviction.* Step 1: contact a multilingual community legal centre. Step 2: request an interpreter at any hearing. Step 3: ensure the principles of fairness, equality and access are flagged in the application.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Initiative: Koori Court at Shepparton.* Fairness: respects culturally responsive procedures. Equality: extends substantive equality. Access: requires guilty plea and specific eligibility, which limits use. Strength: lower reoffending evidence. Gap: limited geographic coverage. Recommendation: expand to more locations with community partnership. Sources cited.
