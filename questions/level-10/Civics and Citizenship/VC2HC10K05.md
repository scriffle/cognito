## Core content

**1. Jurisdiction.** **Jurisdiction** is the authority of a court to hear and decide certain cases, defined by the type of case (criminal, civil, family, administrative) and the seriousness or value. *Quick check:* Why does a court need to be empowered before it can hear a case?

**2. Victorian court hierarchy.** Magistrates' Court (summary offences, smaller civil claims). County Court (more serious indictable offences, larger civil claims). Supreme Court (most serious matters, appeals from lower courts). Court of Appeal (within Supreme Court). The High Court of Australia is the final appeal and the only court that can interpret the Constitution. *Quick check:* Why is there a hierarchy?

**3. Tribunals.** A **tribunal** is a specialised body that resolves disputes more cheaply and quickly than a court. VCAT handles tenancy, consumer, planning, guardianship and human-rights matters in Victoria. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal at federal level reviews government decisions. *Quick check:* Why are tribunals attractive for everyday disputes?

**4. Roles at a criminal trial.** The **judge** runs the trial, applies the law, and sentences. The **prosecution** (Office of Public Prosecutions or police prosecutors) presents the case for the state. The **defence** (barristers and solicitors) represents the accused. A **jury** of 12 (in serious indictable trials) decides on the facts. *Quick check:* Why is the jury's role limited to facts, not law?

**5. Worked example: a serious assault trial.** The accused is charged. They are represented by a lawyer (or self-represented if they choose). The prosecution presents evidence and witnesses. The defence cross-examines and presents its own evidence. The judge gives directions on law to the jury. The jury deliberates and returns a verdict. If guilty, the judge sentences. The accused can appeal. *Follow the thinking:* charge → trial → verdict → sentence → appeal.

**6. Rights of the accused.** Australian law protects: the presumption of innocence; the right to silence; the right to legal representation; the right to a fair and timely hearing; the right to challenge evidence; the right to appeal. *Quick check:* Why does presumption of innocence sit at the centre of fair trials?

**7. Rights of victims.** Victims have rights to: be treated respectfully; be informed of the progress of their case; provide a victim-impact statement at sentencing; access support services; and, in some cases, restitution or compensation. Victims' rights have grown since the 1970s. *Quick check:* Why must rights of victims and accused both be protected?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's Office of Public Prosecutions handles serious criminal cases. Victoria Legal Aid supports those who cannot afford lawyers. The Victorian Victims of Crime Commissioner advocates for victims. VCAT runs accessible tribunal hearings.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Koori Court is a culturally responsive division of the Magistrates' Court for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander defendants in specific conditions, with Elder and respected-person involvement. The Victorian Aboriginal Legal Service represents Aboriginal Victorians in courts. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Court interpreter services and multilingual legal-aid clinics in Melbourne's south-east and west support accused and victims from non-English-speaking backgrounds. The Federation of Community Legal Centres connects many of these services.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Judges sentence based on emotion." No. Judges follow legislation and precedent; victim-impact statements inform but do not override these.
- "If you are charged, you must be guilty." No. The accused is presumed innocent.
- "Juries decide the law." No. Juries decide facts; judges decide law.
- "Tribunals are not real courts." They are different but legitimate, with rights of appeal where appropriate.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why a court needs jurisdiction.* Without authority granted by law, its decisions are not binding.
2. *Why a hierarchy.* Cases of different seriousness need different levels; appeals correct errors.
3. *Why are tribunals attractive.* Cheaper, faster, less formal, often suitable for everyday disputes.
4. *Why jury decides facts, not law.* Lay people are well placed to weigh evidence; legal interpretation is the judge's specialised role.
5. *Why presumption of innocence sits at the centre.* It protects against wrongful conviction and forces the state to prove its case.
6. *Why protect both accused and victim rights.* Fair trials require fairness to both. Protecting one set of rights does not negate the other.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *News verdict story.* Likely County or Supreme Court (for serious cases). Jury found facts. The accused had the presumption of innocence and the right to legal representation.
2. *Whitehorse legal-information night.* Victorian court tour; Q&A with a duty lawyer; a moot-court demonstration with student volunteers.
3. *Courts vs tribunals.* Tribunals are usually less formal, can be navigated without a lawyer, and focus on specific subject areas. Courts can hear a wider range of cases and apply broader powers.
4. *Jury trial debate.* For: community involvement and legitimacy. Against: complex cases may strain juries; jury bias concerns. Both sides can be addressed through targeted reform.
5. *Vietnamese-speaking witness.* Court interpreter; written information in Vietnamese; community legal-centre support for navigating the process.
