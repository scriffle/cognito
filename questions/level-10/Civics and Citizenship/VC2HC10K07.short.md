# VC2HC10K07 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** How Australia's international legal obligations shape lawmaking and government policies in Australia, including those relating to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, and the issues related to the application of these obligations.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Sign, ratify, implement. Treaty needs domestic law to bind individuals. Obligations and application can drift apart.

## What you are learning
- You are learning how Australia's **international legal obligations** shape **lawmaking and policy**, including for **First Nations peoples**.
- You should be able to read how external commitments meet domestic decisions.

## Core content

Australia signs and ratifies **treaties** through the executive but binds individuals only when parliament passes **implementing legislation** (dualist system). Major treaties: ICCPR (1966), ICESCR (1966), Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), CERD (1965), Paris Agreement (2015). Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander rights are addressed in CERD, UNDRIP (2007, endorsed by Australia in 2009) and the ILO Convention 169 (not ratified by Australia). UNDRIP is not legally binding but informs policy.

Obligations and application can diverge: political will, federal-state complications, conflicts with existing law and disagreements about implementation all matter. Australia reports periodically to UN bodies; civil society submits shadow reports.

**Worked example.** Racial Discrimination Act and Mabo. Australia ratified CERD in 1975 and passed the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth). In Mabo v Queensland (No. 2) (1992), the High Court used racial-equality principles in recognising native title. The Native Title Act 1993 (Cth) followed.

**Cultural context.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria engages with international Indigenous rights frameworks. AIATSIS publishes guidance on UNDRIP. Treaty negotiations in Victoria connect international principles with domestic process. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Sign, ratify, implement: three steps.
- Treaty needs domestic law to bind individuals.
- UNDRIP informs without binding.
- Reporting drives reform.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Signing means binding inside Australia." No. Implementation in domestic law is needed.
- "International law overrides Australian law." Generally no, unless implemented.
- "Endorsement equals ratification." No. Endorsement is non-binding.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why separate signing from implementation?* It preserves parliamentary sovereignty.
- *Australian law implementing a treaty.* Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (CERD); Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (CEDAW).
- *Why does UNDRIP matter without legal force?* It signals policy direction and provides a framework for advocacy and reporting.
- *Why is periodic reporting useful?* It creates a recurring forum to identify gaps and prompt government responses.
- *Short summary of CERD → RDA → Mabo → Native Title Act.* International obligation, then statute, then court application, then further legislation, all working together.
