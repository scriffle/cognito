## Core content

**1. Strategy vs tactic.** A **strategy** is the overall plan: what you want, who to influence, over what timeline. A **tactic** is the specific action: petition, rally, letter, meeting. Strategies use multiple tactics together. *Quick check:* Why is "we will protest" a tactic, not a strategy?

**2. Theory of change.** A **theory of change** explains how your actions will lead to your goal: "If we do A, then B will happen, which leads to the change C." Naming the theory lets you test and adjust. *Quick check:* Why does writing the theory down beat keeping it in your head?

**3. Methods across scales.** Local: council consultations, neighbourhood groups, school SRC. State: state MPs, peak interest groups, state inquiries. National: federal MPs, large NGOs, parliamentary committees. Global: international NGOs, UN bodies, transnational campaigns. Many issues require working across scales. *Quick check:* Why are coordinated multi-scale efforts often more effective?

**4. Worked example: a Year 10 student campaign on school food.** Goal: healthier and more affordable food in Victorian secondary schools. Strategy: build school-level support, push state-level inquiry submission, coordinate with VicSRC. Tactics: peer survey, school-council meeting, parent newsletter, state MP visit, op-ed in a local paper. Theory of change: if peer and parent voices align, plus formal submission, the state may include the issue in the next student-wellbeing review. Evaluation criteria: reach, response from decision-makers, outcomes. *Follow the thinking:* goal → strategy → tactics → theory of change → evaluation.

**5. Evaluating participation.** Common criteria: reach (how many engaged), response (was the recipient affected), outcomes (what changed), sustainability (can the effort continue). *Quick check:* Which criterion is hardest to measure quickly?

**6. Adjusting on the fly.** Participation is iterative. If a tactic does not work, drop it. If a coalition cracks, repair it. If the issue shifts, re-frame. *Quick check:* Why is "stick to the plan no matter what" usually bad strategy?

**7. Personal sustainability.** Long-term participation needs balance: rotate roles, share the load, take breaks, celebrate small wins. Burnout is a common failure mode for student campaigns. *Quick check:* What is one habit that protects against burnout?

## Memorable takeaways

- Write the theory of change.
- Multi-scale beats single-scale.
- Evaluate to improve.
- Sustain by sharing the load.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one Victorian or Australian issue. Design a six-week multi-scale strategy with at least four tactics. Predict outcomes against three criteria and identify two risks. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Single-tactic wins." Real campaigns combine tactics.
- "Bigger means better." Sometimes a small, well-targeted action moves more than a large unfocused one.
- "If we follow the plan, we will win." Reality changes; adjust the plan.
- "Burnout is personal failure." It is a systemic risk to manage.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"Protest" as tactic.* It does not name the goal, audience, theory of change or what else accompanies it.
2. *Writing down the theory of change.* Forces you to be explicit, allows others to test it, and reveals missing assumptions.
3. *Multi-scale advantages.* Different decision-makers respond to different pressures; coordinated action shows breadth and depth.
4. *Hardest criterion to measure quickly.* Outcomes; many take months or years to appear.
5. *"Stick to the plan no matter what" as bad strategy.* Context changes; rigidity wastes effort and signals you are not listening.
6. *Habit against burnout.* Rotate roles; set a fixed weekly time; celebrate small wins; share the workload with allies.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Sustainable-uniform campaign.* Local (school council, parent body), state (school suppliers' standards, state education department). Tactics: peer survey, supplier meeting, parent newsletter, media op-ed.
2. *Casey youth-engagement evaluation.* Survey of participants; analysis of decisions influenced; longitudinal tracking of continued involvement.
3. *Theory of change.* If we mobilise X people and engage decision-maker Y with evidence Z, then Y will publicly commit to action A within timeframe T.
4. *Economic boycotts debate.* For: legitimate consumer pressure. Against: affects workers, complex effects. Both sides can be addressed with targeted, transparent campaigns.
5. *Pacific community climate negotiation participation.* Step 1: coordinate with Pacific NGOs already in UN spaces. Step 2: equip community spokespeople with story and data. Step 3: feed inputs into Australian delegation through MPs and DFAT.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: increased public-transport access in Melbourne's outer suburbs.* Strategy: build a multi-school coalition, attract media, secure two state-MP meetings, submit to state inquiry. Tactics: peer survey, op-ed, photo-essay, MP visit. Outcomes (reach, response, change): expected reach 500 students, response from two MPs, possible inclusion in the next state transport plan. Risks: holiday timing reduces reach; coalition fatigue. Sources cited.
