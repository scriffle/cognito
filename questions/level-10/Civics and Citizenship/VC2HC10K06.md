## Core content

**1. Sources of rights in Australia.** Australia protects rights from four main sources: the Constitution (express and implied), common law (judge-made principles), statutes (Commonwealth, State and Territory legislation), and international treaties (which need to be implemented in domestic law). *Quick check:* Why does Australia have rights from multiple sources rather than one?

**2. Constitutional rights.** **Express rights** in the Constitution include freedom of religion (s 116), trial by jury for certain federal offences (s 80), and acquisition of property on just terms (s 51(xxxi)). **Implied rights** are drawn from the text and structure: the High Court recognises an implied freedom of political communication. *Quick check:* What is one limitation on freedom of religion under s 116?

**3. Common-law rights.** Common law protects rights such as habeas corpus (cannot be detained arbitrarily), right to a fair trial, and protection of property. These principles developed across centuries of court decisions in Britain and Australia. *Quick check:* Why does common-law protection work alongside, not instead of, statute?

**4. Statutory rights.** Parliaments create rights through legislation: the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth), the Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth), the Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth), the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth), and Victoria's Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006). State Equal Opportunity Acts also create rights. *Quick check:* Why are statutes more easily updated than the Constitution?

**5. Worked example: implied freedom of political communication.** In Australian Capital Television v Commonwealth (1992) and subsequent cases, the High Court interpreted the Constitution to imply a freedom of political communication. It is not a personal right; it is a restriction on government action. Parliaments still legislate around it (e.g., defamation laws, election rules). *Follow the thinking:* text and structure → court interpretation → applied to specific laws.

**6. Role of parliaments.** Parliaments create rights, change them and balance them with other interests. They also create complaint and review bodies (the AHRC, VEOHRC). Parliamentary debates and committees often consider human-rights compatibility. *Quick check:* Why is "parliament can both create and limit rights" not a contradiction?

**7. Role of the High Court.** The High Court interprets the Constitution, applies common law, and reviews legislation for constitutional validity. Decisions like Mabo (1992) on native title and Roach v Electoral Commissioner (2007) on prisoner voting reshape rights through interpretation. *Quick check:* Why does High Court interpretation matter even when the Constitution's text has not changed?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006) requires public authorities to consider human rights and gives the Supreme Court power to declare laws incompatible with rights (although the law remains unless amended).
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Native title (Mabo, 1992) and the Native Title Act 1993 (Cth) are major rights developments. The 1967 referendum amended s 51(xxvi) to allow Commonwealth laws for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Treaty negotiations in Victoria are exploring further constitutional and statutory recognition. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Anti-discrimination statutes have shaped public life across many migrant communities. The AHRC and VEOHRC receive complaints and educate the public in multiple languages.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Australia has a Bill of Rights." It does not at federal level.
- "Implied rights are weaker." They have been used to strike down laws.
- "Rights are absolute." They are usually balanced with other interests.
- "Common law is obsolete." It continues to develop alongside statute.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Multiple sources of rights.* History (no single Bill of Rights), federalism (rights at both Commonwealth and State levels), evolution (rights develop through cases and statutes).
2. *Limit on freedom of religion under s 116.* It applies to Commonwealth legislation only and does not cover all conceivable religion-related issues; the High Court has read it relatively narrowly.
3. *Why common law works alongside statute.* Common law fills gaps and develops general principles; statute provides specific rules. Together they cover more ground.
4. *Why statutes are easier to update than the Constitution.* Statutes need parliamentary passage; constitutional change needs a referendum with double majority.
5. *Why parliament can both create and limit rights.* Rights compete with each other and with public interests; parliament balances them through legislation.
6. *Why interpretation matters even without text change.* New facts and contexts force courts to apply old text in new ways; this can recognise rights without rewriting the Constitution.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Right to free speech".* Australia has an implied freedom of political communication, not a general right to free speech. Defamation, hate-speech and other laws limit speech.
2. *Whitehorse staff training.* Decisions must consider rights of affected people; reasons should be given; recordkeeping should support review.
3. *Bill rights checks.* Compatibility with the Charter; any limits on rights must be reasonable and proportionate; affected groups have been consulted.
4. *Federal Bill of Rights debate.* For: clearer protection and consistency. Against: shifts power from parliament to courts. Both sides can be addressed through statutory bills of rights that respect parliamentary sovereignty.
5. *Discrimination response.* Rely on the Racial Discrimination Act, Victorian Equal Opportunity Act, and Charter. Go to VEOHRC, AHRC, or a community legal centre. Document incidents in detail.
