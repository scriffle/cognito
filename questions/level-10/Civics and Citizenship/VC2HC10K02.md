## Core content

**1. What policy is.** **Policy** is a government's plan to address an issue. It can take the form of legislation, regulation, programs or spending decisions. Policy is broader than law. *Quick check:* What is one example of a policy that does not need new law?

**2. The policy cycle.** A common model: agenda-setting → policy development → decision → implementation → evaluation → revision. The cycle is rarely tidy; stages overlap and loop back. *Quick check:* Why is "cycle" a better word than "pipeline"?

**3. Political parties.** Parties bring ideas onto the agenda, develop platforms, and use party discipline to pass legislation in parliament. Inside a party, factions and the parliamentary leadership shape priorities. *Quick check:* What is one way internal party debate shapes policy?

**4. Interest groups and lobbying.** Interest groups (unions, business councils, NGOs, professional bodies) bring expertise, mobilise supporters and make submissions. Registered lobbyists meet officials and ministers. Their input is legal and regulated, but raises questions about access and influence. *Quick check:* Why does Australia have a register of lobbyists?

**5. The media.** Media shape the agenda by deciding what to cover, how to frame it and whom to quote. Public broadcasters (ABC, SBS), commercial media, independent and ethnic media each carry different perspectives. Social media has expanded reach and risk. *Quick check:* What is "agenda-setting" by the media?

**6. Worked example: a federal climate-policy package.** Step 1: agenda-setting (election platforms, IPCC reports, advocacy campaigns). Step 2: development (departments, advisory committees, party rooms). Step 3: decision (cabinet, both houses of parliament, Senate amendments). Step 4: implementation (departments and agencies design regulations, allocate funding, monitor compliance). Step 5: evaluation (Auditor-General, parliamentary committees, public reaction). Step 6: revision in the next cycle. *Follow the thinking:* identify the stage → name the actors → trace one decision.

**7. Why implementation matters.** A bill passing parliament does not guarantee the policy works. Implementation depends on regulations, staffing, IT systems, partnerships and time. Many headlines about "failed policy" are really about implementation challenges. *Quick check:* What is one Australian example where implementation determined success or failure?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian Department of Premier and Cabinet leads state-level policy coordination. The Victorian Auditor-General reviews implementation. Local councils have their own policy cycles for community planning.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander policy in Victoria is shaped through the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria, Closing the Gap targets and Traditional Owner corporations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural advisory bodies and faith-based organisations shape policy on settlement, education, health and language services. Multilingual consultation materials support participation across Melbourne's diverse communities.

## Memorable takeaways
- Policy is broader than law.
- The cycle has many stages.
- Parties, groups, media and parliament all play.
- Implementation makes or breaks policy.
- Evaluation feeds the next cycle.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Policy = law." Policy is broader; it includes regulations, programs and spending.
- "Once a bill passes, the policy is done." Implementation often takes years.
- "Media just reports." Media also shapes which issues become urgent.
- "Lobbyists are unrestricted." There are registers, codes of conduct and disclosure rules.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Policy without new law.* Funding decisions, regulatory changes within existing law, or advisory committees.
2. *Why "cycle".* Stages loop back; revisions and evaluations feed into new policy work.
3. *Internal party debate.* Caucus or party-room debates can shift policy positions, soften or harden them before going public.
4. *Why a lobbyist register.* Transparency: the public can see who is paid to influence government.
5. *Agenda-setting by media.* The choice of what to cover (or not) determines which issues become priorities for the public and politicians.
6. *Implementation example.* The COVID-19 vaccine rollout: legislation existed, but logistics, supply and communication determined results.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Announced policy.* Drafting and consultation; passage through parliament (if law is required); regulations and resourcing for implementation.
2. *Greater Geelong youth-engagement.* Agenda: youth groups and councils. Development: department staff, school principals. Decision: councillors. Implementation: schools and community organisations.
3. *State mental-health policy.* Departmental strategy documents; Auditor-General reports; Hansard debates on relevant bills.
4. *Paid-lobbying debate.* For: provides expertise and access. Against: privileges those who can pay. Both sides agree on disclosure rules and access transparency.
5. *Multicultural settlement policy.* Agenda: community submissions to inquiries. Development: meetings with department officials. Decision: minister and cabinet. Implementation: settlement service providers and councils.
