## Core content

**1. Why governments act internationally.** Australia is part of a connected world: trade, migration, security, climate and health all cross borders. The Australian Government represents the country in agreements, embassies and crises. *Quick check:* Why is climate change a national and international issue at once?

**2. Departments and people.** The Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT) leads diplomacy. The Department of Defence handles defence cooperation. The Australian Border Force handles immigration and customs. Embassies and consulates abroad support citizens. The Prime Minister and the Foreign Minister are the most visible figures. *Quick check:* Who heads Australia's foreign-affairs efforts?

**3. Bilateral and multilateral relations.** **Bilateral** relations are with one other country (e.g., Australia–Indonesia). **Multilateral** relations are with many at once (e.g., the United Nations, ASEAN, Pacific Islands Forum). Both matter. *Quick check:* What is one advantage of multilateral work?

**4. Worked example: Australia in the Pacific.** Australia partners with Pacific nations on climate adaptation, disaster relief, security and economic development. Examples: the Pacific Islands Forum; the Pacific Australia Labour Mobility scheme; humanitarian responses (cyclones, tsunamis). Pacific neighbours raise concerns: Australian climate policy, deep-sea mining, and previous Pacific Solution detention agreements. The relationship is close and contested. *Follow the thinking:* identify the partner → list cooperation areas → name tensions → describe Australia's responses.

**5. Asia engagement.** Australia engages closely with Japan, South Korea, Indonesia, Vietnam, China, India and others through trade (ASEAN, RCEP, free-trade agreements), security (AUKUS, the Quad) and education (Colombo Plan, New Colombo Plan). Engagement is layered and not always consistent. *Quick check:* Why is Australia's relationship with China both important and complicated?

**6. International law and norms.** Australia signs treaties on human rights, environment, trade and security. The treaty-making power belongs to the federal executive; some treaties also require domestic legislation. *Quick check:* Why does an international treaty need to be implemented in domestic law to bind individuals?

**7. Aid, defence, sanctions.** Australia delivers humanitarian aid and development assistance, mainly through DFAT. It maintains a defence force and contributes to international coalitions. It applies sanctions on individuals and entities in response to violations of international law. *Quick check:* What is one tool other than military force that governments use abroad?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's economy depends on Asia-Pacific trade (education, agriculture, services). Many Victorian universities have international student bodies from the region. The Asialink Centre at the University of Melbourne supports public engagement.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have their own connections across the region, especially with Torres Strait communities in Papua New Guinea and Pacific Indigenous networks. The UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues is one international forum where Australian First Nations voices are heard. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Diaspora communities in Melbourne (Indian, Chinese, Vietnamese, Sri Lankan, Filipino, Indonesian and more) maintain personal and professional ties to home countries, shaping how Australia is seen in the region.

## Memorable takeaways
- Australia is a regional and global actor.
- Bilateral and multilateral both matter.
- Diplomacy is more than treaties.
- Aid, trade and security travel together.
- Domestic and international are linked.

## Stretch yourself

## Watch out for these traps
- "Foreign policy is unconnected from daily life." Trade, climate, migration and education are all touched.
- "Diplomacy is just polite letters." It includes economics, defence, intelligence and culture.
- "Aid is charity." It is also strategic and reciprocal; recipients have agency in setting priorities.
- "Australia is only a small player." Its regional weight is significant, especially in the Pacific.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Climate as national and international.* National: emissions, infrastructure, jobs. International: treaties, shared seas and air, refugees, regional partnerships.
2. *Foreign-affairs lead.* The Foreign Minister and Prime Minister, supported by DFAT.
3. *Advantage of multilateral.* Shared rules and burdens; small states get a louder voice; collective action on global problems.
4. *China relationship complications.* Major trading partner; strategic competitor; significant Chinese-Australian community; complex on human rights and regional security.
5. *Why treaties need domestic law.* Treaties bind the country in international law; domestic law makes the obligations enforceable against individuals in Australian courts.
6. *Non-military tool.* Aid, trade incentives, sanctions, diplomacy, cultural exchange.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Aid package.* Humanitarian relief; long-term development; strategic interest; relationship building. Often all four together.
2. *Sister-city event.* Cultural exchange; student exchange; trade-mission briefing.
3. *Australia–Vietnam trade brief.* Free-trade agreements in place; sectoral opportunities; diaspora links; areas of tension or future negotiation.
4. *Humanitarian-visa debate.* Ethical: obligations to people in need. Practical: settlement capacity, social cohesion, processing systems.
5. *Pacific-Island climate advocacy.* Step 1: organise through trusted community networks. Step 2: meet local federal MPs. Step 3: submit to parliamentary committee inquiries on Pacific affairs.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Decision: a Pacific climate-finance commitment.* Partners: Pacific Island countries via the Pacific Islands Forum. Tools: aid (climate finance), treaty (Paris Agreement obligations), diplomacy. Tension: Australian domestic energy policy. Domestic effect: pressure on emissions-reduction policy. Sources cited.
