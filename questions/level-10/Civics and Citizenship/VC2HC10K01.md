## Core content

**1. The Constitution as legal foundation.** The Australian Constitution came into force on 1 January 1901 as part of the Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act (UK). It set up the federation: a Commonwealth government plus the original colonies (now States). It defines parliament, courts and the executive, lists Commonwealth powers, and contains a small set of express rights. *Quick check:* Why is a constitution often called a "rule book" for government?

**2. The federal system.** **Federation** divides power between two levels: Commonwealth and States. Section 51 lists Commonwealth powers (defence, immigration, taxation, foreign affairs). States retain residual powers (most education, health, transport, criminal law). Section 109 resolves clashes: federal law prevails. *Quick check:* Why include two levels of government rather than one?

**3. Democratic institutions named or implied.** The Constitution establishes the Commonwealth parliament (two houses), the Governor-General (representing the King), the High Court, and rules for federal elections. It implies a system of responsible government inherited from Westminster. *Quick check:* What is one democratic value the Constitution names explicitly?

**4. Express and implied rights.** Express rights include freedom of religion (s 116), the right to vote in federal elections (s 41), and the right to trial by jury for some federal offences (s 80). The High Court has recognised an implied freedom of political communication. *Quick check:* Why is "implied" different from "express"?

**5. Worked example: how a referendum works.** A bill proposing constitutional change passes both houses of parliament. After a campaign period, a national vote is held. The change passes only if it wins a **double majority**: a majority of voters nationally, and a majority of voters in a majority of States (at least four of six). The Governor-General then gives royal assent. The change is added to the Constitution. *Follow the thinking:* bill → both houses → national vote → double majority → assent.

**6. Why most referendums fail.** Of the 45 referendum proposals since 1901, only 8 have passed. Reasons include public conservatism about constitutional change, partisan opposition, complexity of proposals and short campaign times. The 1967 referendum (extending Commonwealth power to make laws for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples) and the 1977 referendum (mandatory judicial retirement age) are notable successes. The 2023 Voice referendum did not pass. *Quick check:* Why is "complexity" a real barrier in a referendum campaign?

**7. The Constitution today.** The Constitution evolves through court interpretation (High Court decisions like Mabo, 1992) and political practice, even without textual change. Treaty processes in Victoria add another layer of constitutional thinking. *Quick check:* Why is "the Constitution is fixed" only partly true?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The High Court of Australia, headquartered in Canberra, sits occasionally in Melbourne. The Australian Electoral Commission runs referendums; the Victorian Electoral Commission supports state-level practice.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Nations peoples were not consulted in drafting the Constitution. Section 51(xxvi) (the "races power") was amended by the 1967 referendum to allow Commonwealth laws on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. The Uluru Statement (2017) called for a constitutionally enshrined Voice; the 2023 referendum on this did not pass. Treaty negotiations in Victoria are exploring state-level constitutional change. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Australian citizenship under the Constitution applies regardless of background. Multilingual referendum information from the AEC, including in Vietnamese, Mandarin, Arabic and other languages, supports informed participation by diverse communities.

## Memorable takeaways
- Constitution divides, limits, enables.
- Federation: two levels of government.
- Express rights are few; implied rights exist.
- Double majority is a high bar.
- Most referendums fail.

## Watch out for these traps
- "The Constitution lists all our rights." It contains only a few express rights.
- "Federation means weak central government." Federal powers are extensive and have grown over time.
- "Referendums are won by good arguments alone." They also depend on bipartisan support, campaign resources and public mood.
- "Constitutional change always means rewriting." Interpretation by the High Court also changes how the Constitution works.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"Rule book" for government.* It sets out who does what, who has power, and how disputes are resolved. Without it, institutions would lack a shared foundation.
2. *Why two levels.* Local and state issues need local decision-making; national issues need national coordination. Federalism manages both.
3. *Democratic value named.* The Constitution requires regular elections for both federal houses (e.g., s 28 sets the maximum term for the House of Representatives).
4. *Express vs implied.* Express rights are written in the text. Implied rights are interpreted by the High Court from the structure and text taken together.
5. *Why complexity is a barrier.* Voters reasonably want to understand what they are agreeing to; complex proposals can be hard to explain in a short campaign.
6. *Why "fixed" is only partly true.* The text is hard to change, but court interpretation and political practice evolve.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Friend on "outdated" Constitution.* The Constitution does not list every modern right; it has a few express rights, implied rights, and is interpreted by the High Court. Updating happens through referendum or interpretation, and reform is a current debate.
2. *Manningham referendum information.* What change is proposed; who supports and opposes it; what the double majority means.
3. *Federal system for new migrants.* Commonwealth handles immigration and defence; States handle education and hospitals; many decisions involve both levels.
4. *Easier constitutional change debate.* For: makes reform possible when needed. Against: stability and protection from sudden majorities. Both sides agree informed deliberation matters.
5. *Vietnamese-Australian voter information.* Translated materials from the AEC; community-language radio sessions; trusted-leader briefings.
