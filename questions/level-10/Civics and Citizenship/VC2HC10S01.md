# VC2HC10S01 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC10S01. Develop and evaluate questions to investigate contemporary political, legal and civic issues.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Investigating contemporary civics and citizenship issues).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Strong questions are specific and testable.
  - Evaluation reveals what a question can and cannot answer.
  - Adjusting the question is part of doing research.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Literacy; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **develop and evaluate questions** to investigate **contemporary political, legal and civic issues**.
- You are learning to make sure your question is worth investigating before you start.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **investigable question**, **scope**, **bias-check** and **operationalisation** in your own words.
- Refine a broad topic into three increasingly investigable questions.
- Evaluate one question against five criteria.
- Justify when to change a question and when to keep it.

## Core content

**1. From topic to question.** A topic is general ("voting"). A question is specific ("How does compulsory voting affect turnout in Victorian state elections compared with elections in selected Asia-Pacific countries since 2015?"). Refining narrows place, time, actor and measure. *Quick check:* Why is "compulsory voting" still a topic, not a question?

**2. Five criteria for a strong question.** (1) Specific: clear about place, time, actors and what counts as an answer. (2) Investigable: sources exist and are accessible. (3) Open: allows multiple perspectives. (4) Neutral: avoids loaded wording. (5) Operationalisable: you can define what counts as the variable. *Quick check:* Which criterion is most likely to be overlooked?

**3. Bias-check.** Re-read your question and ask: Does it assume a conclusion? Does it privilege one group? Does it leave another out? Adjust if needed. *Quick check:* Why is a bias-check different from changing your opinion?

**4. Operationalising variables.** **Operationalisation** turns abstract terms into observable measures. "Civic engagement" could be measured by turnout, donations, volunteering hours, or survey responses. Naming the measure lets others test it. *Quick check:* What is one operational measure of "trust in government"?

**5. Worked example: evaluating a question on media trust.** Initial: Are Australians losing trust in media? Specific? No. Investigable? Maybe. Open? Yes. Neutral? Mostly. Operationalisable? Depends on the measure. Refined: How has trust in ABC News, as measured by the Reuters Institute Digital News Report, changed between 2015 and 2024 among Australians aged 18 to 24? Evaluated against five criteria, this scores well on each. *Follow the thinking:* draft → evaluate → refine → re-evaluate.

**6. When to change vs keep a question.** Change when: you cannot find adequate sources; the variable cannot be operationalised; the question has been answered by reputable studies already. Keep and refine when: the question is interesting but vague; sources exist but need triangulating; only one criterion needs work. *Quick check:* Why is "keep refining" often better than "abandon"?

**7. Stakeholder considerations.** Year 10 questions affecting First Nations or other communities should follow ethical and cultural guidance: respect community priorities, cite their own voices, and avoid extracting stories. *Quick check:* Why does community priority shape the research question, not just the method?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Parliament of Victoria research papers, the Victorian Auditor-General reports and Royal Commissions all publish accessible primary sources for student investigation. Local councils and university research centres also publish.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS publishes ethical research guidelines for working with First Nations communities. The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria publishes its own materials. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual community media, ethnic community councils and community legal centres provide voices and statistics often missing from mainstream sources.

## Memorable takeaways
- Topic ≠ question.
- Five criteria sharpen.
- Bias-check before sources.
- Operationalise the variable.
- Refining is part of the work.

## Stretch yourself
Pick a civic issue. Draft a question. Evaluate it on five criteria. Refine and re-evaluate. Justify the final version against the original in 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Specific = narrow." Specific means clear about scope; it can still be substantive.
- "Neutral = no perspective." Neutral framing avoids loaded language; you can still investigate strongly.
- "If sources exist, the question is fine." Check whether the available sources actually fit your question.
- "Indigenous topics are too sensitive to research." They are valid with appropriate ethics and partnership.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *"Compulsory voting" as topic.* It names a subject but lacks place, time, measure and a clear question.
2. *Most overlooked criterion.* Operationalisability: students often pick variables they cannot measure.
3. *Bias-check vs changing opinion.* Bias-check is about the wording and structure of the question; opinion is about your view of the answer.
4. *Operational measure of trust.* Survey responses (e.g., the Australian Election Study); turnout; complaint volumes.
5. *Why "keep refining" beats "abandon".* You retain time invested and learn how to ask better; abandoning is sometimes right but should be a last resort.
6. *Why community priority shapes the question.* If the community is the focus, the question that they consider useful matters most.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Social media and politics".* Refine to: How did Year 10 students in the Victorian electorate of Calwell use Instagram and TikTok to discuss the 2023 referendum, based on a small sample of posts and self-reports?
2. *Brimbank student research.* Council services and youth engagement; transport for school commutes; multicultural-event programming.
3. *Housing question.* Define what counts as "affordable"; specify the time window; identify the data source (REIV, ABS, council).
4. *Sensitive minority research.* Reframe to focus on systemic factors rather than individuals; involve community partners; protect identifiable information.
5. *Wadawurrung research.* Step 1: contact the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: co-design the question. Step 3: ensure outputs benefit the community first.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: youth voter turnout.* Initial: Why do young people not vote? Refined v1: Has youth voter turnout in Victorian federal elections changed since 2010? Refined v2: How has 18–24 voter turnout in three Melbourne federal electorates changed between 2010 and 2024, based on AEC data and student surveys? Justification: more specific, investigable, operationalisable, and open to multiple explanations.
