## Core content

**1. What makes a democracy resilient.** A resilient democracy can absorb shocks (economic, security, public-health) without losing core institutions, rights and trust. Resilience comes from working institutions, broad participation, shared values and the ability to reform. *Quick check:* Why is "no change" not the same as resilience?

**2. Common challenges.** Polarisation; misinformation and disinformation; declining trust in institutions; foreign interference; economic inequality; identity-based exclusion; climate stress on communities; rapid technological change. *Quick check:* Which challenge is most visible to you in your community?

**3. Democratic backsliding.** **Democratic backsliding** is when democratic norms (free elections, free press, independent courts, civil rights) weaken over time, often within an electoral framework. Some countries in the region and globally have shown elements of backsliding. *Quick check:* Why is "still has elections" not enough to mean a country is fully democratic?

**4. Social cohesion.** **Social cohesion** is the sense that "we" includes everyone in the country. The Scanlon Foundation tracks Australian cohesion year by year. Cohesion is challenged by inequality, racism and rapid change; it is supported by shared values, inclusive institutions and trusted community organisations. *Quick check:* What is one Australian programme that explicitly aims to build cohesion?

**5. Worked example: a response to misinformation.** Challenge: false claims spreading online affect public-health and election trust. Responses: media-literacy education in schools; transparent moderation policies on platforms; legal tools (defamation, misinformation laws); strengthening trusted public broadcasters (ABC, SBS); civic-science initiatives. None alone is enough. *Follow the thinking:* identify the challenge → list responses → match scale (individual, community, institution, regulation) → reflect on trade-offs.

**6. Regional and global tools.** Multilateral bodies (UN, OECD), regional groupings (ASEAN, Pacific Islands Forum), international election observation, human-rights monitoring and disaster cooperation all build resilience across borders. *Quick check:* Why is regional cooperation part of democratic resilience?

**7. Reform and renewal.** Democracies that survive long-term keep reforming: extending suffrage, addressing corruption, modernising institutions, recognising First Nations rights. Stagnation invites backsliding; thoughtful reform invites trust. *Quick check:* Why is "reform" a continual rather than one-off task?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Scanlon Foundation, the Victorian Multicultural Commission and the Department of Premier and Cabinet publish annual snapshots of cohesion and resilience. Victoria's Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006) is part of the protection framework.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have demonstrated democratic resilience across generations through continued advocacy, leadership and institutions like the First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural advisory bodies, faith-based organisations and ethnic community councils across Melbourne contribute to cohesion daily. Multilingual public-health and civic communication strengthens trust across communities.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Resilience means no change." It means change without breaking core institutions.
- "Cohesion means unanimity." It means coexisting respectfully across disagreement.
- "All countries are backsliding." Not all; honest analysis avoids broad-brush claims.
- "Tech is the only fix or the only problem." Both are oversimplifications.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "no change" is not resilience.* Stagnant institutions become rigid and lose legitimacy; resilience requires adaptive reform.
2. *Visible challenge.* Often misinformation online; polarisation in public debate; cost-of-living pressures fuelling distrust.
3. *Why "still has elections" is not enough.* Elections can occur in systems with restricted media, judicial interference or persecution of opposition.
4. *Cohesion programme.* The Australian Multicultural Strategy; Reconciliation Action Plans; Welcoming Cities; specific council-level cohesion strategies.
5. *Regional cooperation and democratic resilience.* Shared standards and peer review strengthen each member; isolated democracies are more vulnerable.
6. *Why reform is continual.* Societies change; institutions must adapt; without reform, gaps grow into crises.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Decline" claim.* Cite one specific challenge (e.g., misinformation) and one resilience signal (e.g., high turnout, an independent ABC). Honest analysis acknowledges both.
2. *Greater Dandenong cohesion.* Multilingual community events; school-led intercultural projects; small-grants programme for community organisations.
3. *Misinformation brief.* Identify the type of misinformation; design layered responses (education, platform engagement, regulation); evaluate impact.
4. *Protest movements debate.* For: raise issues that elections can miss; renew civic energy. Against: can polarise; depend on peaceful, lawful conduct. Both sides agree peaceful protest is part of democracy.
5. *Racism concerns.* Step 1: document incidents and report to the VEOHRC or AHRC. Step 2: support affected community members. Step 3: lead a cross-community conversation hosted by trusted community organisations.
