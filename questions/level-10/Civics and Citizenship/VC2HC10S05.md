# VC2HC10S05 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC10S05. Evaluate the power of Australian and international political actors with regard to contemporary issues, and these political actors' connections with institutions.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Evaluating democratic institutions and systems).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Power is held by actors and channelled by institutions.
  - Power can be hard, soft and structural.
  - Connections amplify or constrain individual power.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Intercultural Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate** the **power of Australian and international political actors** on **contemporary issues**, and their **connections with institutions**.
- You are learning to weigh power carefully across actors and contexts.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **hard power**, **soft power**, **structural power** and **institutional connection** in your own words.
- Identify three Australian and three international political actors.
- Evaluate the power of one actor on a specific issue.
- Justify your evaluation against alternative readings.

## Core content

**1. Forms of power.** **Hard power** is direct authority (passing laws, military force, sanctions). **Soft power** is influence through attraction (culture, education, diplomacy, expertise). **Structural power** is the ability to shape the rules and agenda within which others act. *Quick check:* Which form is hardest to see?

**2. Australian actors.** Prime Minister, Premiers, federal and state ministers, MPs, regulators (ACMA, ACCC), the AEC, the High Court, the Reserve Bank of Australia, major banks, large media owners, peak interest groups, civil-society leaders. *Quick check:* Which actor exercises significant structural power?

**3. International actors.** Foreign governments and their leaders, the UN and its agencies (UNESCO, WHO), regional bodies (ASEAN, Pacific Islands Forum), multinational corporations, transnational NGOs (Amnesty, Greenpeace), media networks. *Quick check:* Why are multinational corporations considered political actors?

**4. Institutional connections.** Actors' power often flows through institutions: a minister through a department; a CEO through a corporation; a leader through a party. Strong connections amplify reach; weak connections limit it. *Quick check:* What happens to a leader's power when they lose institutional support?

**5. Worked example: contemporary climate action.** Domestic actors: PM (hard power through cabinet decisions and legislation), state premiers (hard power within state jurisdictions), the Clean Energy Council (soft power on policy), major energy companies (structural power on infrastructure). International actors: UN climate-treaty bodies (soft + structural), the European Union (regulatory rule-making), countries with large emissions (hard power in negotiations). *Follow the thinking:* identify the issue → list actors → assess each form of power → trace through institutions → conclude on relative weight.

**6. Power changes with context.** A foreign minister has more power in trade negotiations than in domestic court rulings. An NGO has more power on the international stage than in cabinet. Evaluate power in context, not in abstract. *Quick check:* Why is "this actor is powerful" usually too simple?

**7. Power and accountability.** All forms of power should be reviewable. Disclosure regimes, freedom-of-information, judicial review, parliamentary committees, free press and elections together provide accountability. Failures in accountability reduce democratic resilience. *Quick check:* Why does accountability of soft power matter?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian Premier and ministers exercise significant power within state jurisdiction. The Victorian Auditor-General and IBAC are accountability institutions. Major Melbourne corporations (banks, media) exercise structural power on national debates.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria exercises authority within its mandate. Traditional Owner corporations exercise power over land-management decisions in their Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural advisory bodies, faith-based organisations and community leaders exercise soft power through advocacy and trust networks. Diaspora communities can amplify international actors' influence through community ties.

## Memorable takeaways
- Hard, soft, structural.
- Power flows through institutions.
- Context shapes weight.
- Accountability runs alongside.
- Evaluate, do not just list.

## Stretch yourself

## Watch out for these traps
- "Power equals office." Not all power flows through formal positions.
- "Soft power is weak." It often shapes the agenda before formal decisions.
- "International actors do not influence Australia." Trade, climate and security show otherwise.
- "Multinational corporations are not political." Their decisions affect public life and policy.

## Try drawing this
A "power-and-institutions web": actors at the centre, connecting lines to institutions, with line thickness showing strength of connection.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Hardest form to see.* Structural power, because it shapes the rules others act within and is often taken for granted.
2. *Significant structural power.* The Reserve Bank of Australia (interest rates and financial system rules); major media owners (which issues become visible).
3. *Multinationals as political.* Their investment, hiring and lobbying choices shape policy and public life.
4. *Leader without institutional support.* Their ability to act declines sharply; soft power may persist but hard power evaporates.
5. *Why "powerful" alone is too simple.* Power varies by issue, time and the specific institutional channel used.
6. *Why soft-power accountability matters.* Hidden influence on agenda-setting can shift policy without scrutiny.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Federal climate target.* PM, Treasurer, ministers (hard); state Premiers and the Clean Energy Council (mixed); affected industry workers and Pacific neighbours (often overlooked).
2. *Yarra advocacy.* State MPs (hard), peak interest groups (soft), media (soft + structural).
3. *Power mapping steps.* List actors; assess each form of power; trace institutional connections and weigh impact on a defined outcome.
4. *Corporations vs governments debate.* For: efficiency, expertise. Against: accountability, democratic legitimacy. Both sides agree that disclosure and antitrust frameworks help.
5. *Pacific community climate advocacy.* Soft power: lived-experience stories shared in Australian and international forums. Structural power: coalition with Australian and Pacific NGOs. Hard power: votes through their families and diaspora ties.
