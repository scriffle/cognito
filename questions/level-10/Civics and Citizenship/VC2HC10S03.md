# VC2HC10S03 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC10S03. Recommend changes to political, legal and/or civic institutions and justify these recommendations.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Evaluating democratic institutions and systems).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Reform proposals need evidence and a plan.
  - Justification beats wish-listing.
  - Costs and trade-offs must be named.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Personal and Social Capability.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **recommend changes** to **political, legal and/or civic institutions** and **justify** these recommendations.
- You are learning to write a reform proposal that someone could actually act on.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **reform**, **justification** and **trade-off** in your own words.
- Outline a recommendation in three parts: what, why and how.
- Identify two stakeholders who would support and two who would oppose.
- Defend your recommendation against a likely objection.

## Core content

**1. What a recommendation looks like.** A **recommendation** is a specific, actionable proposal for change. It names the institution, the change, the rationale and the implementation steps. Vague calls to "do better" are not recommendations. *Quick check:* Why is "do better" not a recommendation?

**2. Three-part structure.** What: name the specific change. Why: state the evidence and reasoning. How: identify steps, actors and timeline. Strong proposals can be read by a decision-maker in five minutes and understood. *Quick check:* What is missing if your proposal lacks the "how" part?

**3. Justification.** **Justification** explains why this change is the right one. Use evidence (data, expert testimony, case studies) and reasoning (the change will produce the intended effect). Anticipate counterarguments. *Quick check:* Why does anticipating objections strengthen a recommendation?

**4. Worked example: a school-level reform.** Recommendation: Victorian secondary schools should provide a structured "Year 10 civics-action week" with options across the four substrands (Government, Laws, Citizenship, Participation). Why: state government strategy emphasises civic engagement, but Year 10 students report uneven access. How: a 5-day program co-designed with VicSRC, optional partner organisations (council, advocacy groups), and a small grant per school. Trade-offs: time away from other subjects; equity of access. *Follow the thinking:* clear what → solid why → specific how → named trade-off.

**5. Trade-offs.** Every reform has costs. List them honestly: time, money, complexity, disruption. Reforms that ignore trade-offs lose credibility and often fail in implementation. *Quick check:* Why is "no trade-offs" almost never true?

**6. Stakeholders.** Identify who benefits, who pays, and who must agree. A recommendation that wins the right supporters and addresses opponents has a better chance. *Quick check:* What is one stakeholder whose support is often forgotten?

**7. Justifying with rigour.** Justification needs precision: cite sources, quote specific numbers, and link the change to the desired outcome. Vague references weaken proposals. *Quick check:* Why does "research shows" usually weaken rather than strengthen?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Law Reform Commission proposes reforms regularly. Royal Commissions in Victoria (e.g., Family Violence, Mental Health) produce recommendation-heavy reports. Local councils invite reform suggestions through public consultations.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria recommends reforms through the treaty process. Reconciliation Australia's Reconciliation Action Plans contain specific commitments. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multicultural advisory bodies (e.g., Victorian Multicultural Commission), ethnic community councils, and faith-based organisations propose reforms on settlement services, education, language access and health.

## Memorable takeaways
- What, why, how.
- Justify with evidence.
- Name trade-offs honestly.
- Identify stakeholders.
- Specific beats vague.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one Victorian or Australian institution. Recommend one change. Justify with at least two sources. Identify two supporters and two opponents. Defend against one likely objection. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More money fixes it." Money helps, but design and delivery matter too.
- "Best practice is enough justification." Cite specific evidence, not just labels.
- "Stakeholders will adjust." Resistance is normal; plan for it.
- "Reform is always good." Some reforms have unintended harms; consider these too.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "do better" is not a recommendation.* It does not name what to do, how, or why; no one can act on it.
2. *Missing "how".* Decision-makers cannot estimate cost or timing; the proposal may never be implemented.
3. *Why anticipating objections helps.* It shows you have considered the proposal seriously; it prepares the audience to engage rather than resist.
4. *Why "no trade-offs" is almost never true.* Resources, time and political capital are finite; gains usually come with costs.
5. *Forgotten stakeholder.* Frontline workers who will implement the change; affected community members who are not in formal lobbying networks.
6. *Why "research shows" weakens.* It hides the source; specific citations are stronger.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *School SRC reform.* What: introduce rotating chairs to broaden leadership. Why: current chairs concentrate experience; rotation builds skills broadly. How: amend SRC charter, run training, evaluate after one year.
2. *Cardinia youth-engagement.* Multilingual youth-advisory positions; school-council partnerships; small-grants programme for youth-led projects.
3. *Voting accessibility reform.* Mobile polling in remote and high-CALD areas; longer pre-poll hours; clearer multilingual instructions.
4. *Most-benefit but harms-some debate.* Ethical considerations: distributive fairness and minority protection. Plan: include compensating measures or carve-outs.
5. *Wadawurrung heritage protection.* Step 1: support their proposal as primary authors. Step 2: provide research assistance only as requested. Step 3: amplify their recommendation through your channels with credit.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Institution: VCAT.* Recommendation: increase multilingual access in tenancy and consumer divisions. Why: ABS data on Victoria's CALD population; Auditor-General findings on delays and uneven access. How: dedicated funding, recruitment of bilingual staff, partnership with community legal centres. Supporters: tenant-advocacy groups, multicultural councils. Opponents: budget agencies citing cost. Objection: cost. Defence: efficiency gains from quicker resolution, plus equity benefit. Sources cited.
