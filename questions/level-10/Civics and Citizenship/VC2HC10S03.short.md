# VC2HC10S03 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Recommend changes to political, legal and/or civic institutions and justify these recommendations.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** What, why, how. Justify with evidence. Name trade-offs honestly.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **recommend changes** to institutions and **justify** them.
- You should be able to write a proposal someone could act on.

## Core content

A **recommendation** is a specific, actionable proposal that names the institution, the change, the rationale and the implementation steps. Three-part structure: **what** (the change), **why** (evidence and reasoning), **how** (steps, actors, timeline). Anticipate counterarguments. Identify **stakeholders** who benefit, who pays, and who must agree.

Every reform has **trade-offs**: time, money, complexity, disruption. Reforms that ignore trade-offs lose credibility and often fail in implementation. Justification needs precision: cite sources, quote specific numbers, link the change to the desired outcome.

**Worked example.** A school-level reform: Victorian secondary schools provide a structured "Year 10 civics-action week" with options across the four substrands. Why: state government strategy emphasises civic engagement, but Year 10 access is uneven. How: 5-day program co-designed with VicSRC, optional partner organisations, small grant per school. Trade-offs: time away from other subjects; equity of access.

**Cultural context.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria recommends reforms through treaty. Reconciliation Action Plans contain specific commitments. Multicultural advisory bodies propose reforms on settlement, education and language access. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- What, why, how.
- Justify with evidence.
- Name trade-offs honestly.
- Specific beats vague.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More money fixes it." Money helps, but design and delivery matter.
- "Best practice is enough justification." Cite specific evidence, not just labels.
- "Reform is always good." Some reforms have unintended harms.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why is "do better" not a recommendation?* It does not name what to do, how, or why; no one can act on it.
- *Why anticipating objections helps.* It shows the proposal has been thought through.
- *Why is "no trade-offs" almost never true?* Resources, time and political capital are finite.
- *Why does "research shows" weaken proposals?* It hides the source; specific citations are stronger.
- *Short summary of the civics-action-week proposal.* A focused, fundable, co-designed program with named partners and an honest trade-off statement.
