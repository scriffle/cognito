## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC10K11. The influence of a range of media, including social media, in shaping identity and attitudes to diversity.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Citizenship, diversity and identity).
- **Big ideas:**

  - Different media reach different audiences differently.
  - Algorithms now sit between message and audience.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **influence of a range of media**, including **social media**, in **shaping identity** and **attitudes to diversity**.
- You are learning to read media as a system, not just as content.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **media**, **representation** and **algorithm** in your own words.
- Identify three types of media that shape Australian attitudes.
- Describe one positive and one negative way media has influenced attitudes to diversity.
- Explain why social-media algorithms add a new layer to media influence.

## Core content

**1. Media as a system.** **Media** includes public broadcasters (ABC, SBS), commercial outlets (news, TV, radio), independent and ethnic media, online news, social platforms, podcasts and streaming. Each has its own audience, business model and editorial choices. *Quick check:* Why does business model affect editorial choices?

**2. Representation matters.** **Representation** is how groups are shown in media: who appears, in what roles, with what voice. Repeated patterns of representation shape how people see themselves and each other. *Quick check:* Why does seeing yourself reflected in stories matter at any age?

**3. Influence on identity.** Media gives language for who we are: shows like Heartbreak High and Wakefield, films like Sweet Country, and books across cultures shape self-perception. Australian media production now reflects more diverse voices than 20 years ago. *Quick check:* What is one Australian show or film that has shaped public conversations recently?

**4. Influence on attitudes to diversity.** Coverage choices, framing and headlines all shape attitudes: who is "Australian", whose stories are heard, whose communities are policed in coverage. Positive examples include SBS's multilingual programming and NITV's Indigenous storytelling. Negative examples include moral-panic framing of specific communities (e.g., past "African gangs" coverage). *Quick check:* Why does framing differ from facts?

**5. Worked example: social-media algorithms.** An **algorithm** is a set of rules a platform uses to decide what to show you. It learns from your behaviour and serves content that keeps you engaged. This can create "filter bubbles" where you mostly see people like you. It can amplify novelty, outrage and division. Some platforms now publish their algorithm policies, but transparency remains partial. *Follow the thinking:* user behaviour → algorithmic choice → content shown → user response → reinforced pattern.

**6. Misinformation and amplification.** Social media amplifies content quickly, including misinformation. Public bodies (ACMA), journalists and civil-society groups push for media-literacy education and platform accountability. *Quick check:* Why is "speed" a key feature of online misinformation?

**7. Building media literacy.** Skills include: identifying source, distinguishing news from opinion, checking citations, recognising paid content, understanding how algorithms work, varying your feed deliberately. *Quick check:* What is one habit that increases your media literacy?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria hosts major newsrooms (The Age, ABC Melbourne), independent outlets (Crikey, The Saturday Paper), university journalism schools, and SBS headquarters in Federation Square.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** NITV, IndigenousX, NIRS and individual columnists provide First Nations voices in media. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** SBS Radio broadcasts in over 60 languages. Multilingual newspapers and community media in Melbourne reach audiences not served by mainstream outlets.

## Watch out for these traps
- "If it has lots of likes, it must be true." Engagement is not accuracy.
- "Mainstream media is unbiased." All media has framing choices.
- "Algorithms are neutral." They embed business and design choices.
- "Diversity in media is a recent fad." Pluralism has long been part of Australian media; what is new is volume and reach.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Business model and editorial.* Outlets funded by ads chase engagement; subscription outlets serve loyal readers; public broadcasters serve a broader public. Each shapes what is covered and how.
2. *Why representation matters.* It validates identities, shapes aspirations, and reduces stereotyping when done well.
3. *Recent show or film.* Heartbreak High; Total Control; Mystery Road; Bluey (international success with Australian voices).
4. *Framing vs facts.* Facts are what happened; framing is how the story is shaped, including word choice, images and what is left out.
5. *Why social media amplifies fast.* Algorithms reward novelty and engagement; sharing is one tap; correction is slower.
6. *Habit increasing literacy.* Reading at least one outside-your-bubble source weekly; checking a citation before sharing.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Viral housing video.* Trace the original source; cross-check with a reputable outlet; consider whether key context is missing before sharing.
2. *Casey media-literacy workshop.* Side-by-side coverage comparison; algorithm-tracing exercise; fact-checking a viral claim with citations.
3. *Junior journalist.* Speak with community organisations; use accurate names and terms; check imagery for stereotyping; reflect on framing choices.
4. *Algorithm regulation.* For: protects users from harm and misinformation. Against: risks limiting expression and innovation. Both sides agree transparency rules help.
5. *Misrepresentation response.* Step 1: document the story and contact the outlet. Step 2: respond through community-led media (SBS Radio in language). Step 3: file a complaint with the press council if appropriate.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: youth mental health.* Coverage in ABC (focus on policy and services), commercial tabloid (focus on individual stories, sometimes alarming), and community-language outlet (focus on culturally specific supports). Two differences: tone and emphasis on systemic vs individual factors. Possible effect: audiences may attribute responsibility differently based on the framing they encounter. Sources cited.
