# VC2HC10S02 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC10S02. Evaluate information, data, perspectives and ideas from a range of sources on contemporary issues.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Investigating contemporary civics and citizenship issues).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Evaluation is more than collection.
  - Source quality varies; honest evaluation says so.
  - Different perspectives carry different weight on different claims.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Literacy; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate information, data, perspectives and ideas** from a **range of sources** on **contemporary issues**.
- You are learning to grade sources for use, not just collect them.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **provenance**, **methodology** and **conflict of interest** in your own words.
- List five criteria for evaluating a source.
- Compare two sources on the same claim and justify which carries more weight.
- Identify when expert testimony should be preferred over personal experience and when the reverse is true.

## Core content

**1. Provenance.** **Provenance** is where the source comes from: who created it, when, and on whose behalf. Knowing provenance is the first step in evaluation. *Quick check:* Why is "looked good on the web" not provenance?

**2. Methodology.** **Methodology** is how the source produced its information: a survey design, an experimental method, a news-gathering process, a literature review. Methodology shapes what the source can support. *Quick check:* What is one methodology question to ask a poll?

**3. Conflict of interest.** A **conflict of interest** exists when the source has a stake in the result. It does not automatically discredit the source, but it should be disclosed and weighed. *Quick check:* Why is disclosure better than secrecy when conflicts exist?

**4. Five criteria for evaluation.** (1) Authority: who made it? (2) Currency: when? (3) Method: how? (4) Independence: any conflicts? (5) Verifiability: can others check the claims? *Quick check:* Which criterion is most often missed by students?

**5. Worked example: claims about youth crime.** Source A: an opinion column citing a few cases. Source B: an Australian Institute of Criminology report using national statistics. Source C: a community-led survey of young people about their interactions with police. Each can be useful; they answer different questions. A gives perspective and framing; B gives systemic trends; C gives lived experience. The right source depends on the claim you are evaluating. *Follow the thinking:* identify the claim → identify the source → match strength of source to claim → triangulate.

**6. Personal experience and expert testimony.** Personal experience is essential when the claim is about lived experience or impact. Expert testimony is essential when the claim is about systems, mechanisms or large-scale patterns. Strong analysis uses both, attributed appropriately. *Quick check:* Why is "experts know best" too simple?

**7. Communicating evaluation.** Evaluation is not just for you; it is part of your final analysis. Say what sources you used, why you weighted them as you did, and where uncertainty remains. *Quick check:* Why does naming uncertainty improve the credibility of your conclusions?

## Memorable takeaways
- Evaluate, do not just collect.
- Provenance is the first check.
- Method shapes what the source can support.
- Disclose conflicts.
- Personal and expert sources answer different questions.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Government data is unbiased." Independent, but still framed.
- "Personal stories are anecdote." They are essential evidence on lived experience.
- "If a source agrees with me, it must be strong." Confirmation bias is common; check it.
- "Newer is always better." Currency matters for fast-moving issues; older primary sources still matter.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "looked good" is not provenance.* Visual polish is not authorship; sources can look professional and still be unreliable.
2. *Poll methodology question.* What was the sample size and sampling method? How was the question worded?
3. *Why disclosure beats secrecy.* Readers can weigh the source themselves; hidden conflicts undermine trust when discovered.
4. *Most often missed criterion.* Methodology: students often accept summary numbers without asking how they were produced.
5. *Why "experts know best" is too simple.* Experts are reliable on systems and mechanisms; lived-experience voices are essential on impacts and meaning.
6. *Why naming uncertainty helps.* It signals careful evaluation and prevents overclaiming.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Two statistics, one issue.* Trace each to its source; compare methodologies; check which fits the specific claim you need.
2. *Bayside research-skills evening.* Source-evaluation game; live fact-check with parents; library tour of credible databases.
3. *Stakeholder submission.* Identify the author and their interests; check methodology; verify key facts independently.
4. *Anonymous student survey.* Issues: privacy and informed consent for under-18s; manipulation risks. Use only with ethics protocols and parental consent if required.
5. *Community testimony.* Treat as primary evidence; obtain permission; protect identities where needed; pair with documentary sources where useful.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: rental affordability in Melbourne.* Primary: REIV rent data, ABS housing data. Secondary: Tenants Victoria report, an academic study. Community-led: a renters' submission to a parliamentary inquiry. Scoring: government data scores highest on authority and verifiability; community-led source scores highest on lived experience. Use both, attributed. Sources cited.
