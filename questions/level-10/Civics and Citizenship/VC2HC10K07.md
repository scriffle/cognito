## Core content

**1. How Australia becomes bound by treaties.** Australia signs and ratifies treaties through the executive (Cabinet and the Governor-General). Treaties bind Australia in international law but do not automatically apply inside Australia. Australia uses a **dualist** system: parliament must pass implementing legislation. *Quick check:* Why does Australia separate signing from implementation?

**2. Major treaties Australia has ratified.** International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966); International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966); Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989); Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (1965); Paris Agreement on climate change (2015). *Quick check:* What is one Australian law that implements a treaty?

**3. Worked example: Racial Discrimination Act and Mabo.** Australia ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (CERD) in 1975. Parliament passed the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth). In Mabo v Queensland (No. 2) (1992), the High Court used the racial-equality principle reinforced by the Act in recognising native title. The Native Title Act 1993 (Cth) followed. International obligation, statute, court decision and further legislation worked together. *Follow the thinking:* treaty ratified → statute passed → court applies → further legislation.

**4. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander rights in international law.** Key instruments include CERD, the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007), and the International Labour Organization's Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention (1989, not ratified by Australia). Australia endorsed UNDRIP in 2009. UNDRIP is not legally binding but informs policy. *Quick check:* Why does endorsing UNDRIP matter even without legal force?

**5. Issues with applying obligations.** A treaty does not always become law smoothly. Obstacles include political will, conflict with existing laws, federal-state complications, and disagreements about implementation. The UN has criticised aspects of Australian asylum policy and Indigenous policy at times. *Quick check:* What is one Australian example where obligations and application have diverged?

**6. Policy as well as law.** Some obligations shape policies more than statutes: climate action under the Paris Agreement, education under the Convention on the Rights of the Child, refugee processing under the Refugee Convention. Government policy responses can change between elections. *Quick check:* Why does policy implementation matter as much as legal implementation?

**7. Reform and reporting.** Australia reports periodically to UN bodies on its compliance. Civil-society organisations submit shadow reports. Reports drive parliamentary inquiries and sometimes new legislation. *Quick check:* Why is "periodic reporting" useful for rights protection?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities (2006) draws on the ICCPR. State agencies must consider human-rights compatibility. The Victorian Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission supports compliance.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The First Peoples' Assembly of Victoria engages with international Indigenous rights frameworks. AIATSIS publishes guidance on UNDRIP and other instruments. Treaty negotiations in Victoria connect international principles with domestic process. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Many Victorians arrived under refugee or migration treaties (Refugee Convention 1951, Migration Act provisions). Multilingual community organisations advocate for rights under international frameworks.

## Memorable takeaways
- Sign, ratify, implement: three steps.
- Treaty needs domestic law to bind individuals.
- Obligations and application can drift apart.
- UNDRIP informs without binding.
- Reporting drives reform.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Signing means binding inside Australia." No. Implementation in domestic law is needed.
- "International law overrides Australian law." Generally no, unless implemented.
- "Endorsement equals ratification." No. Endorsement is non-binding.
- "Australia complies with all treaties fully." Compliance is reviewed and not always complete.

## Model responses

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why separate signing from implementation.* It preserves parliamentary sovereignty: parliament decides what becomes domestic law, not the executive alone.
2. *Australian law implementing a treaty.* The Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (CERD); the Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (CEDAW); the Migration Act provisions implementing the Refugee Convention.
3. *Why endorsing UNDRIP matters.* It signals policy direction, encourages domestic reform and provides a framework for advocacy and reporting.
4. *Obligations and application diverging.* UN Human Rights Committee findings on Australia's mandatory immigration detention.
5. *Why policy implementation matters as much as legal.* Even a strong statute fails if funding, staffing and oversight are weak.
6. *Why periodic reporting is useful.* It creates a recurring forum to identify gaps, hear from civil society and prompt government responses.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"Australia is bound by everything the UN says".* Signing shows intent; ratification commits internationally; only implementing legislation creates duties enforceable in Australian courts.
2. *Casey Paris Agreement forum.* What is the council's emissions plan? How does it align with state targets? How can residents verify progress?
3. *Community legal-centre advice.* Whether the treaty has been ratified; whether implementing legislation exists; whether complaint mechanisms (e.g., AHRC, UN bodies) are open.
4. *Ratifying ILO 169.* For: stronger Indigenous-rights framework. Against: complexity of integration with existing law and consultation processes. Both sides agree consultation with First Nations is essential.
5. *Pacific Island climate engagement.* Step 1: contact federal MPs and ministers. Step 2: submit to parliamentary inquiries on Pacific affairs. Step 3: build coalitions with Australian climate-justice organisations.
