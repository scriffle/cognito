# VC2HC10K01 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** The role of the Australian Constitution in providing the basis for Australia's federal system of government and democratic processes, including democratic institutions, and the process for constitutional change through a referendum.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Constitution divides, limits, enables. Double majority is a high bar. Most referendums fail.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **role of the Australian Constitution** in providing the **basis for federation**, **institutions** and **referendum change**.
- You should be able to read the Constitution as a working framework.

## Core content

The Constitution came into force in 1901. **Federation** divides power: s 51 lists Commonwealth powers (defence, immigration, taxation); States retain residual powers. Section 109 resolves clashes: federal law prevails. The Constitution establishes parliament, the Governor-General, and the High Court. Express rights are few (freedom of religion s 116; trial by jury for some federal offences s 80). The High Court has recognised an implied freedom of political communication.

A **referendum** requires a bill to pass both houses, then a national vote needing a **double majority**: nationally and in a majority of States (at least four of six). Only 8 of 45 referendums have passed. The 1967 referendum extended Commonwealth power to legislate for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. The 2023 Voice referendum did not pass.

**Worked example.** A referendum pathway. Bill passes both houses → campaign period → public vote → double-majority count → Governor-General gives royal assent → Constitution is changed.

**Cultural context.** First Nations peoples were not consulted in drafting the Constitution. The Uluru Statement (2017) called for a constitutionally enshrined Voice. Treaty negotiations in Victoria are exploring state-level constitutional change. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Constitution divides, limits, enables.
- Federation: two levels of government.
- Express rights are few; implied rights exist.
- Double majority is a high bar.

## Watch out for these traps
- "The Constitution lists all our rights." It contains only a few express rights.
- "Referendums are won by good arguments alone." Bipartisan support and campaign resources also matter.
- "Constitutional change always means rewriting." Court interpretation also changes how it works.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why two levels of government?* Local and national issues both need decision-making at the right scale.
- *Express vs implied rights.* Express rights are written in the text; implied rights are interpreted by the High Court.
- *Why is complexity a barrier in a referendum?* Voters reasonably want to understand the change; complex proposals are hard to explain quickly.
- *Why is "the Constitution is fixed" only partly true?* The text is hard to change, but court interpretation and political practice evolve.
- *Short summary of the referendum pathway.* Bill through both houses → national vote → double majority (national + majority of States) → royal assent → text changes.
