# VC2HC10K06 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** The role of parliaments and the High Court of Australia in protecting rights under the Constitution, common law, and through federal and state/territory laws.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Rights from many sources. Parliaments make and update them. High Court interprets the Constitution.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **role of parliaments** and the **High Court** in **protecting rights**.
- You should be able to find a right's source and its protector.

## Core content

Australia protects rights from four sources: the **Constitution** (express like s 116 freedom of religion; implied like freedom of political communication); **common law** (habeas corpus, fair trial); **statutes** (Racial Discrimination Act 1975, Sex Discrimination Act 1984, Privacy Act 1988, Victorian Charter of Human Rights 2006); and **international treaties** (need domestic legislation to bind individuals). Australia has no single federal Bill of Rights.

Parliaments create rights, change them, balance them with other interests and establish complaint bodies (AHRC, VEOHRC). The High Court interprets the Constitution, applies common law, and reviews legislation for constitutional validity.

**Worked example.** Implied freedom of political communication. In Australian Capital Television v Commonwealth (1992), the High Court interpreted the Constitution to imply a freedom restricting government action. It is not a personal right; parliaments still legislate around it (defamation, election rules).

**Cultural context.** Native title (Mabo, 1992) and the Native Title Act 1993 (Cth) are major rights developments. The 1967 referendum amended s 51(xxvi). Treaty negotiations in Victoria are exploring further recognition. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Rights from many sources.
- Constitution: a few, well protected.
- Statutes: most everyday rights.
- High Court: interpreter.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Australia has a Bill of Rights." It does not at federal level.
- "Rights are absolute." They are usually balanced with other interests.
- "Common law is obsolete." It continues to develop alongside statute.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why multiple sources of rights?* History, federalism and evolution have produced layered protection.
- *Why are statutes easier to update than the Constitution?* Statutes need parliamentary passage; constitutional change needs a referendum.
- *Why can parliament both create and limit rights?* Rights compete with each other and with public interests; parliament balances them.
- *Why does interpretation matter without text change?* Courts apply old text to new contexts.
- *Short summary of "free speech" in Australia.* No general right; there is an implied freedom of political communication that restricts government action, balanced by defamation and other laws.
