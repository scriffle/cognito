# VC2HC10K05 — Civics and Citizenship (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** The key features and jurisdictions of Australia's court system; the operations of courts and tribunals, including the roles of courts, judges, lawyers and juries in trials; and the rights of the accused and the rights of victims.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship.
- **Big ideas:** Jurisdiction defines who hears. Roles at trial keep it fair. Accused has rights, so does the victim.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **key features and jurisdictions** of Australia's **court system**, including **trial roles** and **rights of accused and victims**.
- You should be able to read a trial as a structured process.

## Core content

**Jurisdiction** is the authority of a court to hear certain cases. Victorian hierarchy: Magistrates' Court (summary, small civil), County Court (serious indictable, larger civil), Supreme Court (most serious, appeals). High Court of Australia is the final appeal and interprets the Constitution. **Tribunals** (VCAT, AAT) resolve everyday disputes more cheaply.

Trial roles: the **judge** applies the law; the **prosecution** presents the state's case; the **defence** represents the accused; the **jury** decides facts in serious indictable trials. Rights of the accused: presumption of innocence, silence, legal representation, fair and timely hearing, appeal. Rights of victims: respectful treatment, case information, victim-impact statements, support services and in some cases restitution.

**Worked example.** A serious assault trial. The accused is charged and represented. The prosecution presents evidence; the defence cross-examines and presents its own. The judge directs the jury on law. The jury returns a verdict. If guilty, the judge sentences. The accused can appeal.

**Cultural context.** The Koori Court is a culturally responsive division of the Magistrates' Court for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander defendants under specific conditions, with Elder involvement. The Victorian Aboriginal Legal Service represents Aboriginal Victorians. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Jurisdiction defines who hears.
- Hierarchy enables appeals.
- Roles at trial keep it fair.
- Accused has rights, so does the victim.

## Watch out for these traps
- "If charged, you must be guilty." No. The accused is presumed innocent.
- "Juries decide the law." No. They decide facts; judges decide law.
- "Tribunals are not real courts." They are different but legitimate.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Why does a court need jurisdiction?* Without authority granted by law, its decisions are not binding.
- *Why are tribunals attractive?* Cheaper, faster, less formal.
- *Why does the jury decide facts, not law?* Lay people weigh evidence; legal interpretation is the judge's specialised role.
- *Why protect rights of both accused and victims?* Fair trials require fairness to both.
- *Short summary of a serious assault trial.* Judge directs law; prosecution and defence present evidence; jury decides facts; judge sentences; accused may appeal.
