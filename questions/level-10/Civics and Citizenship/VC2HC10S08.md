# VC2HC10S08 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot

- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Communicating).

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct evidence-based arguments** using **civics and citizenship knowledge, concepts and different perspectives**, and to **determine the most effective method of communication**.
- You are learning to make a case that earns belief and chooses its channel deliberately.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **argument**, **counter-argument**, **synthesis** and **method of communication** in your own words.
- Build a four-part argument: claim, evidence, reasoning, counter-argument.
- Choose a communication method for a stated audience and justify it.
- Adapt one argument for two formats while preserving the core.

## Core content

**1. What a civic argument needs.** A solid argument has a **claim** (specific and testable), **evidence** (named sources, with dates), **reasoning** (the mechanism linking evidence and claim) and engagement with a **counter-argument** (the strongest opposing view, fairly stated). *Quick check:* Why does engaging counter-arguments make your argument stronger?

**2. Concepts and knowledge.** Civics arguments draw on concepts (rule of law, democracy, federation, rights, citizenship) and knowledge (institutions, history, current events). Concept use is precise: prefer "implied freedom of political communication" to vague "free speech" when accuracy matters. *Quick check:* Why does concept precision build trust?

**3. Perspectives.** Different perspectives bring different evidence and values to the same issue. Strong civic arguments name perspectives explicitly and weigh them. **Synthesis** is the work of combining perspectives into a coherent argument. *Quick check:* What is one risk of including perspectives only as decoration?

**4. Worked example: an argument on lowering the federal voting age.** Claim: Australia should consider lowering the federal voting age to 16. Evidence: data on civic literacy in countries that have lowered it; AEC analysis of youth turnout; school surveys. Reasoning: civic skills are taught in secondary school; including young voters earlier may build lifelong engagement. Counter-argument: concerns about cognitive and political maturity at 16. Engagement: cite research and counter-research; propose a staged approach (lower bound for state elections first). *Follow the thinking:* claim → evidence → reasoning → counter → synthesis → conclusion with limits.

**5. Choosing a method.** Methods include: parliamentary submission, op-ed, social-media campaign, podcast, infographic, video, presentation, letter to MP. Choose by audience, length, complexity and reach. *Quick check:* Which method suits a 90-second video for peers?

**6. Adapting without diluting.** The same claim and evidence can take different forms for different audiences. Adapting changes language and emphasis, not accuracy. *Quick check:* What is the test of "did I dilute the argument"?

**7. Citation and integrity.** Civic arguments cite sources by name and date. Quote sparingly; paraphrase carefully; acknowledge limits. Integrity beats persuasion in the long run. *Quick check:* Why is "long-run integrity" worth the short-run cost?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Victorian Parliament's research papers, the Victorian Auditor-General reports, the Productivity Commission and university research centres provide accessible primary sources for arguments.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Cite Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors directly. ## Stretch yourself
Pick one civic issue. Build a four-part argument with at least three sources, two perspectives and one counter-argument. Choose two communication methods for two audiences. Justify each. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More words means stronger." Clarity, not length, persuades.
- "Sounding neutral" means no view. Neutral framing avoids loaded words; you can still argue strongly.
- "Counter-arguments weaken you." Engaging them strengthens you.
- "Long quotes prove the point." Use sparingly; reasoning is yours.

A "communication compass": at the centre, your argument. Spokes: audience, method, evidence, perspectives, counter. Add one detail at each spoke.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why engage counter-arguments.* It demonstrates you have considered the strongest opposing view; it strengthens credibility and persuades thoughtful readers.
2. *Why concept precision builds trust.* It signals careful thinking; vague terms invite dismissal or misinterpretation.
3. *Risk of decorative perspectives.* They look inclusive but do not affect the conclusion; serious analysis weighs perspectives in the reasoning.
4. *Method for 90-second peer video.* Short video with captions and one strong visual and a clear call to action.
5. *Test of dilution.* The core claim and evidence remain accurate; only language and emphasis change.
6. *Why long-run integrity matters.* Persuasion built on poor citation collapses on contact with scrutiny; trust compounds when integrity is consistent.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Compulsory voting and "anti-freedom".* Claim: compulsory voting strengthens democratic legitimacy. Evidence: turnout data; AEC analysis; comparative country data. Reasoning: high turnout reduces unequal political voice. Counter: liberty argument against compulsion. Engagement: distinguish compulsion to vote from compulsion of vote content; note minor penalties; accept liberty concerns and propose civic-education reforms.
2. *Yarra housing op-ed.* Intro with the issue and stakes; three claims with evidence (rental data, housing supply, vacancy rates); counter-argument considered; conclusion with concrete recommendation.
3. *NGO media kit.* Media release for journalists; fact sheet for policymakers; short explainer for the public. Each audience needs different tone and length.
4. *Amplifying a community story.* Issues: consent and protection of the person. Get clear permission, allow review of the final piece, provide an opt-out path.
5. *Wadawurrung finding adaptation.* Step 1: co-author with Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: use plain language with named credit. Step 3: review before public release; tune the format to each audience while preserving the science.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Issue: better youth mental-health services in Melbourne's outer suburbs.* Argument: targeted state investment is justified. Evidence: prevalence data, service-gap reports, peer-reviewed effectiveness studies. Perspectives: GPs, schools, young people, community organisations. Counter-argument: cost. Engagement: cost-effectiveness analysis. Methods: a 600-word op-ed for general audiences; a 200-word brief for the state Minister. Sources cited.
