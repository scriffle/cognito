# VC2HC10K03 — Civics and Citizenship

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2HC10K03. The key features and values of Australia's system of government compared with at least one other democratic or non-democratic system of government in the Asia-Pacific region.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Civics and Citizenship (Government and democracy).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Systems of government differ in structure and values.
  - Comparing reveals what each system protects and what it omits.
  - The Asia-Pacific holds many models.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Intercultural Capability; Ethical Capability; Asia and Australia's Engagement with Asia.

## What you are learning
- You are learning the **key features and values** of Australia's **system of government** **compared with at least one other** democratic or non-democratic system in the **Asia-Pacific region**.
- You are learning to compare systems honestly, not to rank them.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **parliamentary democracy**, **presidential system** and **one-party state** in your own words.
- Outline Australia's system in five features.
- Compare Australia with one Asia-Pacific country on three features.
- Explain why "comparison" is more useful than "ranking".

## Core content

**1. Australia's system in five features.** Federal parliamentary democracy: two houses of parliament; constitutional monarchy with King as head of state (Governor-General as representative); compulsory voting at federal elections; independent judiciary; protected freedoms (with limits). *Quick check:* Why is "constitutional monarchy" different from "monarchy"?

**2. Other models in the Asia-Pacific.** New Zealand: parliamentary democracy with one chamber. Japan: parliamentary democracy with a constitutional monarchy. Indonesia: presidential democracy with multi-party elections. Singapore: parliamentary democracy with dominant-party rule and tight media controls. China: one-party state under the Communist Party of China. *Quick check:* What is one feature shared by Australia, New Zealand and Japan?

**3. Worked example: Australia and Indonesia.** Australia: parliamentary democracy, compulsory voting, three layers of government, constitutional monarchy. Indonesia: presidential democracy since 1998 reform, directly elected president, decentralised system across provinces and regencies, secular state with religious diversity. Both protect freedoms and hold elections. Differences: head of state and head of government are the same person (President) in Indonesia, separated in Australia (Governor-General and Prime Minister). *Follow the thinking:* match features → note differences → consider values behind each choice.

**4. Values behind systems.** Australia values stability, separation of powers and rule of law. Different countries emphasise different combinations: NZ emphasises Maori Treaty integration; Japan emphasises social consensus; Singapore emphasises efficient governance and ethnic balance; China emphasises party-led development. *Quick check:* Why are values not the same as preferences?

**5. Comparing rather than ranking.** Comparison shows what each system protects and what it leaves out. Ranking implies one system is right for every context. Comparison respects that systems evolve in local conditions. *Quick check:* What is one risk of ranking?

**6. Diplomatic relations and shared institutions.** Australia engages with Asia-Pacific countries through ASEAN dialogues, the Pacific Islands Forum, bilateral agreements and regional security partnerships (e.g., AUKUS). Different systems can cooperate even when they differ. *Quick check:* Why does cooperation across different systems matter?

**7. Strengths and shortcomings.** Every system has both. Australia's compulsory voting raises turnout but compels participation. Singapore's efficiency comes with limits on media. China's one-party model concentrates power. Comparing helps you understand the trade-offs each system makes. *Quick check:* Why is "every system has shortcomings" a useful starting point?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian schools and universities engage with Asia-Pacific partners through sister-school programs and study tours. The Asialink Centre at the University of Melbourne supports public understanding.

Their perspectives enrich civic learning.

## Memorable takeaways
- Compare, do not rank.
- Features sit on top of values.
- Asia-Pacific holds many models.
- Cooperation crosses systems.
- Every system has trade-offs.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one Asia-Pacific country. Compare its system with Australia's on at least four features. Identify two values behind one of the differences and one shared challenge both countries face. Cite reputable sources. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Australian democracy is the best." Comparison reveals strengths and limits; "best" depends on what you value.
- "Non-democratic systems do not work." Many deliver economic results; the trade-offs are with freedoms and accountability.
- "Compulsory voting equals democracy." It is one practice; many democracies use voluntary voting.
- "Asia-Pacific countries are all similar." They span every major system type.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Constitutional monarchy vs monarchy.* In a constitutional monarchy, the monarch's powers are limited by a constitution; in a pure monarchy, they are not.
2. *Shared feature.* Independent judiciary; parliamentary democracy; protected freedoms (with local variations).
3. *Why values are not just preferences.* Values are deeper commitments that shape laws and institutions; preferences shift more easily.
4. *Risk of ranking.* It assumes one system is universally best, ignoring context, history and trade-offs.
5. *Why cooperation across systems matters.* Shared challenges (climate, trade, security) need coordinated action regardless of differences.
6. *Why "every system has shortcomings" helps.* It prevents overclaiming for any system and supports honest comparison.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *"All governments are the same".* They differ on head of state, voting, separation of powers and protection of freedoms. Compare two specific countries to show the differences.
2. *Casey multicultural civics night.* Side-by-side panels on Australia, NZ and Indonesia; map of Asia-Pacific systems; Q&A with multicultural community speakers.
3. *Australia–Indonesia brief.* Both are democracies with parliaments; Indonesia is presidential; both engage through ASEAN dialogues.
4. *Partnering with non-democracies.* For: shared challenges and economic ties. Against: legitimising practices that breach rights. Both sides can be addressed through targeted engagement and human-rights advocacy.
5. *Australia vs Vietnam.* Australia: multi-party parliamentary system. Vietnam: one-party state. Both deliver public services but with different accountability mechanisms. Citizens have different rights to participate.
