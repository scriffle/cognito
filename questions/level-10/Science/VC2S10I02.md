## Core content

**1. Validity vs reproducibility.** **Validity** is whether the study measures what it claims. **Reproducibility** is whether others can repeat it and get similar results. Both must be designed in. *Quick check:* Why is a precise but invalid measurement still a problem?

**2. Sources of error.** Random errors arise from small unpredictable variations and shrink with repetition. Systematic errors arise from miscalibration or design choices and shift the whole dataset. Both should be named in advance. *Quick check:* What is one systematic error in a stopwatch experiment?

**3. Sources of bias.** **Bias** is a non-random influence that distorts results. Selection bias: who or what is sampled. Observer bias: how observations are recorded. Confirmation bias: looking only for evidence that supports a favoured idea. *Quick check:* Why does double-blinding reduce some forms of bias?

**4. Worked example: a community air-quality study in Sunshine.** Setup: students place sensors at five sites for two weeks. Validity: do the sensors measure the pollutant claimed? Reproducibility: same calibration, time and protocol across sites. Errors: sensor drift (systematic), wind variation (random). Bias: site selection should not exclude high-traffic streets. Ethics: anonymise photos taken near homes. Cultural protocols: contact the Bunurong Land Council Aboriginal Corporation if any sites are on culturally significant land. *Follow the thinking:* validity → reproducibility → error → bias → ethics → protocols.

**5. Risk management at Year 10.** Risk assessments use hazards, who, controls and an updated review. They cover physical, chemical, biological and psychological hazards. *Quick check:* What is one psychological risk in a school study about body image?

**6. Ethical issues.** Ethics asks who could be harmed, who consents, and how data are stored. Even publicly available data can be misused. Privacy and respect for human and non-human participants are essential. *Quick check:* Why does informed consent require more than a signature?

**7. Cultural protocols on Country.** A **protocol** is a set of agreed rules for behaviour. Protocols on Country include consent from Traditional Owner corporations, briefings, and limits on photographing or sampling significant sites. AIATSIS and Victorian Aboriginal organisations publish public guidelines. *Quick check:* Why is community-led science more than consultation?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** EPA Victoria, Melbourne Water and the Department of Health publish field protocols suitable for adaptation in school studies. WorkSafe Victoria publishes school risk-assessment templates.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS and Victorian Traditional Owner corporations have published clear consent and protocol frameworks. Joint-management research with Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung, Wadawurrung, Bunurong and Gunaikurnai peoples in Victoria includes cultural protocols at every step. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Health-research projects in multilingual communities across Melbourne use translated consent processes. Researchers from Vietnamese, Sudanese and Tamil backgrounds work as community liaisons to design culturally responsive ethics protocols.

## Memorable takeaways
- Validity asks "the right thing". Reproducibility asks "the same way".
- Bias hides in choices.
- Repeats handle random. Calibration handles systematic.
- Ethics and protocols are method.
- Community-led beats consulted.

## Watch out for these traps
- "If we are careful, bias goes away." No. Bias must be named, designed against and checked.
- "Risk assessments are routine paperwork." No. Updated risk thinking changes methods.
- "Permission for research is a quick form." No. Especially on Country, real consent involves time and partnership.
- "Cultural protocols slow down science." No. They make science usable, accountable and trustworthy.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Precise but invalid problem.* The data are repeatable but do not measure what is claimed, so the conclusion is wrong even though the numbers look tight.
2. *Stopwatch systematic error.* Reaction-time delay in starting and stopping. Average reaction time can shift the recorded time by tenths of a second.
3. *Why double-blinding reduces bias.* Neither the participant nor the observer knows the assignment, so expectations cannot bias responses or recordings.
4. *Psychological risk in a body-image study.* Distress for participants. Use opt-in, opt-out at any point, and provide support information.
5. *Why informed consent is more than a signature.* It requires understanding, voluntary agreement and the right to withdraw.
6. *Why community-led is more than consultation.* Consultation can be cosmetic. Community leadership shapes the question, methods and reporting.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Self sleep diary.* Bias: you may judge sleep quality more positively after good days. Ethical: data is personal; do not share without your own consent. Mitigation: anonymise self-data and use objective measures (e.g., a wearable) where possible.
2. *Brimbank noise.* Stratified sampling by suburb and time of day, including high- and low-traffic areas. Ethical safeguard: avoid recording near homes; if needed, anonymise voice content and meet privacy laws.
3. *Microplastics in creek.* Errors: contamination of samples, instrument bias, weather variation. Reduce by using clean lab procedures, blank samples and consistent sampling windows. Risk row: "Slippery banks → students at risk → use grip footwear and partner buddy."
4. *Facial recognition on students.* Issues: privacy and consent. Alternative: use open-source datasets with informed-consent licensing and discuss algorithmic bias rather than running on classmates.
5. *Wurundjeri wattle study.* Step 1: contact the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Cultural Heritage Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: agree on the question and method. Step 3: train under ranger guidance on Country. Step 4: share findings with the corporation before any public report.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Topic: school lunchroom CO₂ levels and student-reported alertness.* Method names variables, validity (CO₂ meter calibrated against reference), reproducibility (same site, same time, same survey), errors (sensor drift), bias (selection of participants), ethics (anonymised survey, opt-out), protocol (no Country-specific protocol relevant; acknowledgement of Country given).
