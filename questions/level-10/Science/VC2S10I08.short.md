# VC2S10I08 — Science Inquiry Skills (Short)

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** Communicate and justify scientific ideas, findings and arguments by selecting appropriate formats, vocabulary, conventions and representations for diverse audiences.
- **Year / Area:** Year 10, Science.
- **Big ideas:** Audience first. Conventions are shared grammar. Same accuracy, many artefacts.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **communicate** and **justify** science by selecting **formats**, **vocabulary**, **conventions** and **representations** for **diverse audiences**.
- You should be able to adapt one finding for at least three audiences.

## Core content

A **format** is the type of communication: report, brief, infographic, video, social post. Use technical words when they save explanation, and define them on first use. Match register to audience.

Scientific **conventions** include SI units, captioned figures and tables, consistent citations, and uncertainty reported with the result. **Justification** connects evidence and reasoning, names uncertainty and acknowledges alternatives. Tables, graphs, diagrams, photos and models all carry the message; captions guide the eye.

**Worked example.** A study on microplastics in Port Phillip Bay. Audience 1: a journal report with methods, results and references. Audience 2: a council brief with one chart, two paragraphs, three actions. Audience 3: a 90-second TikTok with one strong visual and a call to action. Same data, same accuracy, three artefacts.

**Cultural context.** First Languages Australia and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish guidance on respectful language and visuals when communicating science involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Memorable takeaways
- Audience first, format second.
- Define jargon on first use.
- Captions guide the eye.
- Same accuracy, many artefacts.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More charts means better communication." No. Each chart must earn its space.
- "Plain language means dumbed down." No. It is clear, not shallow.
- "Public communication does not need accuracy." No. Public trust depends on it.

## Model responses

Use these to check your thinking after you have had a go.

- *Peer vs primary-student differences.* Length, vocabulary, examples, pace and number of visuals change; accuracy stays the same.
- *Format for a 90-second social story.* Short video with on-screen captions and one strong visual.
- *Why follow conventions even when not graded?* They speed shared understanding and trust.
- *Why is "this shows that…" weak?* It claims more than the evidence may support and skips reasoning.
- *Short summary of the microplastics example.* The same finding becomes a peer report, a council brief and a social post, each tuned to its audience while keeping the science accurate.
