## Core content

**1. Variation is the raw material.** Within any population, individuals differ in traits that have a genetic basis. **Variation** comes from mutation, sexual reproduction and migration. *Quick check:* Why is a uniform population vulnerable to environmental change?

**2. Selection favours fit traits.** **Natural selection** is the process where individuals whose traits suit their environment leave more offspring. Over generations, those traits become more common. The environment includes climate, predators, food, disease and other species. *Quick check:* Why is "survival of the fittest" not just about strength?

**3. Isolation and divergence.** When populations are separated (geographic, behavioural, ecological), gene flow stops. Different selection pressures can lead to new species. The Galapagos finches and Australian eucalypts both show this. *Quick check:* Name one barrier that could isolate two populations.

**4. Adaptation is gradual.** **Adaptation** is a trait shaped by natural selection that suits the organism to its environment. Adaptations are usually not perfect, just better than alternatives. *Quick check:* Why is "designed for" misleading when we describe adaptations?

**5. Worked example: peppered moths and Australian rabbits.** During the Industrial Revolution, peppered-moth populations shifted from light to dark forms as soot darkened tree trunks. After pollution controls, light forms recovered. In Australia, the rabbit calicivirus (RHDV) initially killed most rabbits; survivors had genetic resistance, and rabbit populations have partially rebounded. Both are documented examples of natural selection in action. *Follow the thinking:* identify variation → identify selection pressure → trace allele frequency change → check evidence.

**6. Common ancestry and evidence.** Multiple lines of evidence support evolution: fossils show change over time; comparative anatomy shows shared structures (vertebrate limbs); embryology shows shared development; DNA sequences show shared ancestry; biogeography matches plate tectonics. *Quick check:* Why are independent lines of evidence stronger than any single one?

**7. Adaptation vs lifetime change.** A trained athlete builds muscle within a lifetime. That change is not inherited. Evolution acts on populations across generations through inherited variation. *Quick check:* Why was Lamarck's "use and disuse" wrong about inheritance?

## Watch out for these traps
- "Evolution is just a theory, so it might be wrong." No. In science, "theory" means a well-tested explanation supported by many lines of evidence.
- "Individuals evolve." No. Populations evolve across generations.
- "Evolution always means progress." No. It means better fit to current conditions.
- "Aboriginal stories of Country are not science." No. They are observation-based and increasingly aligned with peer-reviewed ecology.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why a uniform population is vulnerable.* Without variation, there is no raw material for adaptation if the environment changes.
2. *Why "survival of the fittest" is not just strength.* Fitness is reproductive success, which depends on traits that suit the environment, not just physical strength.
3. *One isolating barrier.* Mountain ranges, oceans, rivers, behavioural differences, or temporal differences in mating seasons.
4. *Why "designed for" is misleading.* Adaptations emerge from selection on existing variation, not from intentional design.
5. *Why independent evidence is stronger.* Each line could be questioned alone; together they are hard to explain by anything other than evolution.
6. *Why Lamarck's "use and disuse" was wrong.* Acquired traits are not encoded in DNA, so they are not passed on. Inheritance works on genetic variation, not lifetime change.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Mosquitoes and repellents.* Variation in mosquito populations means some tolerate repellents better. Repeated exposure selects for resistant variants. Over time, repellents become less effective.
2. *Melton bushfire-resilient plants.* Look for thick bark (insulation), epicormic buds (regrowth from trunks), and seed-banking strategies (lignotubers or hard-coated seeds). These suit Australia's fire-adapted environments.
3. *Antibiotic resistance.* Variation in bacteria; strong selection from antibiotic use; rapid generation time amplifies resistance traits across populations.
4. *Extinction-prevention gene editing.* Issues: ecological unintended consequences and consent of affected stakeholders, including Traditional Owners.
5. *Wadawurrung ranger programs.* Long-term observations of species and habitat shifts contribute to evolutionary understanding. Discuss with permission and credit, using published, community-approved material.

Selection pressure: predator avoidance and food in soil and leaf litter. Evidence: monotreme fossils and genomic data showing deep divergence from other mammals.
