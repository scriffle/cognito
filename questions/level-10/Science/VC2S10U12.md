# VC2S10U12 — Science Understanding

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10U12. Space exploration aims to expand our understanding of the universe's origins and structure, and to solve the challenges of humans living beyond Earth.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Earth & Space Sciences).
- **Big ideas:**

  - Telescopes and probes extend our senses.
  - Living beyond Earth needs new biology, chemistry and ethics.

## What you are learning

- You are learning to read missions, technologies and ethics together.

By the end of this you should be able to:

- Describe how telescopes capture light beyond visible wavelengths.
- Identify three challenges of long-duration spaceflight.
- Discuss one ethical question raised by space activity.

## Core content

**1. Why explore space?** Two motives: curiosity (origins of the universe, life elsewhere) and capability (satellite communications, navigation, weather). Both drive science and technology. *Quick check:* Why is "exploration" not the same as "tourism" in space science?

**2. Telescopes across wavelengths.** Visible light is one slice. Radio (Square Kilometre Array Australia), infrared (JWST), X-ray and gamma-ray telescopes each see different physics. Some are on the ground; some are in orbit to avoid atmospheric blocking. *Quick check:* Why is the JWST positioned far from Earth?

**3. Probes, orbiters, landers, rovers.** A **probe** flies past or to a target. An **orbiter** circles a body. A **lander** touches down. A **rover** moves around the surface. The Mars Curiosity and Perseverance rovers are examples. *Quick check:* Why is sending data faster than sending samples back?

**4. Worked example: Mars exploration.** Goals: search for past or present life and characterise the climate. Methods: orbiters map the planet; rovers analyse rocks and soils, looking for **biosignatures** (chemical or structural traces of life). Challenges: long delay in radio signals, cold temperatures, dust, radiation, limited power. *Follow the thinking:* mission goal → instruments → data → uncertainty → next mission.

**5. Living beyond Earth.** Long-duration missions face microgravity (bone and muscle loss), radiation (cancer risk), confinement (mental health), life-support failure and food supply. Solutions in development include rotation for artificial gravity, shielding and bioregenerative life support. *Quick check:* Why is mental health a science question, not just a personal one, in space?

**6. Australian capability.** Australia hosts deep-space tracking stations (Tidbinbilla), key parts of the Square Kilometre Array, and growing space industry presence (CSIRO, ASA). Indigenous Knowledge holders contribute to discussions of Country and sky. *Quick check:* Why does Australia's geography matter for radio astronomy?

**7. Ethics in space.** Space junk, planetary protection (avoiding contamination of other worlds), commercial mining, and equitable access to orbit are active ethical issues. *Quick check:* Why is "leave no trace" relevant to space?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** RMIT and the University of Melbourne run aerospace and space-science programs. Swinburne hosts public astronomy programs, and Monash partners with international observatories.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander astronomical traditions span thousands of years and include detailed knowledge of stars, planets and seasonal sky changes. Researchers like Duane Hamacher, working with Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and other knowledge holders, publish in peer-reviewed journals. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Researchers from Indian, Chinese and European-Australian backgrounds at the Australian Astronomical Observatory and CSIRO Astronomy and Space Science contribute to international space programs.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Space exploration is just for billionaires." No. Most missions are publicly funded and serve broad scientific goals.
- "Everything we know about space comes from visible light." No. Most modern astronomy uses other wavelengths.
- "Living in space is just about engineering." No. Biology, psychology and ethics matter just as much.
- "Aboriginal astronomy is myth, not science." No. It is observation-based and increasingly aligned with peer-reviewed research.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Exploration vs tourism.* Exploration aims to gather new knowledge with measurable goals and methods. Tourism aims at experience for the participant.
2. *Why JWST is far from Earth.* It uses infrared, which is sensitive to heat from Earth and the Sun. Distance plus a sun-shield keeps it cold and stable.
3. *Why send data, not samples.* Data are fast and cheap; sample-return is technically hard, expensive and slow.
4. *Why mental health is science in space.* Confined, isolated and remote settings affect cognition and decision-making, both safety-critical issues in spaceflight.
5. *Why does Australian geography matter for radio astronomy?* Remote, low-population areas (e.g. Western Australia) have low radio interference, ideal for sensitive observations.
6. *Why "leave no trace" matters.* Space debris and planetary contamination can harm other missions and possible biosignatures.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Satellite trail.* It could be a low-Earth-orbit communications, observation or scientific satellite. Low-Earth orbit allows higher resolution and shorter signal-delay than geostationary orbit.
2. *Casey astronomy night.* Telescope viewing of the Moon and planets; an exhibit on Aboriginal astronomy with community-approved content; a hands-on infrared-vs-visible-light demonstration.
3. *Aerospace engineer.* Low orbit: better resolution, atmospheric drag; medium orbit: good for global communications; geostationary: stays above one location, large coverage but at long signal-delay.
4. *Asteroid mining.* Issues: equitable benefits and planetary-protection rules. Need international agreements before commercial-scale activity.
5. *SKA partnership with Wajarri Yamatji.* Step 1: written agreement with the Wajarri Yamatji Aboriginal Corporation for site use. Step 2: shared cultural-heritage management. Step 3: ongoing community benefits and employment.

Methods: large radio telescope arrays in WA. Science questions: when and how galaxies formed; nature of dark matter. Ethical consideration: ongoing Wajarri Yamatji partnership.
