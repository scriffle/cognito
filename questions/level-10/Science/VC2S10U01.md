## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10U01. The structures of reproductive cells and organs in plants and animals are related to their functions, and both sexual and asexual reproduction help species survive.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Biological Sciences: Reproduction & Genetics).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Reproductive structures match their function at every level.
  - Sexual reproduction generates variation.
  - Asexual reproduction is fast and reliable when conditions are stable.

- You are learning to link form to function in reproduction at the cell, organ and organism level.

## Core content

**1. Gametes and fertilisation.** A **gamete** is a reproductive cell. Sperm and pollen are male gametes; eggs and ova are female. **Fertilisation** is the fusion of two gametes to form a zygote. *Quick check:* Why do gametes have half the normal chromosome number?

**2. Specialised gamete structures.** A sperm has a tail for swimming, a head with packed DNA, and mitochondria for energy. An egg is large with stored nutrients and a protective coat. A pollen grain has a tough wall to survive air or insect transport. *Quick check:* What structural feature lets pollen survive long journeys between flowers?

**3. Reproductive organs in flowering plants.** Flowers contain stamens (anthers produce pollen, filaments support them) and carpels (stigma receives pollen, style is the path, ovary holds ovules). Pollination delivers pollen to stigma; double fertilisation forms seed and endosperm. *Quick check:* Why are flowering plants often colourful or fragrant?

**4. Reproductive organs in mammals.** Testes produce sperm; ovaries produce eggs. Hormonal cycles coordinate timing. Internal fertilisation and gestation protect developing offspring. *Quick check:* Why might internal fertilisation be an advantage on land?

**5. Worked example: comparing kangaroo paw and red kangaroo reproduction.** Kangaroo paw (*Anigozanthos*): bird-pollinated flowers, with the stigma positioned to brush the head of feeding honeyeaters. Red kangaroo (*Osphranter rufus*): joeys born early and complete development in the pouch; can pause embryonic development under drought (embryonic diapause). *Follow the thinking:* identify gametes → match structures to function → list trade-offs → describe ecological context.

**6. Asexual reproduction.** **Asexual reproduction** produces offspring from one parent. Examples: budding in yeast, runners in strawberries, fragmentation in some sea stars, parthenogenesis in some lizards. It is fast and energy-efficient but creates no new variation. *Quick check:* Why is asexual reproduction risky in a changing environment?

**7. Two strategies, one species.** Many species use both. Strawberries spread by runners (asexual) but also produce seeds (sexual). Aphids reproduce asexually for most of the year but switch to sexual reproduction in autumn. The strategy depends on conditions. *Quick check:* Why is having both strategies sometimes a hedge?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria conserves rare plants and runs public programs on flower structure. Phillip Island Nature Parks monitors reproduction in Little Penguins, whose breeding success links to ocean health.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and other Victorian peoples have long-running knowledge of plant reproductive cycles, including yam-daisy management and seed harvesting. The Indigenous Knowledge Institute publishes peer-reviewed work on these practices. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Migrant home gardeners across Melbourne propagate fruit and vegetables using both seeds (sexual) and cuttings (asexual). Vietnamese, Italian and Greek family gardens are rich classrooms of plant reproduction.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Asexual means simple." No. It is often complex and well-suited to certain environments.
- "Eggs are passive." No. They actively support fertilisation and early development.
- "Only animals have sex." No. Plants and many other organisms do.
- "Variation only matters in pests." No. Variation is the raw material for adaptation across all species.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why gametes have half the normal chromosome number.* Fertilisation restores full chromosome number. Halving (meiosis) keeps total chromosomes constant across generations.
2. *Pollen survival feature.* A tough outer wall (exine) protects pollen during travel by wind, water or insects.
3. *Why flowers are often colourful or fragrant.* To attract pollinators that transfer pollen between flowers, supporting fertilisation.
4. *Internal fertilisation on land.* It protects gametes from drying out and reduces exposure to predators or pathogens.
5. *Why asexual reproduction is risky in change.* No new variation means the population cannot adapt quickly to new conditions.
6. *Why "both strategies" hedges bets.* When conditions are stable, asexual is efficient; when conditions change, sexual reproduction provides variation for survival.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Bee on lavender.* Pollination: pollen sticks to the bee's body, transferring to the next flower's stigma. The bee gains nectar; the flower gains pollen movement.
2. *Knox native-plant corridor.* Diverse plants support diverse pollinators, which improves cross-pollination across species and through the season. A monoculture limits pollination windows.
3. *Strawberry breeder.* Use sexual reproduction (crossing two varieties) to generate new combinations, then asexual reproduction (runners) to propagate the successful new variety reliably.
4. *IVF in conservation breeding.* Issues: animal welfare and consent (for human-mediated reproduction), and effects of low genetic diversity in captive populations. Programs must balance immediate survival with long-term diversity.
5. *Wadawurrung seed collection.* Discuss with Elder permission and credit. Explain seed dormancy, viability and ecological context in scientific terms while keeping Wadawurrung leadership of the cultural framing. Use only published, community-approved material.
