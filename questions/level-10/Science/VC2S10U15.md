## Core content

**1. Conservation of energy.** The total energy in a closed system stays constant; it just changes form or location. This is one of the most reliable laws in physics. *Quick check:* Why is "perpetual motion" impossible by this law?

**2. Transfer vs transformation.** **Transfer** moves energy without changing form (heat from a pan to your hand). **Transformation** changes form (chemical to thermal in a candle, electrical to light in a globe). *Quick check:* Is sunlight warming the bricks of a Melbourne wall a transfer, a transformation, or both?

**3. Efficiency.** **Efficiency** = useful energy out ÷ total energy in, often as a percentage. Modern LEDs are about 30% efficient; an incandescent bulb about 5%. Real systems lose some energy as heat or sound. *Quick check:* If a kettle uses 100 kJ and heats water with 90 kJ, what is its efficiency?

**4. Why heat appears everywhere.** Friction, electrical resistance and air drag all produce heat. The second law of thermodynamics says useful energy decreases as it spreads out. Engineers manage these losses. *Quick check:* Why do brakes get hot?

**5. Worked example: a hydroelectric dam.** Water held high has gravitational potential energy. Falling, it gains kinetic energy. Turbines convert kinetic to mechanical to electrical energy. Some energy lost to turbulence and heat. Hydroelectric efficiency is high (often 80–90%) compared with combustion engines (25–40%). *Follow the thinking:* identify form at each stage → estimate losses → calculate overall efficiency.

**6. Sankey diagrams.** A **Sankey diagram** shows energy flows as arrows whose width is proportional to the energy. They make losses visible. Use them for cars, light bulbs and home heating. *Quick check:* Why is a Sankey diagram clearer than a list of percentages?

**7. Implications for design.** Improving efficiency reduces energy demand, emissions and cost. Insulation, regenerative braking, heat-pump heating and LED lighting all exploit conservation by capturing or limiting losses. *Quick check:* Why does a heat pump have efficiency above 100% in the everyday sense?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Sustainability Victoria publishes home and school energy audits. The Snowy Hydro Snowy 2.0 project (in NSW but supplying Victoria) illustrates pumped-hydro storage. Victorian power-station efficiency varies: brown-coal around 25–30%, modern combined-cycle gas 50–60%.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander practices of cool burning, food preparation and shelter design demonstrate energy-efficient management of Country. The Indigenous Knowledge Institute publishes peer-reviewed work on these. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Vietnamese-Australian, Italian-Australian and Sudanese-Australian communities across Melbourne use seasonal cooking, drying and preserving methods that reflect long-running attention to energy use.

## Memorable takeaways
- Energy never disappears.
- Some always becomes thermal.
- Efficiency = useful out / total in.
- Sankey makes losses visible.
- Smart design captures the losses.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Energy is created by power stations." No. They transform stored energy into electrical energy.
- "Efficiency over 100% is impossible." It is, in the strict sense; heat pumps appear over 100% only because they move heat, not generate it.
- "Heat is wasted energy." Often, but heat is also useful energy in heaters and cookers.
- "Renewable means efficient." Not directly; some renewables have lower efficiency but lower fuel cost and emissions.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why perpetual motion is impossible.* Real systems lose useful energy to friction and heat. Without a continual input, motion eventually stops.
2. *Sunlight on bricks.* Both. Light is transferred from Sun to bricks, then transformed into thermal energy as the bricks absorb it.
3. *Kettle efficiency.* 90%.
4. *Why brakes get hot.* Kinetic energy of the vehicle is converted into thermal energy through friction in the brake pads.
5. *Why Sankey diagrams are clearer.* Arrow widths show proportions at a glance, making losses obvious.
6. *Heat pump above 100% appearance.* It moves more thermal energy than the electrical energy it consumes; it does not create energy, it transfers it.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Walking upstairs.* Chemical energy in food → metabolised in muscle cells → kinetic energy of legs → gravitational potential energy of body. Losses: heat from muscles, sound, deformation.
2. *Banyule sports centre.* LED lighting (electrical → light efficiency), heat-pump replacement of gas heating, insulation upgrades to reduce thermal losses.
3. *Water utility pumping.* Choose efficient pumps matched to flow needs; avoid unnecessary pressure losses; schedule pumping to off-peak times if grid emissions allow.
4. *School solar.* Equity: who pays for the system and who benefits (school community vs renters at the school). Ethics: end-of-life recycling for panels and batteries.
5. *Wadawurrung cool burning.* Discuss with permission and credit. The technique releases energy at a low rate, reducing the chance of high-energy wildfires later. Use only published, community-approved material.
