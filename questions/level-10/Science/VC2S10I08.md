# VC2S10I08 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10I08. Communicate and justify scientific ideas, findings and arguments by selecting appropriate formats, vocabulary, conventions and representations for diverse audiences.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - The audience determines the message.
  - Justification needs both clarity and evidence.
  - Conventions are the shared grammar of science.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Literacy; Digital Literacy; Intercultural Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **communicate** and **justify** scientific ideas, findings and arguments by **selecting** appropriate **formats**, **vocabulary**, **conventions** and **representations** for **diverse audiences**.
- You are learning to make your science readable, defensible and useful to the people who need it.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **format**, **convention** and **justification** in your own words at Year 10 level.
- Choose a format, vocabulary and representation for a stated audience.
- Apply standard scientific conventions, including units, citations and figure labels.
- Adapt one finding for three different audiences without losing accuracy.

## Core content

**1. Audience first, format second.** Before you write, identify your reader's needs, time and prior knowledge. A medical resident, a councillor and a Year 5 class all need the same finding, told differently. *Quick check:* What changes between writing for peers and writing for primary-school students?

**2. Format choices.** A **format** is the type of communication: scientific report, policy brief, infographic, video, social post, public talk. Each carries trade-offs in length, depth and reach. *Quick check:* Which format suits a 90-second story on a new finding for social media?

**3. Vocabulary and register.** Use technical words when they save explanation, and define them on first use. Match register to the audience: formal for scientific reports, plain language for the public, careful neutrality for policy briefs. *Quick check:* When would "transpiration" be the right word, and when would "water moving through plants" be better?

**4. Scientific conventions.** Units in SI form; figure and table captions describe what is shown and the take-home; citations follow a consistent style; uncertainty is reported with the result. These are the shared grammar of science. *Quick check:* Why are conventions worth following even when no one is grading you?

**5. Worked example: a study on microplastics in Port Phillip Bay.** Finding: microplastic counts vary by beach and tide. Audience 1: scientific report for a journal: methods, results, figures, references. Audience 2: a council brief: one chart, two paragraphs, three actions. Audience 3: a TikTok explainer: 90 seconds, one strong visual, one call to action. Same data, same accuracy, three artefacts. *Follow the thinking:* identify audience → choose format → adapt vocabulary → respect conventions → keep accuracy.

**6. Justification.** **Justification** explains why you reached your conclusion. It connects evidence and reasoning, names uncertainty and acknowledges alternatives. *Quick check:* Why is "this shows that…" weaker than "this evidence supports… because… acknowledging…"?

**7. Representations beyond words.** Tables, graphs, diagrams, photos and models all communicate. Captions guide the eye. Use full axes unless cropping is justified and explained. *Quick check:* Why does a misleading chart undermine the science behind it?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Museums Victoria, the Royal Society of Victoria and the Doherty Institute model multi-audience communication. Many Victorian researchers publish papers and accessible explainers in parallel.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** First Languages Australia and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish guidance on respectful language and visuals when communicating science involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and Country. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** SBS Radio communicates science in over 60 languages. Bilingual public-health teams in Melbourne adapt formats and vocabulary across Vietnamese, Arabic, Mandarin and Dinka audiences during health campaigns.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You finished a Year 10 ecology project. Adapt the key finding for your science teacher, your grandparent and a Year 5 student.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Yarra plans a urban-cooling campaign. Write a 70-word public Facebook post and a 200-word council brief on the same data.
3. **Vocational.** A junior media officer at a Victorian hospital prepares a press release. Outline three conventions she should follow and one common mistake to avoid.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to publicly identify a private business in their pollution study. Identify two ethical risks and propose a respectful way to communicate the finding.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung Traditional Owners and your class co-produce a community report on cool burning. Outline three steps for respectful, accurate communication.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "Audience: ___. Purpose: ___. Format: ___. Key data: ___. Caption: ___. Action: ___."
- **Words to keep close.** audience, format, register, convention, citation, caption, justification.
- **Try this picture.** Draw three audience cards for the same finding: peer, public, primary student. Note format, vocabulary and one visual change for each.
- **A different way to show what you know.** Build the three artefacts (report, brief, social post) for one finding and compare them.

## Stretch yourself
Take one of your own findings or a published Australian science finding. Produce three short artefacts on the same finding: a 280-character social post, a 200-word policy brief, and a 600-word peer-style report. Justify each adaptation in two sentences.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More charts means better communication." No. Each chart must earn its space.
- "Plain language means dumbed down." No. Plain language is clear, not shallow.
- "Conventions slow people down." No. They speed shared understanding.
- "Public communication does not need accuracy." No. Public trust depends on it.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Peers vs primary students.* Length, vocabulary, examples, pace and number of visuals all change. Accuracy stays the same.
2. *90-second social media story.* Short video format with on-screen captions and one strong visual.
3. *"Transpiration" vs "water moving through plants".* Use the technical word when the audience already knows it or you define it once. Use the phrase for younger or non-specialist audiences.
4. *Why follow conventions.* They make work easier to read, compare and trust. They also speed up peer feedback.
5. *Why "this shows that…" is weak.* It claims more than the evidence may support and skips reasoning.
6. *Why a misleading chart undermines the science.* Readers cannot tell whether the underlying data are trustworthy.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Ecology project.* Teacher: full report with methods. Grandparent: a short narrative paragraph with one photo. Year 5 student: a picture-book page with three facts and one call to action.
2. *Yarra urban cooling.* Facebook: short stat, one image, one action. Brief: claim, two charts, three recommendations, sources.
3. *Hospital press release.* Conventions: accurate quotes, named spokesperson, embargo respected, statistics cited. Mistake: hype that overstates the finding.
4. *Pollution and a private business.* Risks: defamation and unfair targeting. Respectful approach: report aggregated industry-level data, with named regulator follow-up rather than individual naming.
5. *Wadawurrung cool-burning report.* Step 1: co-author with the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: use plain language with named credit. Step 3: review before public release.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Finding: nighttime urban heat persists longer in suburbs with less canopy.* Social post: short headline, one number, one map, call to action. Policy brief: 200 words with one chart and three actions. Peer-style report: methods, figures with captions, references. Justification names audience needs and the action each piece is asking for.
