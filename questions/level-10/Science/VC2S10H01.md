# VC2S10H01 — Science as a Human Endeavour

## Snapshot

- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Science as a Human Endeavour).

- You are learning to recognise where a claim sits in this process and how much weight it deserves.

## Core content

**1. Why public processes matter.** Private claims become public knowledge through transparent steps. Without those steps, errors and frauds spread. The public processes are slow on purpose. *Quick check:* Why is "trust me" not a scientific argument?

**2. Replication.** **Replication** is doing a study again, often by independent teams, to see whether the result holds. A single result is interesting; replicated results are dependable. The "replication crisis" of the 2010s in some fields revealed how often single results do not survive. *Quick check:* Why are independent replications stronger than the original lab repeating its own work?

**3. Peer review.** **Peer review** is critical reading by other experts before publication. Reviewers ask whether the methods, statistics and conclusions hold up. Peer review is imperfect but improves drafts substantially. *Quick check:* Why might peer review still miss flaws?

**4. Publication and the literature.** Publishing in peer-reviewed journals lets others replicate, build on or challenge a finding. Pre-prints (not yet peer-reviewed) appeared faster during COVID-19 but with extra caveats. *Quick check:* What is the trade-off between speed and rigour?

**5. Building consensus.** Across many studies, **scientific consensus** emerges when independent lines of evidence point the same way. Bodies like the IPCC summarise consensus on climate change; medical bodies do the same on vaccines and treatments. *Quick check:* Why is "many independent lines of evidence" different from "many studies"?

**6. Worked example: the Helicobacter pylori story revisited.** Marshall and Warren proposed a bacterial cause of stomach ulcers in the early 1980s. Replication, larger trials and broader peer review followed. Consensus shifted, treatment changed, and the work won the 2005 Nobel Prize. The same processes can also reject claims, as happened with cold fusion in 1989. *Follow the thinking:* claim → publication → independent replication → peer scrutiny → consensus or rejection.

**7. Open to challenge.** Even consensus knowledge can be revised by new evidence. Science is always open. Pseudoscience claims certainty that is not earned. Real expertise sounds humble about uncertainty. *Quick check:* Why is "settled science" sometimes a misleading phrase?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Murdoch Children's Research Institute, the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute and the Doherty Institute publish and replicate studies in their fields. Each has dedicated public engagement programs.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge systems have their own forms of validation through community elders, intergenerational testing and consensus. AIATSIS and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish work on these processes. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Researchers from Iranian, Vietnamese and Anglo-Australian backgrounds collaborate at Monash and Melbourne on cancer immunotherapy, often replicating each other's results across labs to build confidence in new treatments.

## Memorable takeaways
- Knowledge is built in public.
- Replication beats reputation.
- Peer review is filtering, not gatekeeping.
- Consensus is many lines, not many voices.
- Always open to revision.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Peer review proves a study is correct." No. It increases the chance, not the certainty.
- "Old findings cannot be wrong." No. Many old findings have been revised by better methods or data.
- "Consensus is just opinion." No. Scientific consensus emerges from independent lines of evidence over time.
- "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge has no validation." No. It uses different processes including elders, community testing, and intergenerational consensus.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "trust me" is not scientific.* Science depends on transparent methods, open data, replication and peer review. Personal authority alone is not a check.
2. *Why independent replication is stronger.* It rules out lab-specific equipment, samples or biases. Multiple labs reaching the same answer is harder to explain by chance.
3. *Why peer review still misses flaws.* Reviewers are time-pressed; data can be hidden; statistical errors can go unnoticed. It improves drafts but does not certify them.
4. *Trade-off between speed and rigour.* Faster sharing (pre-prints) helps urgent decisions but increases the risk of acting on flawed data. Rigour catches errors but takes time.
5. *Many lines vs many studies.* Independent lines of evidence draw on different methods and data sources. Many studies of the same kind can share the same flaw.
6. *Why "settled science" can mislead.* It implies finality. Even strongly supported ideas can be refined by new evidence. Better phrasing names the strength of agreement and remaining uncertainty.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Alzheimer's headline.* A single result needs independent replication. Has the study been peer reviewed in a major journal? What does the broader consensus say? Big claims need big evidence.
2. *Manningham water-treatment.* Demand peer-reviewed evidence of safety and effectiveness; require independent replication on Australian water; ensure operating standards meet ADWG and EPA requirements before adoption.
3. *Pharmacist reading contradictory studies.* Compare study quality (size, design), check both for peer review and replication, and consult systematic reviews or clinical guidelines that synthesise across studies.
4. *Pressure to overstate findings.* Issues: integrity breach, public-trust damage, harm if results misguide decisions. Safeguards: pre-registration of studies, independent statistical review, and institutional whistleblower protections.
5. *Yorta Yorta and Western validation.* Western peer review and replication apply within Western methods. Yorta Yorta validation includes elders' confirmation and intergenerational consensus. Co-authored outputs honouring both processes can build robust shared findings.
