## Core content

**1. Atoms rearrange.** A **chemical reaction** breaks bonds and forms new ones. Atoms are not created or destroyed; they swap partners. *Quick check:* Why do reactions change properties even though atoms remain the same?

**2. The Law of Conservation of Mass.** Mass before a reaction equals mass after. Antoine Lavoisier showed this in the late 1700s using sealed-vessel experiments. The principle holds for all chemical reactions. *Quick check:* Why does burning paper appear to "destroy mass" if you do not catch the gases?

**3. Balanced equations.** A **balanced equation** has equal numbers of each kind of atom on both sides. Coefficients (whole numbers in front) are adjusted to balance the equation. *Quick check:* Balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.

**4. Reactants and products.** The **reactants** are on the left; **products** are on the right. The arrow shows direction. State symbols (s, l, g, aq) describe phase. *Quick check:* What does (aq) mean in an equation?

**5. Worked example: combustion of methane.** Reactants: CH₄ + O₂. Products: CO₂ + H₂O. Balance: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Atom count: 1 C and 4 H on each side; 4 O on each side. Conservation of mass: total mass of reactants equals total mass of products. *Follow the thinking:* identify reactants and products → count each element → adjust coefficients → check totals.

**6. Predicting common product types.** Combustion: hydrocarbon + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Acid + base → salt + water. Metal + acid → salt + hydrogen gas. Decomposition: AB → A + B. *Quick check:* What products would you predict from sodium hydroxide reacting with hydrochloric acid?

**7. Why balancing matters.** Equations describe real chemistry. Unbalanced equations imply atoms appearing or disappearing. Balanced equations let chemists calculate quantities for industry, lab and medicine. *Quick check:* Why does a pharmacist need balanced equations to prepare a precise dose?

## Watch out for these traps
- "Mass disappears in fire." No. The gases produced have mass; closed systems prove it.
- "Coefficients can be any number." No. Use the smallest whole-number ratios.
- "Balanced equations describe rate." No. They describe stoichiometry, not speed.
- "State symbols are decoration." No. They describe what state the substance is in, which affects reactions and observations.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why properties change despite same atoms.* Bonds and arrangements differ in products, so molecules behave differently even with the same atoms.
2. *Burning paper "destroying" mass.* The carbon dioxide and water vapour escape into air; in a closed system, mass is conserved.
3. *Balancing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.* 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
4. *(aq) meaning.* Dissolved in water (aqueous solution).
5. *NaOH + HCl products.* NaCl + H₂O. Salt and water from acid–base neutralisation.
6. *Why pharmacists need balanced equations.* To calculate exact doses and ensure no leftover or harmful intermediates.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Cake CO₂.* NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. Already balanced (1 Na, 1 H, 1 C, 3 O on each side).
2. *Melton fireworks.* Combustion of magnesium (2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO), reactions of strontium salts (red colours), barium salts (green colours). Safety: distance from spectators, fire-fighting equipment, and trained operators.
3. *Buffer preparation.* Each component must be measured accurately; the equilibrium between weak acid and conjugate base depends on stoichiometric ratios. Errors lead to incorrect pH.
4. *"Burn it all" demo.* Issues: toxic emissions and lack of controlled conditions. Alternative: small, supervised combustion of known compounds in a fume hood with full risk assessment.
5. *Wadawurrung ochre heating.* Discuss with permission and credit. Heating dehydrates iron-oxide hydrates: Fe₂O₃·H₂O → Fe₂O₃ + H₂O. Use only published, community-approved material.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Reaction: combustion of methane in Victorian gas heaters.* CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. From 1 g of methane (about 0.0625 mol), about 2.75 g of CO₂ and 2.25 g of water are produced. Safety/environmental note: incomplete combustion can produce CO, requiring proper ventilation; all combustion adds CO₂ to the atmosphere.
