# VC2S10I05 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10I05. Analyse and compare processed data to identify and explain qualitative and quantitative patterns, trends, relationships and anomalies.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Look for both numbers and shapes in the data.
  - Compare with thought to context, not just statistics.
  - Anomalies deserve explanation, not deletion.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Critical and Creative Thinking; Digital Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **analyse** and **compare** processed data to **identify and explain** qualitative and quantitative **patterns**, **trends**, **relationships** and **anomalies**.
- You are learning to read your processed data carefully and connect what you see to the model behind the study.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **trend**, **relationship**, **anomaly** and **correlation** in your own words.
- Describe a trend with direction, magnitude, timeframe and uncertainty.
- Compare two datasets to identify a relationship and explain its plausibility.
- Investigate one anomaly with a likely cause and a follow-up test.

## Core content

**1. Qualitative and quantitative findings together.** Numbers are not the only data. Qualitative observations (colour, texture, behaviour) often guide interpretation of quantitative trends. *Quick check:* Why might recording shape alongside numbers help in a microscopy study?

**2. Describing a trend properly.** Direction (up/down/flat), magnitude (how much), timeframe (over what period) and uncertainty (range, confidence). "Annual mean temperature rose by about 1.2°C from 1960 to 2020, with year-to-year variability of about ±0.5°C." *Quick check:* Why is "it got hotter" weaker than the version above?

**3. Comparing datasets.** Putting two datasets side by side can reveal relationships. Always compare like with like: same definitions, methods, sample sizes. *Quick check:* Why would comparing 10 years of one site against 100 years of another mislead?

**4. Correlation vs causation.** **Correlation** is when two variables move together. Causation needs a mechanism and other evidence. Even strong correlation may reflect a common cause or chance. *Quick check:* Name one famous spurious correlation.

**5. Worked example: comparing two suburbs' canopy cover and heat.** Two Melbourne suburbs: Hampton (high canopy) and Sunshine (low canopy). Compare canopy percentage and summer maximum surface temperatures from 2015 to 2024. Quantitative trend: Sunshine warmed faster. Qualitative observation: Sunshine has more dark surfaces. Relationship: canopy correlates with cooler surface temperatures. Anomaly: a 2022 cool summer reduced the gap. Hypothesised cause: shade and evapotranspiration. Follow-up: control for population density and elevation. *Follow the thinking:* tidy → describe trend → compare → identify anomaly → propose mechanism.

**6. Anomalies as clues.** Anomalies can be measurement errors, recording errors, real outliers or signals of a rare event. Investigate before deleting. The right action is to explain. *Quick check:* What is one Australian dataset that showed a real anomaly that turned out to be important?

**7. Statistical caution.** Be cautious about overclaiming. A small effect or low sample size is fragile. Multiple comparisons inflate false positives. Use confidence intervals and pre-registered analyses where possible. *Quick check:* Why does running many sub-analyses produce some "significant" results by chance?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** EPA Victoria, Bureau of Meteorology Australia and Department of Health publish long-running datasets that students can analyse. The 2009 Melbourne heatwave dataset is widely used in school studies.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Long-running observations by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge holders capture qualitative and quantitative changes in Country, such as seasonal indicators and species abundance. The Indigenous Knowledge Institute and Country Needs People publish peer-reviewed work on these patterns. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual public-health teams across Melbourne track flu, asthma and heat-illness data in Vietnamese, Mandarin and Arabic communities. Their analyses inform language-appropriate responses.

## Memorable takeaways
- Quantitative and qualitative together.
- Direction, magnitude, timeframe, uncertainty.
- Compare like with like.
- Correlation hints. Causation needs more.
- Investigate, then explain anomalies.

## Stretch yourself
Take two related public datasets (e.g. rainfall and bushfire counts). Identify one relationship, one trend, one anomaly. Discuss confounding factors and propose a follow-up. 250 words or one annotated dual figure.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Two things moving together must be linked." No. Coincidence is common, especially over short timeframes.
- "Anomalies are mistakes." No. Investigate first.
- "Bigger numbers always mean stronger evidence." No. Sample size, scatter and study design also matter.
- "Aboriginal ecological observations are anecdotal." No. They are long-running, systematic and increasingly published in peer-reviewed work.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why record shape alongside numbers.* Visual differences (e.g., morphology) can explain or warn about quantitative results.
2. *"It got hotter" vs proper trend statement.* The proper version states direction, size, timeframe and uncertainty, making it testable and useful.
3. *Why a 10-year vs 100-year comparison can mislead.* Different averaging periods include different climate patterns, inflating or hiding effects.
4. *A famous spurious correlation.* Per-capita cheese consumption and number of people who died by becoming tangled in bedsheets (a textbook example of coincidence).
5. *Australian anomaly that mattered.* A spike in deaths during the 2009 Melbourne heatwave revealed a real public-health crisis, not a data error.
6. *Why multiple comparisons inflate false positives.* Running many tests increases the chance that some appear significant by chance alone, even when no real effect exists.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Screen time vs sleep.* Plot scatter, compute correlation. Note any nights where short sleep followed low screen time (anomaly) and explore other causes (exams, illness).
2. *Frankston attendance vs bus reliability.* Compare suburbs; visualise as scatter. Confounder: socioeconomic factors affecting attendance regardless of bus reliability.
3. *Stain method comparison.* Use paired analysis on the same slides; visualise differences with box plots; flag pairs where the difference is unusually large for re-staining.
4. *Grades by ethnicity.* Issue: stigmatising minority groups. Alternative: examine school-resourcing differences against grades to look at structural factors.
5. *Yorta Yorta bird arrivals.* Describe long-term observations of seasonal arrival, the trend of earlier arrival in recent decades, and any missing years. Use ranger and modern monitoring data together, aligning language with the corporation's own reports.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Datasets: Victorian summer rainfall and bushfire count, 2000–2024.* Trend: increasing bushfire days. Relationship: lower seasonal rainfall correlates with more fire days. Anomaly: 2019–20 stands out for severity beyond rainfall alone, suggesting other factors (heatwave, fuel build-up). Follow-up: test fuel-moisture index as an additional predictor.
