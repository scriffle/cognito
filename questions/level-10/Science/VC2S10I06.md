# VC2S10I06 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10I06. Evaluate the validity and reproducibility of investigation methods and conclusions by identifying assumptions, biases, errors and areas of uncertainty.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Evaluation is the second half of analysis.
  - Validity, reproducibility and bias each ask different questions.
  - Naming uncertainty strengthens the conclusion.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **evaluate** the **validity** and **reproducibility** of investigation methods and conclusions by **identifying assumptions, biases, errors and areas of uncertainty**.
- You are learning to grade your own and others' work with structured questions.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **validity**, **reproducibility**, **bias** and **uncertainty** in your own words at Year 10 level.
- Identify two assumptions in any school investigation.
- Distinguish a methodological limit from a real-world limitation.
- Suggest a specific follow-up that would address one major weakness.

## Core content

**1. Validity revisited.** **Validity** asks whether the study measures what it claims. A reaction-time test that times button presses on a slow keyboard may be measuring keyboard latency, not reaction time. *Quick check:* What is one validity threat in a school self-report survey?

**2. Reproducibility revisited.** **Reproducibility** asks whether the same approach produces similar results. Independent replication is stronger than internal repeats. *Quick check:* Why is "I did it twice myself" less convincing than "two other groups replicated"?

**3. Bias and confounding.** Selection bias, observer bias and confirmation bias each distort findings. **Confounding** is when a third variable explains an apparent relationship. *Quick check:* If students who eat breakfast score higher in tests, what is one possible confounder?

**4. Errors revisited.** Random errors are reduced by repetition. Systematic errors require calibration or design changes. Both shape the conclusion. *Quick check:* Which kind of error survives many repetitions of the same flawed method?

**5. Worked example: evaluating a "phones and grades" survey.** A class collects self-reported phone use and recent grades. Validity: self-report is imperfect; grades may not capture learning. Reproducibility: another class could repeat with the same survey, but answers may shift with context. Bias: respondents who feel strongly may answer more; selection bias. Confounding: sleep, socio-economic status, study habits. Uncertainty: small sample, narrow range of grades. Follow-up: use phone-app logs and standardised tests in a larger sample with consent. *Follow the thinking:* validity → reproducibility → bias → confounding → uncertainty → follow-up.

**6. Uncertainty as a feature.** Naming uncertainty (sample size, measurement precision, generalisability) helps readers judge the conclusion. *Quick check:* Why is "this study is preliminary, larger studies are needed" often the most honest closing line?

**7. Methodological vs real-world limits.** A methodological limit is something you could fix with better design. A real-world limit reflects what the system allows. *Quick check:* Which limit is "you cannot afford a calibrated sensor"?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** EPA Victoria, the Department of Health and the Bureau of Meteorology Australia publish peer-reviewed reports with explicit evaluation sections, including limits and uncertainties. Reading one is a useful template for school work.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Country Needs People and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish program evaluations that list cultural protocols and limitations. These show how Western evaluation frameworks can sit alongside community-led review. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Researchers from Greek-Australian, Lebanese-Australian and Anglo-Australian backgrounds in Melbourne hospitals publicly publish weaknesses in their early-stage trials and update protocols accordingly.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A friend claims a new running shoe makes them faster. List two assumptions, two biases and one follow-up that would test the claim.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Greater Geelong concludes from a six-week survey that residents support a major land-use change. Evaluate the evidence and suggest a stronger evaluation.
3. **Vocational.** A junior pathologist reports a new diagnostic method. Outline three checks she should make before publishing.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to use existing health data to study a sensitive question. Identify two ethical issues and propose a safer evaluation method.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung rangers and a university team report different conclusions about waterbird recovery. Outline a process that combines evaluation fairly.

## Memorable takeaways
- Evaluate as you go.
- Validity, reproducibility, bias, error, uncertainty.
- Confounding hides in plain sight.
- Name limits in the conclusion.
- "Suggests" beats "proves".

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "An assumption was ___. A bias risk is ___. A confounder might be ___. An error source is ___. A follow-up that addresses this is ___."
- **Words to keep close.** validity, reproducibility, bias, confounding, random error, systematic error, uncertainty, generalisability.
- **Try this picture.** Draw a five-box "evaluation card": validity, reproducibility, bias, error, follow-up. Add one specific item to each before submitting any conclusion.

## Stretch yourself
Pick one published Victorian science article. Identify two assumptions, one source of error, one piece of conflicting evidence and one unanswered question. Suggest a follow-up study. 250 words.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Evaluation is just self-criticism." No. It is what makes a finding trustworthy.
- "Confounding only happens in human studies." No. It can affect any observational study.
- "Bigger samples remove all bias." No. Bias is non-random; bigger samples do not always fix it.
- "Uncertainty undermines authority." No. Quantified uncertainty supports trust.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Validity threat in self-report.* Social desirability bias, where respondents answer to look good rather than honestly.
2. *Why internal repeats are weaker.* They cannot rule out lab-specific quirks. Independent replication tests the broader claim.
3. *Confounder for breakfast and grades.* Household routines: families that ensure breakfast may also ensure homework and sleep, which influence grades.
4. *Error that survives repetition.* Systematic error: a miscalibrated sensor will keep giving biased results no matter how many trials you run.
5. *Why "preliminary, larger studies needed" is honest.* It tells readers the study is a useful start, not a final answer.
6. *Cost-limited sensor.* Real-world limit. The science could be done better with more resources, but design and analysis can still strengthen the work.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Faster running shoe.* Assumptions: same conditions, no fitness change. Biases: placebo, selection of fast days. Follow-up: paired runs on the same route at the same pace target, with and without the shoe, repeated across weeks.
2. *Greater Geelong survey.* Limit: six weeks is short, sample may be self-selected. Stronger evaluation: multi-method study with stratified sampling, in-person interviews and longer time window.
3. *Pathology diagnostic method.* Compare with current standard on blinded samples; check inter-operator agreement; calculate sensitivity and specificity.
4. *Sensitive health-data project.* Issues: privacy and consent. Method: use de-identified aggregate data, governed by an ethics committee with patient representation.
5. *Wadawurrung waterbird disagreement.* Hold a joint review meeting; table methods, sample sites and assumptions; agree shared and different conclusions; co-publish with named contributors and joint recommendations.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Article: an EPA Victoria report on PFAS in groundwater.* Two assumptions: laboratory detection limits stable over time; sampled sites represent the catchment. Source of error: variability between sampling teams. Conflicting evidence: a community-led dataset showed a higher level at one site. Unanswered question: how do PFAS levels change across seasons? Follow-up: monthly sampling across one full year, with paired community-led sampling at the same sites.
