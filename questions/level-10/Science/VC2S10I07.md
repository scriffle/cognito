# VC2S10I07 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10I07. Construct evidence-based arguments that support conclusions or evaluate claims, while considering ethical issues and cultural protocols for using secondary data.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**

  - Secondary data requires its own checks.
  - Ethics and cultural protocols shape what you can responsibly say.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **construct evidence-based arguments** that **support** conclusions or **evaluate** claims, while **considering ethical issues and cultural protocols** for using **secondary data**.
- You are learning to write tight, fair arguments that earn their conclusions.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **claim**, **evidence**, **reasoning** and **secondary data** in your own words at Year 10 level.
- Construct a CER paragraph that anticipates counter-evidence.
- Evaluate the source, methods and consent status of secondary data.
- Identify one ethical or cultural protocol that affects how you can use a dataset.

## Core content

**1. Claim, evidence, reasoning at Year 10.** A **claim** is specific and testable. **Evidence** is data that fit the claim. **Reasoning** connects them through a mechanism. Year 10 arguments also acknowledge counter-evidence and assess weight, not just direction. *Quick check:* Why is "the data show…" weaker than "the data show… because…"?

**2. Strong vs weak claims.** Strong claims are precise about magnitude, scope and timeframe. Weak claims are sweeping. *Quick check:* Rewrite "exercise helps mental health" into a more testable Year 10 claim.

**3. Matching evidence to claim.** A claim about a relationship needs paired measurements. A claim about change over time needs long-term data. A claim about cause needs both correlation and mechanism, ideally tested. *Quick check:* What kind of evidence would best support "shade reduces playground surface temperature"?

**4. Secondary data and its limits.** **Secondary data** is data collected by someone else. The Bureau of Meteorology Australia, ABS, EPA Victoria and academic open-data repositories are common sources. Always check: who, when, how, what was excluded, peer review, version. *Quick check:* Why is the date of access part of a citation?

**5. Worked example: are heatwaves becoming more frequent in Melbourne?** Claim: "Heatwaves in Melbourne are becoming more frequent, increasing public-health risk." Evidence: Bureau of Meteorology Australia long-term data, Department of Health hospital admissions during heatwaves, peer-reviewed analyses of trends. Reasoning: rising baseline temperatures shift the distribution, making extreme heat days more common; elderly and chronically ill people are more vulnerable. Counter-evidence: year-to-year variability is large; some recent summers were mild. Conclusion: trend is robust over multi-decade analysis with named uncertainty. Ethics and protocols: aggregate hospital data; cite Department of Health and BoM sources by date. *Follow the thinking:* claim → relevant evidence → mechanism → counter-evidence → ethics → conclusion.

**6. Ethical and cultural data care.** Reuse of data still requires consent and care. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander data carries additional cultural protocols. The CARE Principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) guide use. *Quick check:* Why is "open data" not the same as "free to use however"?

**7. Avoiding hype and underclaim.** Hype overstates. Underclaim ignores robust evidence. Both fail readers. Year 10 arguments name evidence weight and limits in the same paragraph. *Quick check:* Why is "the data strongly suggest, with the limits below" often the most useful framing?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** EPA Victoria, the Department of Health and the Bureau of Meteorology Australia publish peer-reviewed reports with clear evidence-and-limits structures. Reading one is a useful template.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS and the Indigenous Knowledge Institute publish CARE-aligned guidelines for Indigenous data governance. Victorian Traditional Owner corporations have published consent and protocol frameworks for research and reporting. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Public-health researchers in Melbourne have built ethical secondary-data pipelines for multilingual communities, with translated consent and feedback to participating communities.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A friend posts that "vaccines do not reduce flu cases". Construct a CER paragraph using public sources and name one counter-evidence point you considered.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Manningham debates installing bike lanes. Build an evidence-based argument using local crash and air-quality data, citing sources and dates.
3. **Vocational.** A junior data analyst at a Victorian hospital uses de-identified data to argue for a new screening program. Outline three things to check before publishing.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to compare student-mental-health data from two schools. Identify two ethical issues and propose an alternative that uses only aggregate data.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A team uses ranger-collected wildlife data to argue for a conservation plan. Outline three steps to comply with CARE principles.

## Memorable takeaways
- Claim, evidence, reasoning.
- Anticipate counter-evidence.
- Cite source and access date.
- Open data is not a free pass.
- Acknowledge limits in the same paragraph.

## If you need more support

- **Try this picture.** Build a "CER+ ladder" with five rungs: claim, evidence, reasoning, counter-evidence, limits.

## Stretch yourself
Pick a current Australian science claim. Find one supporting and one complicating piece of secondary data. Build a 250-word CER+ response that handles the conflict honestly, cites sources by name and date, and names ethical or cultural considerations.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More evidence is always better." No. Relevant, well-cited evidence beats a long list.
- "If a graph is in the news, it is reliable." No. Always check source, date and method.
- "Open data has no ethics." No. Re-identification, stereotyping and misuse remain risks.
- "Strong claims are bold." Only if the evidence supports them. Otherwise they are reckless.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "the data show…" needs "because…".* Reasoning explains the mechanism that links data to claim. Without it, two readers can interpret the same data differently.
2. *Stronger version of "exercise helps mental health".* "Adolescents who do at least 60 minutes of moderate physical activity most days have lower rates of depressive symptoms in studies of Australian school students, with an effect size that is small to moderate."
3. *Best evidence for "shade reduces playground surface temperature".* Paired temperature measurements at the same surface in sun and shade, at the same hour, across multiple sites and trials.
4. *Why citation date matters.* Conditions and methods change. Old data may not represent current realities; new versions may use different definitions.
5. *Why open data needs ethics.* Open access does not waive consent or protect against re-identification. Misuse can still cause harm.
6. *Why naming limits is useful.* Honest framing builds trust and helps readers act on appropriate confidence levels.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Vaccines and flu.* Claim: vaccines reduce flu severity and incidence at the population level. Evidence: peer-reviewed studies showing vaccine-effectiveness percentages. Reasoning: vaccines prime immune response, reducing severe outcomes. Counter-evidence: effectiveness varies by year due to virus mutation. Conclusion: vaccines reduce risk, with variability, supported by sustained evidence.
2. *Manningham bike lanes.* Use VicRoads crash data and EPA air-quality data, cite by year. CER paragraph claims that protected bike lanes correlate with fewer cyclist injuries and modest air-quality improvements in similar Australian cities; counter-evidence includes implementation costs and short-term congestion.
3. *Hospital screening proposal.* Check de-identification standards, sample bias, peer-reviewed effectiveness for the population in question, and cost-effectiveness reporting.
4. *Two-school mental-health comparison.* Issues: privacy and stigma. Alternative: school-level aggregate data with ethics-board approval, no individual identifiers, and consent processes for any new survey.
5. *Ranger-collected wildlife data and CARE.* Step 1: written agreement with the Traditional Owner corporation. Step 2: shared authorship and data ownership. Step 3: report-back to community before public release.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Claim: Victoria's renewable energy generation increased substantially between 2014 and 2024.* Supporting data: Australian Energy Regulator reports for Victoria. Complicating data: capacity-factor variability across seasons. CER+ response names the trend, the limits, and the methodological notes from the regulator. Sources cited with publication date. Ethical note: data are aggregated and publicly licensed; no individual or community is identifiable.
