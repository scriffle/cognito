# VC2S10H04 — Science as a Human Endeavour

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10H04. Scientific knowledge can be interpreted differently by different groups in society, and societal values and needs can influence which research gets funded and pursued.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Science as a Human Endeavour).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Interpretation depends on context, values and need.
  - Funding shapes what gets studied.
  - "Pure" science still sits in society.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Ethical Capability; Intercultural Capability; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that scientific knowledge can be **interpreted differently** by different groups in society, and that **societal values and needs** can **influence** which research gets **funded and pursued**.
- You are learning to read scientific debates with attention to who is asking what and why.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **interpretation**, **funding bias** and **research agenda** in your own words.
- Give one example where the same data is interpreted differently by different groups.
- Identify three factors that influence research funding.
- Explain why under-funded fields can leave knowledge gaps.

## Core content

**1. The same data, two readings.** **Interpretation** means how data are read in context. The same climate data can be read by economists, ecologists and farmers in different ways, each useful for different decisions. *Quick check:* Why does asking "for whom" change how data are read?

**2. Values inside science.** Researchers bring values: which problems matter, which methods feel right, which uncertainties they tolerate. Naming values does not invalidate science; ignoring them does. *Quick check:* Why is "view from nowhere" not a useful description of any researcher?

**3. Funding shapes the agenda.** **Funding bias** is when topics are studied because money is available, not because they matter most. The reverse is also true: under-funded fields stay quiet. The Australian Research Council and the National Health and Medical Research Council shape much of national research output. *Quick check:* Why might "neglected diseases" stay neglected even with strong scientific interest?

**4. Worked example: COVID-19 research priorities.** During 2020–2022, billions of dollars went into vaccines, antivirals and public-health surveillance. This produced rapid breakthroughs. At the same time, many other infectious-disease and chronic-disease projects were paused or delayed. Some communities (children, rural) and topics (long COVID, mental health) caught up later as values and evidence shifted. *Follow the thinking:* identify the priority → identify what got less attention → identify whose needs were met first → ask what should change next time.

**5. Differing interpretations across communities.** Climate change, vaccination, fluoridation and GM foods are interpreted differently across communities. Often the disagreement is about values (risk tolerance, cost) rather than data. *Quick check:* Why does framing a debate as "facts vs feelings" usually obscure rather than clarify?

**6. Power and access.** Who collects the data, who runs the lab, who reviews the papers and who reads them shapes what counts as knowledge. Diverse research communities catch blind spots. *Quick check:* Why does diversity in research workforces lead to a richer evidence base?

**7. Holding it together.** Science remains the best tool for testing claims about how the world works. Recognising values does not slip into "everything is opinion". It strengthens the work. *Quick check:* Why does naming bias make science more, not less, trustworthy?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victorian universities and the Melbourne-based Doherty Institute publish research priorities openly. Sustainability Victoria's strategic plan reflects current public values on climate and waste.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** AIATSIS publishes guidelines for funding, conducting and interpreting research with Indigenous communities. Joint-management research between Traditional Owner corporations and universities ensures community values shape research agendas. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Research with Vietnamese-Australian, Greek-Australian and Sudanese-Australian communities shows how chronic-disease research benefits when funding and methods reflect community priorities, not just university priorities.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** A friend says climate scientists are biased because they get climate funding. Reply in three sentences using clear distinctions about funding and replication.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Yarra plans to fund local research on traffic noise. Outline three values that should shape the agenda and one safeguard against bias.
3. **Vocational.** A junior policy analyst reviews competing economic studies of a proposed mine. Outline three things she should check about each study before recommending one.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates funding cancer research vs Alzheimer's research. Identify the ethical values in tension and propose a fair process for setting priorities.
5. **Cross-cultural.** A research project with Yorta Yorta knowledge holders interprets fish-population data. Outline how shared interpretation can strengthen the conclusions.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Funding always means bias." No. Independent peer review, transparency and replication can keep funded research honest.
- "If interpretation matters, science is just opinion." No. Some interpretations are better supported by evidence than others.
- "Diverse research teams are slower." No. They are often slower up front and faster overall, with fewer blind spots.
- "Aboriginal-led research lacks rigour." No. Community-led science applies different but equally rigorous validation processes.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "for whom" changes data.* Different audiences have different needs. The same temperature record matters differently to a winemaker, a planner and a public-health official.
2. *Why "view from nowhere" does not exist.* Every researcher works from somewhere: training, language, funding, time. Naming the position is honest, not biased.
3. *Why neglected diseases stay neglected.* They affect smaller, poorer or politically less visible groups. Funding follows where pressure and money point.
4. *Why "facts vs feelings" obscures debates.* It hides that disagreement is often about values (risk, cost, fairness) using shared facts.
5. *Why diversity catches blind spots.* Different backgrounds notice different unsafe assumptions. The result is more robust questions and findings.
6. *Why naming bias strengthens science.* Acknowledged biases can be checked. Hidden biases cannot.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Climate funding criticism.* Independent replication and transparent data limit funding bias. Most climate scientists are funded across many sources, and the conclusions hold across institutions and countries.
2. *Yarra traffic noise research.* Health, equity and amenity values should shape the agenda. Safeguard: independent panel including residents and academic peer reviewers.
3. *Mine economic studies.* Who funded it, what the assumptions are, what was excluded, and how independent the peer review was.
4. *Cancer vs Alzheimer's.* Values include burden of disease, fairness, scientific opportunity, equity. Fair process: a transparent national priority-setting body with patient and clinician input.
5. *Yorta Yorta and Western fish data.* Shared interpretation triangulates: ranger observations, ecological monitoring, traditional indicators. The combined picture is harder to dismiss and more useful for management.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Debate: synthetic fertiliser use in the Murray-Darling.* Two interpretations: agronomy (productivity, food security) vs ecology (eutrophication, biodiversity). Values: rural livelihoods vs water health. Funding context: industry-driven productivity research dominates; ecological research often charity- or government-funded. Recommended research priority: long-term controlled trials integrating yield, runoff and biodiversity, jointly funded.
