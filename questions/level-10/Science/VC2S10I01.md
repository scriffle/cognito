# VC2S10I01 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10I01. Formulate investigable questions, predictions and hypotheses that guide investigations aimed at testing and developing explanatory models.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Good questions test or develop explanatory models.
  - Predictions must follow from a model.
  - Hypotheses are about mechanisms, not just outcomes.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Critical and Creative Thinking; Numeracy; Literacy.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **formulate** investigable questions, predictions and hypotheses that **guide** investigations aimed at **testing and developing** explanatory models.
- You are learning to design questions that probe a model, not just describe an outcome.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **explanatory model**, **prediction** and **hypothesis** in your own words at Year 10 level.
- Write a hypothesis that names a mechanism.
- Distinguish a hypothesis-testing study from a model-developing study.
- Anticipate possible alternative explanations for your predicted result.

## Core content

**1. Models behind questions.** An **explanatory model** describes how and why a phenomenon happens. Examples: kinetic theory, plate tectonics, neuron action potentials. Good investigations test predictions of a model. *Quick check:* Why is "do plants grow with light?" a weaker question than "does light intensity affect photosynthesis rate via a measurable mechanism?"

**2. Predictions follow models.** A useful **prediction** is logically tied to the model: "If the model is right, we should see X under conditions Y". Predictions that any common-sense observation could meet are weak. *Quick check:* Why do strong predictions help rule out competing explanations?

**3. Hypotheses with mechanisms.** A Year 10 **hypothesis** does not just state "X affects Y"; it names the proposed mechanism: "If light intensity increases, then photosynthesis rate will increase, because more photons drive more electron-transport reactions, until enzymes become saturating." *Quick check:* What three parts make up a strong hypothesis at this level?

**4. Worked example: testing osmosis in potato cores.** Wondering: why do potato cores in salt water shrink? Investigable question: "How does the concentration of salt solution affect the change in mass of potato cores after 24 hours?" Prediction: as salt concentration rises, mass change shifts from gain to loss. Hypothesis: "If salt concentration in the surrounding solution is higher than inside the potato cells, then water will flow out, because water moves down its concentration gradient through cell membranes, leading to net mass loss." Variables: solution concentration (independent), mass change (dependent), time, potato variety, surface area (controlled). *Follow the thinking:* model → question → variables → prediction → mechanism.

**5. Hypothesis-testing vs model-developing.** Some investigations test an existing model. Others develop or refine a model from data. Climate scientists do both. School science most often tests, but year-10 students can also develop simple models. *Quick check:* Why is "I want to discover what happens" not yet a clear plan?

**6. Alternative explanations.** Strong scientists ask, "if my prediction holds, what else could explain the data?" Listing alternatives helps design controls. *Quick check:* What is one alternative explanation if a treatment group performs better than a control?

**7. Falsifiability.** A hypothesis must be **falsifiable**: data could show it is wrong. Untestable claims are not yet science. *Quick check:* Why is "the universe is run by an unknowable force" not a scientific hypothesis?

- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Traditional Owner corporations run scientific monitoring programs (water quality, fire ecology, bird counts) that combine investigable questions with long-term observation. ## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You wonder if your sourdough rises better at 25°C than 20°C. Write an investigable question, prediction and hypothesis with a mechanism.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Whittlesea plans a bee-friendly garden. Write an investigable question, hypothesis and three controlled variables for a comparison study.
3. **Vocational.** A junior researcher at Monash tests a new battery cathode. Outline her question, prediction and hypothesis, including a mechanism.
4. **Ethical.** A class wants to test which type of caffeine drink helps focus most. Reframe the study to avoid the ethical issue while still testing focus.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung rangers and a CSIRO team plan a soil-moisture study under different vegetation. Write a co-designed investigable question with named variables.

## Memorable takeaways
- A question worth asking tests a model.
- Predictions follow from mechanisms.
- "If… then… because…" with a mechanism.
- List alternative explanations early.
- Falsifiable or it is not science.

## If you need more support
- **Sentence stems to start your writing.** "Investigable question: ___. Hypothesis: If ___ then ___ because ___ (mechanism). Alternative explanation: ___. Test for it by ___."
- **Words to keep close.** model, hypothesis, mechanism, prediction, variable, falsifiability.
- **Try this picture.** Draw a "question funnel": phenomenon → explanatory model → testable prediction → variables → method. Add one example down each layer.

## Stretch yourself
Pick a real Victorian observation (e.g. summer mornings smelling of smoke, lichen patches on rocks, schoolyard heat). Build a chain from observation to model to investigable question to falsifiable hypothesis with mechanism. List two alternative explanations. 250 words or one annotated chain.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More general questions are stronger." No. Specific, narrow questions are more testable.
- "A prediction is the same as a hypothesis." No. The prediction states what you expect; the hypothesis adds the mechanism.
- "Model-developing studies have no hypothesis." Often, but they do have a working idea that the data refines.
- "Falsifiable means easy to disprove." No. It means the data could in principle show it wrong.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Stronger photosynthesis question.* The mechanism-focused version targets a measurable rate and links to a known model, so it can be tested and falsified.
2. *Why strong predictions rule out competing explanations.* They commit you to a specific outcome that other explanations would not produce.
3. *Three parts of a hypothesis at this level.* Independent variable change, dependent variable change, mechanism.
4. *Why "I want to discover what happens" is not a plan.* It does not name a model, prediction or method.
5. *Alternative explanation for treatment success.* Placebo effect, observer bias, selection differences between groups.
6. *Why the "unknowable force" is not scientific.* It cannot be falsified by any observation.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Sourdough.* "How does ambient temperature affect dough volume after 4 hours?" Prediction: 25°C produces greater rise. Hypothesis: "If temperature increases within an enzyme-friendly range, then yeast metabolism rate increases, because warmer temperatures speed up enzyme-catalysed reactions until denaturation begins."
2. *Whittlesea bee-friendly garden.* "Does the proportion of native flowering plants influence pollinator visits?" Hypothesis: "If native plant proportion increases, then native bee visits increase, because local species have co-evolved with native flora." Controlled: garden size, time of day, weather, observer protocol.
3. *Monash battery cathode.* "How does the new cathode formulation affect cycle life?" Prediction: more cycles before degradation. Hypothesis: "If the new cathode reduces dendrite formation, then capacity loss per cycle is lower, because dendrites are a primary degradation mechanism."
4. *Caffeine focus reframed.* "How does scheduled morning vs afternoon timing affect Year 10 students' performance on a 5-minute attention task?" The reframe avoids dosing peers while still testing factors that affect focus.
5. *Wadawurrung soil moisture.* "How does vegetation type (native grass, weed, bare soil) affect soil moisture at 10 cm depth across one week?" Variables: vegetation type (independent), moisture (dependent), site, depth, time, sampling method (controlled).

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Phenomenon: schoolyard heat varies between asphalt and grass.* Model: surface absorbs solar radiation differently and re-radiates as long-wave heat. Question: how does surface type affect surface temperature at midday? Hypothesis: "If surface albedo decreases, then surface temperature increases, because more solar energy is absorbed and re-emitted as heat." Alternative explanations: shade, soil moisture under grass.
