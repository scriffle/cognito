# VC2S10H03 — Science as a Human Endeavour

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10H03. Using science to address real-world sustainability issues can produce diverse outcomes, and the extent to which society adopts scientific solutions depends on their projected impacts.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Science as a Human Endeavour).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Science offers options, not finished answers.
  - Adoption depends on projected impacts, not just feasibility.
  - Diverse outcomes mean trade-offs need to be made openly.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Sustainability; Ethical Capability; Critical and Creative Thinking; Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures.

## What you are learning
- You are learning that using science to address **real-world sustainability** issues can produce **diverse outcomes**, and that **adoption** depends on **projected impacts**.
- You are learning to assess a proposed solution against measurable benefits, costs and risks across communities.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **sustainability**, **adoption** and **impact assessment** in your own words.
- Identify three projected impacts of one proposed sustainability solution.
- Compare two solutions for the same problem and recommend one.
- Explain why a workable solution may still not be adopted.

## Core content

**1. Science as a menu of options.** Most sustainability problems have several scientific responses. Solar, wind, hydro and nuclear all decarbonise electricity. Each has different costs, risks and timelines. *Quick check:* Why is "the science" not the same as "the answer"?

**2. Projected impacts.** **Impact assessment** estimates effects on environment, economy, society, health and equity. Numbers are uncertain, so ranges and assumptions are reported. *Quick check:* Why is naming the assumption as important as naming the number?

**3. Adoption depends on people.** Even effective solutions stall if people, businesses or governments do not act. Adoption is shaped by cost, trust, community engagement and politics. *Quick check:* Why is community engagement part of science, not extra?

**4. Worked example: cleaning up the Latrobe Valley.** Decisions about closing brown-coal plants in Victoria involve climate science (emissions), engineering (replacement supply), economics (cost of new infrastructure), social policy (worker transitions), and Indigenous policy (Country). Diverse projected outcomes mean different paths are debated: faster transition with more support, slower transition with cost protection, or rapid build-out of renewables and batteries. *Follow the thinking:* identify problem → list scientific options → project impacts → score by criteria → recommend with caveats.

**5. The role of timeframes.** Short-term costs may dominate political attention. Long-term benefits, like avoiding 2°C of warming, are harder to value. Science and policy often need both lenses. *Quick check:* Why might a 30-year benefit be discounted in a 4-year political cycle?

**6. Trade-offs across communities.** A solution that is best on average may still harm specific communities. Solar farms displace agricultural land. Mining for batteries can affect Country. Carbon prices can hurt lower-income households without rebates. *Quick check:* What is one design feature that protects vulnerable communities in a transition?

**7. When to act under uncertainty.** Decisions cannot wait for perfect data. The **precautionary principle** suggests acting to prevent severe, irreversible harm even with imperfect evidence. Adaptive management revises decisions as data improves. *Quick check:* Why do many environmental laws explicitly mention uncertainty?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Victoria's renewable-energy targets, the Solar Homes program and the Latrobe Valley Authority are all examples of science-informed sustainability decisions. Each has been peer-reviewed by independent bodies.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Victorian Traditional Owner corporations co-author land and water management plans that combine scientific monitoring with long-running cultural knowledge of Country. The Indigenous Knowledge Institute publishes peer-reviewed work on these collaborations. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Victorian community organisations including those run by Vietnamese-Australian, Sudanese-Australian and Greek-Australian communities partner with councils on retrofit programs and language-appropriate sustainability advice. Adoption is faster when messages are culturally informed.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** Your family debates buying an electric car or upgrading insulation. Outline three projected impacts of each option and how you would decide.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Hobsons Bay is choosing between a desalination expansion and a stormwater capture system. Build a compact impact comparison and recommend one.
3. **Vocational.** A junior engineer at a Victorian utility presents two grid-storage options. Outline three impact criteria she should use, and one ethical question.
4. **Ethical.** A class debates a proposed mine for battery minerals. Identify two ethical issues for affected Traditional Owners and explain how the solution could be redesigned.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung Traditional Owners and a renewables company plan a wind farm near Ballarat. Outline three steps to ensure adoption is genuinely consultative.

## Memorable takeaways
- Science provides options.
- Impacts decide adoption.
- Cost, trust and time matter.
- Trade-offs need to be open.
- Vulnerable communities need protection.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Science decides which solution wins." No. Science narrows the options. Society chooses.
- "If a project is good for the climate, all impacts must be good." No. Most projects produce trade-offs.
- "Long-term benefits always outweigh short-term costs." Not automatically. People are protected by managing costs in the present.
- "Aboriginal consent is just process." No. It is essential and often legally required.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why "the science" is not "the answer".* Science maps the territory and offers options. Choosing among them depends on values, costs and politics.
2. *Why assumptions matter as much as numbers.* The same data can lead to different projections under different assumptions. Naming them lets others test the result.
3. *Why community engagement is part of science.* Without it, the science fails to inform real decisions, and projects fail to be adopted or maintained.
4. *Why a 4-year political cycle discounts 30-year benefits.* Short-term costs are visible; long-term benefits are not. Independent agencies and treaties help carry long-term thinking through political cycles.
5. *One design feature that protects vulnerable communities.* Targeted rebates, training programs, or distributed ownership shares.
6. *Why environmental laws name uncertainty.* It allows action under partial information when delay would cause severe or irreversible harm.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *EV vs insulation.* EV reduces transport emissions and fuel cost; insulation reduces heating cost and emissions year-round; insulation likely wins for a Melbourne home in winter; EV gains weight if commuting distances are high or solar is on the roof.
2. *Hobsons Bay desal vs stormwater.* Desal: high cost, high reliability, energy-intensive. Stormwater: lower cost, depends on rainfall, ecological benefits. Recommend stormwater plus low-flow urban design, with an energy-efficient desal contract held in reserve.
3. *Grid storage options.* Lifetime cost, environmental impact of materials, and reliability under heat and load. Ethical question: are battery materials sourced under enforceable labour and environmental standards?
4. *Battery-mineral mine.* Issues: free, prior and informed consent of Traditional Owners; protection of significant sites. Redesign: alternate site, smaller footprint, royalty arrangements, joint management of rehabilitation.
5. *Wadawurrung wind farm.* Step 1: early, paid involvement of the Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation. Step 2: negotiate co-design and benefit-sharing. Step 3: agree on monitoring of cultural and environmental outcomes.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Decision: large-scale offshore wind in the Bass Strait.* Options: full build-out by 2035 vs staged build-out to 2045. Impacts: emissions reduced; capital cost; supply-chain emissions; jobs in Latrobe Valley; pressure on bird and whale habitats; consultation with Bunurong and Gunaikurnai. Recommendation: staged build-out with adaptive triggers, environmental monitoring, and community ownership shares.
