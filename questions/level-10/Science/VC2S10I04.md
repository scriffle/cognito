# VC2S10I04 — Science Inquiry Skills

## Snapshot
- **Descriptor:** VC2S10I04. Organise, process and summarise data using tables, graphs, descriptive statistics, models, symbols, formulas and mathematical relationships.
- **Year level / Learning area:** Year 10, Science (Scientific Investigation Skills).
- **Big ideas:**
  - Process data before you interpret it.
  - Summary statistics tell different parts of the story.
  - Formulas and models connect numbers to mechanisms.
- **Curriculum hooks:** Numeracy; Digital Literacy; Critical and Creative Thinking.

## What you are learning
- You are learning to **organise**, **process** and **summarise** data using tables, graphs, **descriptive statistics**, models, symbols, **formulas** and **mathematical relationships**.
- You are learning to choose the summary that helps the reader understand the data.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **mean**, **median**, **range** and **standard deviation** in your own words.
- Choose between bar charts, line graphs, scatter plots and box plots.
- Apply a simple relationship (e.g., y = mx + c) to model linear data.
- Use a formula or symbol set to summarise a relationship in compact form.

## Core content

**1. Tidy data first.** A tidy table puts one observation per row, one variable per column, and units in headers. Tidy data makes processing fast and reduces errors. *Quick check:* Why does merging units into the data column cause headaches later?

**2. Descriptive statistics.** The **mean** is the average. The **median** is the middle value. The **range** is max minus min. The **standard deviation** shows how spread out values are around the mean. A small set of stats often beats raw rows. *Quick check:* When is the median better than the mean?

**3. Choosing the right chart.** Bar charts compare groups. Line graphs show change with a continuous variable, typically time. Scatter plots show relationships between two continuous variables. Box plots show distribution and spread. *Quick check:* What kind of graph shows skew and outliers in one picture?

**4. Worked example: solar panel output across two weeks.** Independent: day. Dependent: kWh generated. Data are continuous and ordered in time, so a line graph is appropriate. Compute mean and standard deviation. Add a moving average to smooth daily variability. Fit a relationship: output = a × sunshine_hours + b. *Follow the thinking:* tidy → summarise → visualise → model → interpret.

**5. Formulas and symbols.** Compact notation helps when comparing relationships. For example, current I = V / R; force F = m × a. Knowing the symbols lets you read scientific papers and use spreadsheet formulas. *Quick check:* What does the gradient (m) tell you in y = mx + c?

**6. Models that summarise relationships.** A **model** captures the essence of a relationship. Linear models fit straight lines; curve models fit non-linear data. Use the simplest model that captures the pattern. *Quick check:* When does a curve fit the data better than a line?

**7. Spreadsheet-driven processing.** Modern science processes data with spreadsheets and code. Use named columns, formula cells and documented steps. Save versions before major edits. *Quick check:* Why is "I deleted the wrong column" a sign you need version control?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** Bureau of Meteorology Australia and EPA Victoria release tidy CSV data sets that schools use for graphing and modelling. The Australian Bureau of Statistics offers StatsAcademy resources for descriptive statistics.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Seasonal calendars created by Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and other Victorian peoples are themselves data summaries: many years of observation summarised into visual seasons and indicators. The Bureau of Meteorology has co-published Indigenous Weather Knowledge calendars. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Vietnamese-Australian and Italian-Australian small-business owners across Melbourne keep careful sales-versus-weather logs that are early examples of data summarisation, often used to predict stock needs.

## Try these scenarios

1. **Everyday.** You record your phone's daily screen time for two weeks. Choose two descriptive statistics and one chart type, and justify each.
2. **Civic.** Your council in Manningham publishes annual road-crash data by suburb. Recommend a chart type and one summary statistic for a community newsletter.
3. **Vocational.** A junior data analyst at a Victorian utility models electricity demand against temperature. Outline the data fields, chart type and a simple linear model.
4. **Ethical.** A class compares grades against household income for the year level. Identify the issue and propose an alternative summary that does not single out individuals.
5. **Cross-cultural.** Wadawurrung rangers and a CSIRO team compare bird counts over a decade. Outline a summary that respects long-term observation and modern statistics.

## Memorable takeaways
- One row, one observation. Units in headers.
- Mean, median, range, standard deviation.
- Bar for groups. Line for time. Scatter for relations.
- Simple models beat over-fitted curves.
- Spreadsheets need version control.

## Watch out for these traps
- "More charts means more rigorous." No. Each chart must earn its place.
- "Mean is always the best summary." No. Skewed data are better described with median and range.
- "A trendline proves causation." No. It summarises shape, not cause.
- "Spreadsheets cannot be wrong." No. Formula errors are a major source of public data mistakes.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Merging units into columns.* It breaks numeric processing; formulas cannot run on cells containing both numbers and units.
2. *Median vs mean.* Median is better when data are skewed or have outliers; mean works best for symmetric distributions.
3. *Chart for skew and outliers.* Box plot.
4. *What gradient m tells you.* The rate of change of y with respect to x.
5. *Curve vs line.* When the relationship is non-linear, for example saturation, growth or decay.
6. *Why "wrong column" signals version control.* Without saved versions, the mistake is hard to reverse; a versioning workflow allows recovery.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Screen-time study.* Mean and standard deviation describe central tendency and spread. A line graph shows day-by-day pattern.
2. *Manningham road crashes.* Bar chart of total crashes per suburb; report the median (suburbs vary widely) and identify any outliers.
3. *Demand vs temperature.* Fields: time, temperature, demand. Scatter plot. Linear model for moderate range; consider piecewise model around peak summer days.
4. *Grades and income.* Issue: privacy and stigma. Alternative: use school-level summary statistics or compare resourcing rather than households.
5. *Wadawurrung bird counts.* Combine long-term observational summaries from rangers with statistical summaries (annual mean, trend slope). Co-author findings with named contributors.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Dataset: Melbourne airport mean daily temperatures, 2014–2024.* Tidy table with date, mean temperature in °C. Mean and standard deviation per year. Line graph of annual means. Linear model fitted to year-vs-mean shows a small positive gradient. Discussion notes that the model captures average warming but not extreme events or seasonality.
