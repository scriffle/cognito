## What you are learning
- You are learning to **select equipment**, **generate data with appropriate precision** and **consider sample size** for **reliable results**, using **digital tools** where appropriate.
- You are learning to plan data collection that supports the claim you want to make.

By the end of this you should be able to:
- Define **precision**, **sample size** and **statistical reliability** in your own words.
- Choose between two instruments based on resolution and uncertainty.
- Estimate how many trials or samples you need for a credible result.
- Use one digital tool to generate and record data with version control.

## Core content

**1. Precision, accuracy and resolution.** **Precision** is how close repeated measurements are. **Accuracy** is how close to the true value. **Resolution** is the smallest unit your instrument can show. Year 10 work names all three. *Quick check:* Why is a precise but inaccurate dataset still a problem?

**2. Choosing the right instrument.** Match the smallest difference you need to detect with the instrument's resolution and uncertainty. A digital calliper (0.01 mm) is overkill for measuring leaves but essential for engineering. *Quick check:* Which would you pick to time a 5-second event, a stopwatch or a phone timer with millisecond display?

**3. Sample size and reliability.** **Sample size (n)** is the number of independent measurements. Larger n reduces random error and tightens estimates. Beyond a point, the gains shrink. **Reliability** is whether the same approach would give similar results next time. *Quick check:* Why are three trials usually a minimum?

**4. Worked example: timing reaction speed in a class study.** Question: does caffeine affect reaction time? Equipment: an online reaction-time task on identical laptops; n = 30 students; three repeat trials each. Precision: 1 ms resolution. Controlled: same time of day, same room, same task. Pre-register the analysis plan. Use a spreadsheet to record means and standard deviations. *Follow the thinking:* match instrument to claim → plan sample → control variables → log data digitally.

**5. Estimating sample size.** Simple rule: more is better, but design matters more than size. A pilot study reveals variability and helps decide n. Power calculations give a formal target where effects are small. *Quick check:* What does a pilot study tell you that a full study cannot at the start?

**6. Digital tools.** Spreadsheets, data loggers, online forms and citizen-science platforms all speed collection. They also introduce risks: untested formulas, sync errors, privacy concerns. Use version control, name fields clearly and back up regularly. *Quick check:* Why is "auto-save" not a substitute for version control?

**7. Recording fields you will not regret.** Every record carries label, value, unit, time, instrument, operator and conditions. A complete row makes audit and reproducibility possible. *Quick check:* What field is most often left out of school records?

## Watch out for these traps
- "More decimal places means more accurate." No. Extra zeros beyond resolution are misleading.
- "Bigger n is always better." No. After a point, design quality matters more than additional measurements.
- "Digital tools are objective." No. They reflect choices in calibration, defaults and analysis.
- "Pre-registering limits creativity." No. It limits cherry-picking.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Precise but inaccurate problem.* The data are repeatable but wrong, which can be more dangerous than obviously bad data because it looks trustworthy.
2. *Stopwatch vs phone for a 5-second event.* Phone timer for higher resolution; for human-started events, reaction-time bias dominates either way, so use a sensor-triggered timer if you can.
3. *Why three trials are a minimum.* Two trials cannot distinguish random variation from a real difference. Three lets you compute a mean and a range.
4. *What a pilot study tells you that a full study cannot.* Variability, time per trial, equipment quirks and likely sample-size needs.
5. *Why auto-save is not version control.* Auto-save overwrites; version control preserves earlier states and supports rollback.
6. *Field most often left out.* Time of measurement, operator or instrument identifier.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Oven temperature test.* Probe thermometer (resolution 0.1°C). Sample size: three readings at five positions inside each oven, repeated across three sessions. Table: oven, position, trial, temperature, time, operator.
2. *Casey noise survey.* Use one calibrated sound-level meter brand; train volunteers in handling, height and orientation; require a standard 60-second reading per site.
3. *Pathology cell counts.* Use the same field size and stain protocol; blind operators to slide identity; report inter-operator agreement.
4. *Student health data.* Issues: privacy and consent. Protocol: data minimisation, parental consent, encrypted storage, identifiers replaced with codes, and a published data-management plan.
5. *Wadawurrung waterhole depth.* Calibrate loggers daily against a reference, place sensors at the same depth and orientation, and use a single timezone for clock sync. Co-design metadata fields with rangers.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Study: shade sail effect on midday surface temperature in a Footscray playground.* Instrument: infrared thermometer with 0.1°C resolution. Sample size: n = 30 readings under shade and n = 30 in sun, across three days. Digital tool: Google Sheets with timestamped entries and named versions. Pre-registered analysis: mean difference and 95% confidence interval. Stopping rule: complete three days unless rain interrupts collection.
