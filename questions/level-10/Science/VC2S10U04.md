## Core content

**1. DNA, genes and chromosomes.** **DNA** is a long molecule that stores genetic information using four bases (A, T, G, C). A **gene** is a stretch of DNA that codes for a protein or trait. **Chromosomes** are packaged DNA; humans have 23 pairs. *Quick check:* Why does a cell need to package DNA into chromosomes?

**2. Alleles and combinations.** An **allele** is a version of a gene. Most people inherit two of each gene, one from each parent. **Homozygous** means two copies of the same allele; **heterozygous** means two different alleles. *Quick check:* If two heterozygous parents have a child, what genotype combinations are possible?

**3. Dominant and recessive.** A **dominant** allele's effect appears with one copy. A **recessive** allele needs two copies to show. Many traits do not follow simple dominance, but some do (e.g. tongue rolling, attached earlobes are simplified examples). *Quick check:* What is the genotype of a person showing a recessive trait?

**4. Genotype vs phenotype.** **Genotype** is the genetic combination (e.g. Aa). **Phenotype** is the observable trait (e.g. brown eyes). The same phenotype can come from different genotypes. *Quick check:* Why do AA and Aa give the same phenotype if A is dominant?

**5. Worked example: Mendelian cross.** Two heterozygous parents (Aa × Aa) for a single dominant trait. Punnett square:

|     | A   | a   |
|-----|-----|-----|
| **A**   | AA  | Aa  |
| **a**   | Aa  | aa  |

Genotype ratio: 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. Phenotype ratio: 3 dominant : 1 recessive. *Follow the thinking:* identify alleles → set up the square → count combinations → translate to phenotypes.

**6. Sex chromosomes and inheritance.** In humans, biological sex is largely determined by X and Y chromosomes. Some traits are sex-linked (e.g. red-green colour vision, haemophilia). Patterns differ between sons and daughters. *Quick check:* Why do sex-linked recessive traits appear more often in people with one X chromosome?

**7. Beyond Mendel.** Many traits involve multiple genes (height, skin tone), are influenced by environment (height, weight), or show incomplete dominance and codominance (blood group ABO). Mendelian rules are a starting point, not the whole story. *Quick check:* Why is "the gene for intelligence" misleading?

## Watch out for these traps
- "Genes determine everything." No. Environment and behaviour matter for most traits.
- "Each trait is one gene." No. Many traits involve many genes.
- "Recessive traits are rare." Not always. Frequencies vary by population.
- "DNA is destiny." No. Most outcomes reflect interaction between genes and environment.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why package DNA in chromosomes.* DNA is very long; packaging keeps it organised, protects it during cell division and ensures even distribution to daughter cells.
2. *Aa × Aa offspring genotypes.* AA, Aa, Aa, aa.
3. *Genotype showing a recessive trait.* Two recessive alleles (homozygous recessive, e.g. aa).
4. *Why AA and Aa look the same.* The dominant allele's effect appears with one copy, masking the recessive allele.
5. *Why sex-linked recessive traits appear more often in XY individuals.* They have only one X chromosome, so a single recessive allele is not masked by a second copy.
6. *Why "the gene for intelligence" is misleading.* Intelligence involves many genes and environmental influences; no single gene explains it.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Left-handed child of right-handed parents.* Both parents could carry a recessive allele related to handedness. The child could be homozygous for the recessive allele. Real handedness is more complex than Mendelian inheritance, so this is a simplification.
2. *Whitehorse public-genetics night.* Exhibits: extracting DNA from strawberries, building a Punnett-square game, exploring family tree pedigrees. Each makes inheritance tangible.
3. *Genetic counsellor.* Cover risk in plain language with visuals; present options without pressure; ensure cultural and emotional support.
4. *Direct-to-consumer DNA tests.* Issues: privacy of genetic data, accuracy of ancestry claims. Safeguard: independent regulation of data use and clearer consent.
5. *Indigenous-led genomics.* Step 1: community-led research questions and governance. Step 2: data sovereignty under CARE principles. Step 3: feedback to community before public release.
