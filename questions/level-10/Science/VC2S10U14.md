## Core content

**1. Two models for energy transfer.** Some phenomena are best described as **waves** (sound, water, light); others as **particles** (collisions in a gas, photons in a photoelectric effect). Both models describe energy moving through media or space. *Quick check:* Why is a tennis ball a particle while ocean swell is a wave?

**2. Transverse and longitudinal waves.** A **transverse wave** has oscillations perpendicular to direction of travel (light, water surface, secondary seismic waves). A **longitudinal wave** has oscillations along the direction (sound, primary seismic waves). *Quick check:* What kind of wave can travel through a vacuum?

**3. Wave properties.** **Amplitude** is the size of the oscillation. **Wavelength (λ)** is the distance between successive crests. **Frequency (f)** is how many oscillations pass a point per second, in hertz (Hz). **Wave speed (v)** is how far the wave travels per second. They link as v = f × λ. *Quick check:* If a wave's frequency doubles and speed stays the same, what happens to wavelength?

**4. Worked example: a guitar string.** Pluck a string; it vibrates at a fundamental frequency depending on length, tension and mass. The vibration disturbs air, creating a longitudinal sound wave that reaches your ear. Pitch reflects frequency; loudness reflects amplitude; timbre reflects the mix of frequencies. v = f × λ applies to both string and air waves. *Follow the thinking:* source → vibration → wave → ear → perception.

**5. Light as wave and particle.** Light shows wave behaviour (interference, diffraction) and particle behaviour (photoelectric effect). Modern physics (quantum mechanics) accommodates both with wave-particle duality. *Quick check:* Which model explains a rainbow, and which explains the photovoltaic effect in solar cells?

**6. Sound and the medium.** Sound travels faster in solids than in liquids than in gases, because particles are closer in solids. Sound cannot travel through vacuum because there is no medium. *Quick check:* Why does an underwater sound seem muffled when you surface?

**7. Wave behaviour: reflection, refraction, diffraction.** Reflection bounces waves; refraction bends them between media; diffraction spreads them around obstacles. These behaviours are key to lenses, sonar and radio technology. *Quick check:* Why can you hear someone calling around a corner but not always see them?

## Watch out for these traps
- "Sound is a particle." No. Sound is a wave; particles in the medium oscillate.
- "Higher frequency means faster." Not always; frequency, wavelength and speed are related but distinct.
- "Light needs a medium." No. Light travels through vacuum.
- "Wave-particle duality is contradictory." It is unfamiliar, but not contradictory; quantum mechanics accommodates both behaviours.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Tennis ball vs ocean swell.* A tennis ball is a single object moving as a particle. Ocean swell carries energy through water without water itself moving as a single object.
2. *Wave through vacuum.* Electromagnetic (e.g. light, radio).
3. *Frequency doubles, speed same.* Wavelength halves (v = f × λ rearranges to λ = v/f).
4. *Rainbow vs photovoltaic.* Rainbow shows wave behaviour (refraction and dispersion). Photovoltaic effect shows particle behaviour (photons knocking electrons).
5. *Underwater sound when surfacing.* Water transmits sound efficiently to the body, but air-water interface reflects most of it. Above water, sound still reaches the ears but with different timbre.
6. *Heard around a corner but not seen.* Diffraction is more pronounced for longer wavelengths. Sound waves are much longer than visible light, so they bend around obstacles more easily.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Shout across an oval.* Sound travels as a longitudinal wave through air, decreasing in amplitude with distance. Strong wind shifts the wave path and can carry sound further downwind or block it upwind.
2. *Maribyrnong PA upgrade.* Frequency response (covers human voice range), amplitude management (avoid distortion at high volumes), and wave directionality (focused speakers reduce echo).
3. *School-hall acoustics.* Surfaces (reflection vs absorption), shape (avoiding standing waves), speaker placement (matching room geometry).
4. *Loudspeaker test.* Issues: hearing damage and consent. Alternative: measure speaker output with calibrated meters at safe volumes, with all participants opting in.
5. *Wadawurrung clapsticks.* Discuss with permission and credit. Explain wave physics: collision creates a longitudinal sound wave; pitch depends on size and material. Use only published, community-approved material.
