## Core content

**1. Infectious vs non-infectious.** **Infectious diseases** spread from a source (often another person) via a **pathogen**. **Non-infectious diseases** arise from genetics, environment, behaviour or aging. Examples: COVID-19 (infectious); type 2 diabetes (non-infectious). *Quick check:* Which category includes asthma?

**2. Pathogen types.** Bacteria are single-celled organisms; many are harmless or beneficial, some cause disease (Streptococcus, *E. coli*). Viruses are smaller and need host cells to replicate (influenza, SARS-CoV-2). Fungi cause infections from athlete's foot to invasive disease. *Quick check:* Why do antibiotics work on bacteria but not viruses?

**3. Transmission routes.** **Transmission** is how a pathogen reaches a new host. Common routes: airborne, droplet, surface (fomite), water, food, vector (mosquito), bodily fluids. Different routes call for different controls. *Quick check:* What route is interrupted by hand-washing?

**4. Non-infectious causes.** Genetics (cystic fibrosis), environment (asbestos exposure), lifestyle (smoking, diet, inactivity) and aging all contribute. Many non-infectious diseases are influenced by multiple factors. *Quick check:* Why is "lifestyle disease" sometimes a misleading label?

**5. Worked example: managing COVID-19 in Victoria.** Pathogen: SARS-CoV-2 (virus). Transmission: airborne and droplet, with surface and aerosol routes. Controls: vaccination (immune-system priming), masks (filter droplets), ventilation (dilute aerosols), testing and isolation (interrupt chains), public-health communication (behaviour change). Trade-offs: economic and social costs; disproportionate effects on multilingual and low-income communities. *Follow the thinking:* identify pathogen → name routes → choose controls → balance trade-offs → adapt as evidence updates.

**6. Vaccination and immunity.** Vaccines train the immune system to recognise a pathogen without causing the disease. They protect individuals and reduce community transmission (herd protection). *Quick check:* Why does the flu vaccine change formulation each year?

**7. Public-health systems.** Disease surveillance, contact tracing, sanitation, food safety and vaccination programs reduce the burden of disease. The Department of Health Victoria coordinates many of these. *Quick check:* Why is "sanitation" one of the most powerful health technologies in history?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Doherty Institute, the Murdoch Children's Research Institute and the Department of Health Victoria coordinate research and response. The Royal Children's Hospital runs immunisation programs and vaccine education.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** The Victorian Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisation provides culturally appropriate care for both infectious and non-infectious diseases. Closing-the-Gap targets address chronic-disease disparities. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Multilingual public-health teams across Melbourne deliver vaccination and chronic-disease information in Vietnamese, Mandarin, Arabic, Sudanese and other languages. Adoption rates rise where messages are culturally informed.

## Memorable takeaways
- Infectious has a pathogen; non-infectious does not.
- Bacteria, viruses, fungi need different treatments.
- Break the route, slow the disease.
- Vaccines train, not infect.
- Public health is biology + behaviour + policy.

## Watch out for these traps
- "All germs are bad." No. Many bacteria support digestion, immunity and skin health.
- "Antibiotics fix everything." No. They only work on bacteria, and overuse drives resistance.
- "Non-infectious diseases are entirely lifestyle." No. Genetics, environment and aging also matter.
- "Public health is one-size-fits-all." No. Effective campaigns are tailored to communities.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Asthma category.* Non-infectious. Genetic and environmental factors drive most cases.
2. *Antibiotics on bacteria not viruses.* Antibiotics target bacterial structures (cell walls, ribosomes) that viruses lack. Viruses use host-cell machinery.
3. *Hand-washing route.* Surface (fomite) and direct-contact routes. Droplets transferred to hands and then to faces are interrupted.
4. *"Lifestyle disease" label issue.* It can imply blame and ignore genetics, environment, structural inequality and access to healthy food and exercise.
5. *Why flu vaccine changes yearly.* Influenza viruses mutate. The vaccine is updated to target circulating strains each year.
6. *Why sanitation is so powerful.* It interrupts many disease routes at once (water, food, fomite). Improvements in sanitation drove huge gains in life expectancy historically.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Friend unwell.* Stay home (isolation), wear a mask in shared spaces (droplet control), wash hands and clean surfaces (fomite control).
2. *Hume flu season.* Mass vaccination clinics, public communication in multiple languages, ventilation upgrades in council buildings, plus targeted check-in services for older residents.
3. *Pathologist responsibilities.* Personal protective equipment, sample chain-of-custody, accurate reporting of results to public-health authorities.
4. *Mandatory vaccination debate.* For: protects vulnerable people who cannot be vaccinated; reduces healthcare system burden. Against: bodily autonomy concerns; trust and equity issues if poorly implemented.
5. *Sudanese-Australian flu campaign.* Trusted community-leader endorsement, language-appropriate materials, accessible clinic locations and times, and feedback channels to improve future campaigns.
