- You are learning to read the table and predict element behaviour.

By the end of this you should be able to:

## Core content

**1. Rows and columns mean something.** A **period** is a row; a **group** is a column. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of **valence electrons** (outer-shell electrons). *Quick check:* Why does atomic number increase by exactly one across a period?

**2. Metals, non-metals, metalloids.** Most elements are metals. Non-metals sit on the right; metalloids run along a stair-step boundary (silicon, germanium). Metallic character increases down a group and decreases across a period. *Quick check:* Where on the table would you expect to find a brittle, dull, non-conductive solid?

**3. Reactivity trends.** **Group 1 alkali metals** (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) become more reactive going down because outer electrons are easier to lose. **Group 17 halogens** (F, Cl, Br, I) become less reactive going down because outer-shell electrons are further from the nucleus. *Quick check:* Why are sodium and potassium stored under oil in the lab?

**4. Atomic size.** Atomic size decreases across a period (more protons pull electrons closer) and increases down a group (more shells). *Quick check:* Compare the atomic size of magnesium and calcium.

**5. Noble gases.** **Group 18** elements (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) have full outer shells and are largely unreactive. They are useful in lighting (neon signs), preservation (argon in welding) and cooling (helium). *Quick check:* Why is argon used in welding rather than air?

**6. Worked example: predicting magnesium chemistry.** Magnesium (Mg) is in Group 2, Period 3. Two valence electrons, easily lost, so it forms Mg²⁺. Reacts with oxygen to form MgO; with water (slowly) to form Mg(OH)₂. Burns with a bright white flame, used in fireworks and sparklers. The position predicts behaviour. *Follow the thinking:* group → valence → ion type → typical reactions → real applications.

**7. The story of the table.** Mendeleev arranged elements by atomic mass and predicted gaps for unknown elements (gallium, germanium). The modern table arranges by atomic number. Most predictions held up. *Quick check:* Why was predicting unknown elements a strong test of Mendeleev's idea?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Australian Synchrotron at Clayton uses elemental analysis across the periodic table. Australian mining and processing industries depend on understanding metal reactivity (iron ore in the Pilbara, lithium in Western Australia).
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge of metals and minerals includes use of ochres (iron oxides), and knowledge of mineral-rich locations. The Indigenous Knowledge Institute publishes peer-reviewed work on these traditions. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Bigger atom means heavier." Not always. Atomic mass and size depend on different factors.
- "Reactivity always increases down a group." Only for some groups (alkali metals); for halogens it decreases.
- "Noble gases never react." They mostly do not, but some heavier noble gases form rare compounds.
- "Mendeleev was right because he was a genius." No. He was right because his predictions matched later observations.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why atomic number increases by one across a period.* Each successive element adds one proton to its nucleus.
2. *Brittle, dull, non-conductive solid.* Likely a non-metal in the upper right of the table (e.g. sulfur).
3. *Why alkali metals are stored under oil.* They react vigorously with water and oxygen, so oil prevents contact with air.
4. *Magnesium vs calcium.* Calcium is larger, because it has one more electron shell than magnesium.
5. *Why argon is used in welding.* Argon is unreactive, so it shields hot metal from oxygen and nitrogen in air, preventing oxidation.
6. *Why predicting elements was a strong test.* Confirmed predictions show the framework reflects how nature works, not just a way to organise known facts.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Sodium streetlamp.* Sodium atoms emit light at characteristic wavelengths when excited electrons drop back to lower energy levels. The yellow-orange colour reflects the energy gap of these transitions, set by sodium's atomic structure.
2. *Coastal jetty materials.* Stainless steel resists saltwater corrosion better than plain iron. Aluminium is lighter but reacts with chloride. Galvanising adds a sacrificial zinc layer. Choices depend on cost, lifetime and maintenance.
3. *Unknown sample tests.* Conductivity, lustre and brittleness; reaction with dilute acid; flame test for characteristic colour.
4. *Alkali metals and water.* Issues: explosive reactions and burns. Controlled alternative: small lithium pieces in carefully prepared conditions, or video demonstration with discussion.
5. *Wadawurrung ochre processing.* Discuss with permission and credit. Frame the chemistry (iron oxides hydrated to varying degrees, dehydrated by heating) alongside cultural meaning. Use only published, community-approved material.
