## Core content

**1. Dalton: indivisible spheres.** John Dalton (early 1800s) proposed atoms as solid, indivisible particles. The model explained chemical proportions but said nothing about internal structure. *Quick check:* Why was Dalton's model good enough for chemistry but not for radioactivity?

**2. Thomson: plums in pudding.** J.J. Thomson discovered the **electron** in 1897 using cathode rays. He proposed atoms as positive "pudding" with electron "plums" inside. *Quick check:* What experiment showed electrons existed?

**3. Rutherford: a nucleus.** Ernest Rutherford (with Geiger and Marsden) fired alpha particles at gold foil. Most passed straight through, but a few bounced back, suggesting a tiny dense **nucleus**. The model became "mostly empty space with a positive nucleus". *Quick check:* What did the bouncing alpha particles tell us?

**4. Bohr and beyond.** Niels Bohr added electron shells with fixed energy levels. Quantum mechanics later replaced shells with probability clouds. Each step explained more observations than the last. *Quick check:* Why is the modern atomic model called a "probability cloud" rather than orbits?

**5. Inside the nucleus.** Atoms contain **protons** (positive), **neutrons** (neutral) and **electrons** (negative). The number of protons defines the element. **Isotopes** of the same element differ in neutron count. *Quick check:* What makes carbon-12 and carbon-14 the same element but different isotopes?

**6. Worked example: radioactive decay and carbon dating.** Carbon-14 is unstable. It decays by **beta emission** (a neutron becomes a proton, releasing a beta particle). Half-life: about 5,730 years. By measuring the carbon-14 left in old organic material, scientists estimate age. Carbon dating is used in Australian archaeology, including studies of Aboriginal cultural sites where dating has been done with community consent. *Follow the thinking:* unstable atom → emits particle → becomes new element → half-life → application.

**7. Three main decay types.** **Alpha** decay releases a helium nucleus (large, slow, stopped by paper). **Beta** decay releases an electron or positron (faster, stopped by aluminium). **Gamma** decay releases high-energy electromagnetic radiation (very penetrating, needs lead or thick concrete). *Quick check:* Which decay produces a new element of higher atomic number?

## Victorian and cultural context

- **Victorian anchor.** The Australian Synchrotron in Clayton uses high-energy beams to probe materials. ANSTO (the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation) supports nuclear medicine in Victorian hospitals.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective.** Carbon dating has been used to date some of the oldest known cultural sites in the world, including Madjedbebe in northern Australia (around 65,000 years old). Such studies are conducted under partnership agreements with Traditional Owners, recognising cultural ownership of findings. Specific cultural knowledge should only be read through published, community-approved material.
- **Multicultural Australian perspective.** Researchers from Indian, Iranian and Anglo-Australian backgrounds at Monash and Melbourne universities lead work on nuclear medicine, including diagnostic isotopes for cancer detection.

## Memorable takeaways
- Models grow with evidence.
- Protons define the element. Neutrons make isotopes.
- Unstable atoms decay to stable ones.
- Alpha, beta, gamma differ in particle, mass and penetration.
- Half-life is the clock for dating.

## Watch out for these traps
- "Atoms are tiny solar systems." No. Modern models use probability clouds, not orbits.
- "All radiation is dangerous." No. Background radiation is everywhere; doses matter.
- "Carbon dating works on anything." No. It only works on organic material up to about 50,000 years.
- "Nuclear means weapons." No. Most nuclear science is in medicine, materials and energy.

## Model responses

Use these to check your own thinking after you have had a go.

**Quick checks (Core content)**

1. *Why Dalton's model was enough for chemistry but not radioactivity.* It explained mass and proportions but had no internal structure to allow particles to come out of atoms.
2. *Experiment showing electrons.* Cathode-ray-tube experiments by Thomson, where particles were deflected by electric and magnetic fields.
3. *What bouncing alphas told us.* Atoms have a small, dense, positively charged nucleus; most of the atom is empty space.
4. *Why "probability cloud".* Quantum mechanics describes electrons by probabilities of being in regions, not by fixed orbits.
5. *Carbon-12 vs carbon-14.* Same number of protons (6), different number of neutrons (6 vs 8).
6. *Decay producing a new element of higher atomic number.* Beta-minus decay: a neutron becomes a proton, increasing the atomic number.

**Try these scenarios**

1. *Smoke alarm.* A small amount of americium-241 emits alpha particles that ionise the air inside the alarm. Smoke disrupts the ionisation, triggering the alarm. The source is sealed, low activity and safe in the home.
2. *Geelong radiopharmaceutical facility.* Benefits: jobs, isotope supply for hospitals, scientific spillovers. Risks: regulatory compliance, transport safety, community trust. Trade-offs depend on design and oversight.
3. *Imaging technician safety.* Time, distance, shielding. Minimise exposure time, maximise distance from sources, use lead aprons and screens.
4. *Nuclear power debate.* For: low-carbon energy and reliable baseload. Against: radioactive waste storage and high upfront costs. Both sides involve real ethical considerations on safety, equity and intergenerational responsibility.
5. *Carbon dating on Country.* Step 1: partner with Traditional Owner corporation under written agreement. Step 2: agree on which sites and findings can be made public. Step 3: ensure community ownership of cultural data.

**Stretch task — example shape of a strong answer.** *Application: nuclear medicine in Victoria.* Physics: technetium-99m gamma emission for diagnostic imaging. Institution: ANSTO and Royal Melbourne Hospital. Ethical consideration: managing waste safely while ensuring patient access to advanced diagnostics.
